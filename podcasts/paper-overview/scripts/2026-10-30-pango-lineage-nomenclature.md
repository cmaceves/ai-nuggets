## Script

Paper link: Rambaut et al., "A dynamic nomenclature proposal for SARS-CoV-2 lineages to assist genomic epidemiology." Nature Microbiology, 15 July 2020. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-020-0770-5

Welcome back to SARS-coronavirus-two: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-coronavirus-two literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we spent the episode on nine genomes from Connecticut, a phylogenetic tree, and an airline-passenger model — the paper that showed, in May 2020, that domestic travel had overtaken international travel as the dominant source of new infections in the United States. Today we jump forward two months, to a paper that is almost pure infrastructure. It isn't a new experimental result. It isn't a new structure or a new drug. It is a naming scheme. If you have ever heard someone say the words "B-one-one-seven," or "Delta," or "B-one-six-one-seven-two," or "B-one-one-five-two-nine" — if you have ever read a surveillance report that said a country's cases were ninety percent one lineage and ten percent another — you are using the vocabulary this paper proposed.

Episode eleven. The paper is "A dynamic nomenclature proposal for SARS-coronavirus-two lineages to assist genomic epidemiology," published in Nature Microbiology on the fifteenth of July, 2020. The first author is Andrew Rambaut at the Institute of Evolutionary Biology at the University of Edinburgh — one of the people who essentially invented modern molecular phylogenetics, the author of some of the most widely used software in the field. The senior author is Oliver Pybus at the Department of Zoology at Oxford. Between them, in the author list, are Edward Holmes at Sydney, who was co-corresponding on the first Wuhan genome back in episode two; Áine O'Toole, Verity Hill, and John McCrone, Rambaut's graduate students and postdocs at Edinburgh who would end up writing most of the software that implemented the proposal; Christopher Ruis; and Louis du Plessis at Oxford. The group is almost a who's-who of RNA virus phylogenetics. The system they propose in this paper is now universally called Pango — short for Phylogenetic Assignment of Named Global Outbreak lineages — and it is one of the half-dozen tools that defined how the field actually did its work for the next five years.

Let me set the scene. Mid-July, 2020. The World Health Organization had declared COVID-nineteen a pandemic four months earlier. The United States was in its second wave, driven not by New York and the Northeast this time but by Arizona, Florida, Texas, and Georgia. Brazil had passed two million reported cases. India was approaching a million. The virus was global. And the genomic data was piling up at a pace nobody had ever seen for any pathogen in history — this paper counts thirty-five thousand publicly available complete or near-complete SARS-coronavirus-two genomes as of the first of June. For comparison, that is more sequence in six months than had been generated for influenza A in a decade. The infrastructure for sharing those genomes — the GISAID initiative above all — was working. The infrastructure for analysing them — Nextstrain, which we met last episode, and a growing ecosystem of phylogenetics tools — was working. But the field had a specific problem that this paper is trying to solve, and it is worth stating carefully, because it is subtler than it first sounds.

Here is the problem. Imagine you have thirty-five thousand genomes, each with a few mutations relative to a reference. You can build a tree, and the tree captures the entire evolutionary history of your dataset. That tree is a complete description. But you cannot have a scientific conversation by pointing at branches on a tree. If I tell you the virus circulating in Lombardy in April 2020 is "the one over there on the tree, you know, that bunchy clade in the middle, with the T-two-eight-one-four-four-C mutation," I have communicated exactly nothing. And if you and I are using different reference trees, built on different weeks of data, we may not even be looking at the same object. For public-health communication, for epidemiological comparison, for mentioning in a journal paper or a press release, you need labels. You need to be able to say "the Lombardy outbreak is lineage X" and have your listener understand which group of related viruses you are talking about, and verify it against their own data.

So why doesn't a tree-and-mutations description just work? Because a virus that mutates fast and spreads fast generates many thousands of distinguishable lineages per year, and most of them never go anywhere — they infect a handful of people and die out. The useful information is not the complete catalogue; it is a smaller set of labels for the specific lineages that are driving transmission, that have moved between countries, that have grown large enough that we need to talk about them. That selection — which lineages are important enough to name — is a judgement call that has to be made by a human, against a consistent set of criteria, and then applied dynamically as the pandemic evolves.

A bit of background on how other viruses handle this, because the paper is explicit that it draws on these lessons. For slowly-evolving organisms like bacteria, the number of named lineages stays roughly constant over time, and taxonomy, in the sense of the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses, works fine. For HIV and hepatitis C, which evolve fast but turn over their lineages slowly — the HIV subtypes have been circulating for decades — a mostly static set of names also works. The hard case is a virus like influenza A, which both evolves fast and has rapid lineage turnover: the strains dominant in any given year are replaced every few years by descendants that have escaped prior immunity, and the field needs a nomenclature that can keep adding new names as new clades emerge. Avian influenza uses a hierarchical system of "clades" and "subclades" built on genetic-distance thresholds — H5N1 clade 2.3.4.4b, for instance. SARS-coronavirus-two, when this paper was written, was clearly going to be in the influenza category: fast evolution, likely rapid lineage turnover, long time horizon. The field needed influenza-style vocabulary.

Before Pango, three competing systems were in use. The GISAID database tagged genomes with its own labels — "S", "L", "V", "G", "GH", "GR" — based on a handful of marker amino acid changes. Nextstrain had its own clade naming — "19A", "19B", "20A", "20B", "20C" — based on a date-stamped grouping of related lineages. And various country labs and surveillance groups had rolled their own labels for the particular variants they were tracking. The three systems overlapped in some places and disagreed in others, and none of them could easily be extended as new sub-lineages emerged. The paper is explicit that this was becoming a communication problem — the same virus population was being referred to by three or four different names in different papers, and new groups coming into the field had no principled way to add a label when they discovered a new regionally-important lineage. The authors wanted to replace that patchwork with a single, extensible, principled system that any group could use.

Now the proposal itself. The authors lay out four requirements the system has to satisfy. One: capture local and global patterns of genetic diversity in a timely way. Two: track lineages as they move between countries and populations. Three: be flexible enough to accommodate new diversity as it is generated. Four: be dynamic — able to incorporate both the birth of new lineages and the extinction of old ones. And they lay out a specific feature of SARS-coronavirus-two that makes a scalable naming scheme actually possible: because of how early and intensively the Wuhan outbreak was sequenced, we probably do know the root sequence of the entire SARS-coronavirus-two phylogeny. Many of the earliest sampled genomes are identical to each other and are plausibly identical to the most recent common ancestor of every circulating virus in the world. That is a rare luxury in epidemiology — usually, by the time you discover an outbreak, the root is long gone and you have to infer it. For SARS-coronavirus-two, you can set the reference sequence to be the root sequence and count mutations outward from there. That is the anchor the whole naming system hangs on.

Here is how the names work. At the root of the tree there are two major lineages: A and B. This is where the system does something clever, and it is worth sitting with. Lineage B contains the Wuhan-Hu-one reference genome — accession M-N-nine-zero-eight-nine-four-seven, sampled on December twenty-sixth, 2019, the genome that was first sequenced and published and that the world has been using as the reference ever since. Lineage A contains early Wuhan samples like Wuhan-W-H-zero-four, sampled on January fifth, 2020. The lineage A viruses share two specific nucleotides at positions eight thousand seven hundred eighty-two and twenty-eight thousand one hundred forty-four with the closest known bat relatives, RaTG-thirteen and RmYN-oh-two. Lineage B has different nucleotides at those sites. So although the first published genome happened to be a lineage B virus, the authors argue, based on parsimony and the bat outgroups, that the actual most recent common ancestor of all circulating SARS-coronavirus-two was more likely a lineage A sequence. The naming system doesn't privilege the historical accident of what got sequenced first; it reflects the actual evolutionary structure of the tree. Both lineages were still circulating around the world in July 2020.

Descendent lineages are named by appending numbers. A lineage derived from A that satisfies the authors' criteria is called A dot one. A lineage derived from A dot one is A dot one dot one. A lineage derived from B is B dot one, then B dot one dot one, then B dot one dot one dot seven — which is the pattern you will have heard pronounced "B-one-one-seven" in later episodes and in the news, and which the Pango system coined before Alpha was identified as a variant of concern. The names look like software version numbers for a reason: they are dynamic, hierarchical, and extensible.

The criteria for calling something a new lineage are specific. First, the group of genomes you want to name has to descend from an ancestral already-named lineage, and show phylogenetic evidence of having emerged into a new geographic population — a different country, or a different region of a large country — with substantial onward transmission there. Second, the group has to meet four quantitative conditions: at least one shared nucleotide difference from its parent lineage; at least five constituent genomes, each with ninety-five percent or better genome coverage; at least one shared nucleotide change among the genomes within the proposed lineage; and the branch defining the lineage has to have bootstrap support above seventy percent. The first and third conditions, taken together, insist that a lineage both diverges from its parent and shows internal diversity — the internal diversity is what tells you the lineage has actually been transmitting rather than just being a one-off descendant. The five-genome minimum is a conservative threshold against random noise. The seventy-percent bootstrap is a classical measure of phylogenetic support — if you resample your alignment columns with replacement a thousand times and rebuild the tree each time, in at least seven hundred of those thousand trees, this clade comes out as a group. Bootstrap values are the standard currency of phylogenetic confidence.

There is a design decision about the depth of the name. The system allows nested lineage names down to four levels — A dot one dot one dot one — and then, if a new sub-lineage emerges within that one, instead of extending to a fifth level, it gets a new letter prefix. A dot one dot one dot one would roll over to C dot one. The reasoning is that the goal is to track currently circulating lineages, not to capture the entire history of the virus in the name. The tree itself has the complete history; the name just needs to be short enough that a human can say it and remember it. The alphabetical rollover was done starting from C — A and B were already in use, so the first rollover gets C — and this is also, incidentally, how the system handles recombinants later: when the virus genome is a chimera of two parent lineages and there is no single ancestor, the recombinant simply gets the next available letter. In this paper, as of May 2020, no recombinants had been named, and no C-level lineages had been reached, but the rules were in place for when either came up.

The companion idea to the dynamic naming is dynamic status: active, unobserved, or inactive. A lineage with a sampled member sequenced in the last month is active. A lineage not seen for between one and three months is unobserved. A lineage not seen for more than three months is inactive. The authors are explicit that these numbers are provisional — they anticipate adjusting the thresholds once there is a better sense of real-world lineage turnover. The names of inactive lineages are never reassigned; they just stop being plotted on the current-status maps. This matters because it bounds the number of names you need to track at any one moment. If a new user downloads the system to label today's genomes, they only need to care about the active lineages, which the paper expects to stay in the low hundreds at most.

Here is what they did to generate the initial set of names. They downloaded all SARS-coronavirus-two genomes of at least twenty-nine thousand base pairs from GISAID on May eighteenth, 2020 — this is the authors' snapshot. They trimmed the five-prime and three-prime untranslated regions, which are hard to sequence reliably, and kept the twenty-seven thousand seven hundred sixty-seven genomes that covered at least ninety-five percent of the reference. They aligned them with MAFFT — specifically the F-F-T-N-S-two algorithm, a fast, lower-accuracy option appropriate for very large alignments of very similar sequences — using default parameters. They built a maximum-likelihood tree with I-Q-tree version two, using the standard GTR-plus-gamma substitution model, which allows every pair of nucleotides to have its own substitution rate and lets some positions in the genome evolve faster than others under a gamma distribution. They assessed branch support with ultrafast bootstrapping, a thousand replicates. Then — and this is important — they curated the tree manually. They walked through it with their four criteria and annotated the clades that qualified as named lineages. Pango is not a purely algorithmic system; the initial lineage calls were human judgement calls made against a defined rubric. The algorithmic part comes later, in the software to assign new sequences to the already-named lineages.

Now the results. In the May 2020 snapshot they identified eighty-one lineages. Lineage A had six descendants named — A dot one through A dot six — and two sub-lineages of A dot one, called A dot one dot one and A dot three. Lineage B had sixteen directly derived descendants. Of those, lineage B dot one was already dominant — the paper calls it the predominant known global lineage — and had been subdivided into more than seventy sub-lineages of its own. Lineage B dot two had six descendants. Several other B lineages contained large numbers of genomes but hadn't yet subdivided enough internally to meet the criteria for named sub-lineages — because much of the pandemic by then was imported-case followed by exponential-growth within one country, which generates many closely related sequences but doesn't necessarily meet the "distinct new population with onward spread" criterion. The paper's Table 1 lists each initial lineage with its geographic range and the time span of sampling. If you have ever seen a figure in a 2021 or 2022 paper labelling the "B dot one dot one dot seven" clade or the "B dot one dot six one seven dot two" clade — this is where those labels start.

A detail worth noting. The paper does something unusual: it is also launching, in parallel, a piece of software called Pangolin — short for Phylogenetic Assignment of Named Global Outbreak Lineages — hosted on the Edinburgh group's GitHub. The paper notes that pangolin is "one such implementation" of the algorithmic assignment problem — given a new genome, which already-named lineage does it belong to? The reason the system needs software is that in principle you could assign a new genome by looking up its lineage-defining mutations. But as the pandemic evolves and the same mutations arise independently in different parts of the tree — a phenomenon called homoplasy, which we met briefly last episode — a lookup table of mutations becomes unreliable. The paper argues that phylogenetic placement, done by actually computing where a new genome sits on the existing tree, is more robust. Pangolin, over the next two years, will go through several internal rewrites — a lookup-based mode called "pangoLEARN" trained on a decision tree, then a successor called "UShER" which places new genomes on a huge mutation-annotated reference tree, which we'll meet in a later episode. But the proposal in the paper is agnostic about implementation; the core thing it is defining is the vocabulary.

The discussion. The authors are honest about the limitations. First, phylogenetic inference carries statistical uncertainty, especially during early-pandemic periods when the virus has barely accumulated diversity and many apparent clades are supported by one or two nucleotide differences. They recommend that sequences with less than seventy percent coverage of the coding region not be assigned a lineage at all — too much missing data makes placement unreliable. Second, as diversity accumulates, homoplasies and reverse mutations will become more common, which is why the system has to be re-curated regularly rather than frozen. Third, coronaviruses recombine — they can swap chunks of genome between co-infecting viruses — and recombination means a single tree may not be the right data structure. The system accommodates recombinants as new letter-prefixed lineages, but phylogenetic analysis of a recombinant history is intrinsically harder than analysis of a tree. Fourth, the authors are explicit that their system is not meant to replace finer-grained country-specific labelling systems; they imagine Pango as a global common language that country systems can translate into. They end on an expectation about longevity: they expect the dynamic nomenclature to be most useful for the duration of the global pandemic — which they say may last a few years — and that after that time, the virus will either be eliminated, or more likely become endemic or seasonal, and the lineages still circulating at that point will just keep their Pango names.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment. The Pango system became the dominant global naming scheme for SARS-coronavirus-two within months of this paper's publication and remains so today. The lineage curation process evolved into a volunteer-driven effort hosted at cov-lineages dot org, with a public GitHub issue tracker where researchers around the world would nominate new lineages. Pangolin — the software — became the standard tool for assigning new sequences, running inside major surveillance pipelines in the United Kingdom, the United States, South Africa, Brazil, and many other countries. Every variant of concern announced in the following years — the Alpha variant, which was Pango lineage B dot one dot one dot seven; the Beta variant, B dot one dot three five one; the Gamma variant, P dot one; the Delta variant, B dot one dot six one seven dot two; and the Omicron variant, B dot one dot one dot five two nine — those designations were assigned by the Pango curation group using the system this paper proposed. The WHO Greek-letter names you heard in the news later were a separate, parallel naming layer invented in May 2021 specifically for public communication, sitting on top of the Pango labels, not replacing them. The dot-separated letters-and-numbers scheme that looked slightly strange in July 2020 ended up being the primary language in which the field discussed the evolution of the virus.
