## Script

Paper link: Fauver et al., "Coast-to-Coast Spread of SARS-CoV-2 during the Early Epidemic in the United States." Cell, 7 May 2020. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2020.04.021

Welcome back to SARS-coronavirus-two: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-coronavirus-two literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we spent an hour inside a single infected cell, mapping the viral R-N-As and their modifications. Today we zoom all the way out: nine viral genomes sequenced from the first COVID-nineteen patients in Connecticut, a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree, and an airline-passenger model — pulled together into one of the first papers to say, with sequence-level evidence, that by early March 2020 the virus wasn't arriving in the United States from abroad anymore. It was arriving from the next state over.

Episode ten. The paper is "Coast-to-Coast Spread of SARS-coronavirus-two during the Early Epidemic in the United States," published in Cell on the seventh of May, 2020. The first author is Joseph Fauver. The senior author is Nathan Grubaugh at the Yale School of Public Health, and the author list is a who's-who of genomic epidemiology — Richard Neher at Basel, who maintains Nextstrain; Nick Loman and Josh Quick at Birmingham, who developed the ARTIC amplicon sequencing protocol that most of the pandemic's small-lab sequencing would end up running on; Alex Greninger and Keith Jerome at the University of Washington; Albert Ko also at Yale. These are the people who had, in effect, been training for this moment since Zika — the Grubaugh and Neher groups had used almost exactly the same playbook on Zika in the Americas three years earlier. When SARS-coronavirus-two arrived in Connecticut in early March, they already had the sequencing protocol, the phylogenetics pipeline, and the travel-modelling collaboration spun up and ready.

Let me set the scene. March, 2020. The first United States case was a traveller from Wuhan who arrived in Washington state on January nineteenth. For most of February the C-D-C testing criteria required either travel to a high-risk country or close contact with a confirmed case, which meant that for weeks the United States was essentially looking only where it already knew to look. The federal government imposed travel restrictions on China on January thirty-first, on Iran on February twenty-ninth, and on most of Europe on March eleventh. But on February twenty-sixth, a California resident tested positive who met neither criterion — the first clearly documented case of community transmission inside the United States. Between March first and March nineteenth, reported United States cases went from seventy-four to thirteen thousand six hundred and seventy-seven, and the virus was in all fifty states. The real number of infections was almost certainly in the tens of thousands by then; most of those infections had never been tested.

So here is the question. By early-to-mid March, when a new outbreak flares up somewhere in the United States — Connecticut, in this paper's case — where is the virus actually coming from? Is it still mostly international travellers, which is what the C-D-C surveillance system was built to catch? Or is it domestic — someone driving down from Massachusetts, flying in from Seattle, taking the train from New York? That matters because every public-health lever at the time was pointed at the international problem. Travel bans, airport screening, mandatory self-isolation for returning travellers — all of it was designed around the assumption that infections were being imported from abroad. If the actual importation flux had already shifted to domestic travel, those tools weren't going to help.

The headline. The group sequenced the virus from nine of the earliest COVID-nineteen cases in Connecticut, collected between March sixth and March fourteenth. None of those patients had a history of international travel. On a phylogenetic tree built from a hundred and sixty-eight global SARS-coronavirus-two genomes, their nine fell into three distinct clades — so Connecticut was seeded by at least three separate introductions. Seven of the nine clustered inside a predominantly Washington-state clade, meaning the East Coast outbreak carried a genomic fingerprint from the West Coast. Running an airline-passenger-volume model against reported case counts, they estimated that the risk of a SARS-coronavirus-two infection arriving in the New England region by domestic air travel had already overtaken the international risk by around March tenth — three days before the European travel ban took effect. The take, in a sentence: by the time the United States closed the door on international travel, the virus was already moving between states, and that movement was now the dominant source.

Now the background, because the methodology here is the real teachable content and it needs some scaffolding. Genomic epidemiology — the field this paper sits in — uses the fact that R-N-A viruses mutate as they replicate. Each round of replication introduces a small number of substitutions, essentially at random, into the genome. SARS-coronavirus-two mutates slowly for an R-N-A virus, roughly two substitutions per month across its thirty-kilobase genome, but it does mutate, and those mutations are inherited by descendant viruses. So if two patient samples share a particular substitution that is otherwise rare, that's evidence their viruses share a recent common ancestor; if a cluster of patient samples all share several such substitutions, that's a transmission chain. Phylogenetics reconstructs those relationships as a tree, with each branch tip being a sampled virus and the shape of the tree encoding the sequence of ancestor-to-descendant relationships that would produce the observed pattern of shared and unique mutations. In an outbreak context, you overlay geography on top of the tree: if the samples in a well-supported clade all come from one place, the outbreak there was likely from a single introduction; if the samples from one place are scattered across multiple clades, the place was seeded multiple independent times.

Two technical points before the experiment. First, two things make phylogenetic inference hard in the first weeks of a new outbreak. The virus has barely had time to accumulate diversity — if two Connecticut samples share only one substitution, that could be real shared ancestry or it could be the same mutation arising independently in two lineages, what's called homoplasy. The authors address this directly: they annotate the clade-supporting substitutions on the tree and argue, with a back-of-the-envelope probability calculation, that homoplasy giving the specific nested pattern they see is vanishingly unlikely at this diversity. Second, you can only resolve the structure of the tree as well as your sampling allows. In early March 2020, there were around three hundred and fifty United States genomes in the public databases, most from a small number of states — Washington above all. Because sequence from most other states was essentially absent, several Connecticut samples cluster with Washington simply because that's where the comparable sequence exists, and the authors are explicit that they can't rule out that the actual immediate source was some intermediate, unsampled state. Both caveats matter for how you read the result.

Here is how they did it. They collected residual nasopharyngeal swabs from P-C-R-positive patients at the Yale-New Haven Hospital Clinical Virology Lab and the Connecticut State Department of Public Health — patients from eight different Connecticut cities, none with international travel history, collected March sixth through fourteenth. Of ten samples that met their quality criteria, they successfully sequenced nine. The sequencing approach is called PrimalSeq, developed by Joshua Quick and Nick Loman as part of the ARTIC network, and it's worth explaining because it became the standard protocol for the next three years of pandemic genomic surveillance. The problem with sequencing R-N-A viruses from clinical samples is that most of the R-N-A in a nasal swab is human; the viral R-N-A can be present in trace quantities, especially from patients with low viral loads. If you just do bulk R-N-A sequencing, almost all your reads will be human, and viral coverage will be sparse. PrimalSeq fixes this by designing a tiled panel of P-C-R primer pairs that collectively amplify the entire viral genome in overlapping amplicons — on the order of four hundred base pairs each. You reverse-transcribe your sample, run the multiplex P-C-R, pool the amplicons, and sequence that. The result is that almost all your reads are viral, from across the whole genome, and you can get a complete consensus from samples that would be hopeless by shotgun. They loaded the amplicon libraries onto a MinION flow cell — the small, portable Oxford Nanopore device — and generated the first SARS-coronavirus-two genome in Connecticut about fourteen hours after receiving the sample. That near-real-time clinical turnaround is itself a result; the whole workflow fit into one working shift.

Reads were basecalled with Guppy, consensus genomes were called with the ARTIC bioinformatics pipeline using nanopolish for variants, and positions below twenty-fold coverage were masked out as Ns. The nine Connecticut consensus genomes went into GISAID — the shared global database — immediately, which is important: the group was feeding the data back to the community as it emerged, not holding it until publication. Those nine were then combined with a hundred and fifty-nine other publicly available SARS-coronavirus-two genomes from around the world for a hundred-and-sixty-eight-genome dataset. They aligned the sequences with MAFFT, masked known problematic sites — regions at the five- and three-prime ends where sequencing is unreliable and a handful of positions that look like consistent assembly artefacts — and built a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree using IQ-tree. Maximum likelihood here means: given a model of how nucleotides substitute over time, find the tree topology that makes the observed sequence data most probable. They rooted the tree on the two earliest Wuhan reference genomes.

Then the parallel piece: the airline-travel analysis, which is modelling, not sequencing. The question is how many infectious travellers per day are arriving at a given airport from each source location. To estimate that, you need two things — passenger volumes and prevalence at the source. For passengers, they used I-A-T-A ticket-sales data from January through March 2019, averaged to a daily estimate, for three airports serving Connecticut residents: Bradley in Hartford, Logan in Boston, and J-F-K in New York. 2019 is a stand-in because 2020 data weren't available yet. For source prevalence, they picked the five international locations with the highest reported case counts — China, Italy, Iran, Spain, Germany — and the five domestic ones — Washington, California, Florida, Illinois, Louisiana — and pulled daily reported cases from standard epidemiological trackers. Then they adjusted the reported counts for two things that systematically understate reality. First, there is a lag of about five days between a case becoming infectious and that case appearing in the official count — the person needs to develop symptoms and get tested and be reported. So they shift the time series. Second, most infections are never reported at all; the reporting rate depends on how aggressively a jurisdiction was testing. They categorise each location as "narrow", "moderate", or "broad" testing based on who was eligible for a test at the time, and apply reporting-rate estimates of five, nine, and twenty percent respectively, calibrated to published estimates for China. They convert incident cases to prevalent infectious cases by assuming an infectious period that averages about seven days, and they subtract cases that have already been diagnosed — because those people aren't getting on planes. Multiply prevalence by passenger volume per capita and you have an estimated flux of infectious arrivals per day from each source into each airport.

Now the results. The phylogenetic tree first. The nine Connecticut genomes do not cluster together. They fall into three well-separated regions of the tree, which the authors read as evidence of at least three independent introductions. One Connecticut genome, which they call C-T-Yale-zero-zero-one, sits in a clade dominated by sequences from Asia, specifically China. Another, C-T-Yale-zero-zero-six, falls in a mixed clade with European and Washington-state sequences that are too similar to each other to disentangle definitively. The remaining seven Connecticut genomes cluster inside a large clade that is heavily populated by Washington-state sequences and by other Pacific-Northwest-linked samples. Because none of these patients reported international travel, every one of those three introductions had to have come through a domestic transmission chain — somebody arrived in Connecticut carrying the virus, almost certainly from another part of the United States, and seeded onward spread. The authors are honest that they can't say exactly where the Washington-linked clade actually came from — Washington is one plausible answer because that's where most sequenced United States virus was in early March, but the lineage could have been circulating in other states too, undetected. The result is not "Connecticut got infected by Washington". It's "Connecticut's early outbreak was seeded multiple times from undetected domestic transmission chains, and one of those chains has a genomic fingerprint from the West Coast". That is enough to say, with sequence-level evidence, that trans-continental spread had already happened by early March 2020.

The travel-model results. For the three New England airports combined, 2019 domestic daily passenger volumes were about a hundred times international volumes at Hartford, ten times at Boston, and four times at J-F-K. So even if prevalence had been equal everywhere, the domestic flux into the region was always going to dominate just on volume. By early March, as reported case counts rose in Washington and California faster than in most countries other than Italy, the domestic flux crossed over to being not just larger but larger even in the specific terms of infectious travellers. By their estimate, around March tenth, the domestic importation risk from the top five states exceeded the international importation risk from the top five countries. The federal government's travel restrictions on Europe took effect on March thirteenth. The authors model what the restrictions did — assuming a forty percent and a ninety percent reduction in passenger volume from each restricted country — and the China restriction barely moves the needle because prevalence in China was low enough by then that the flux wasn't big to start with; the Europe restriction does cut international risk substantially, but that cut lands after domestic risk had already overtaken international risk. The order of operations is the key point: the barn door was still being closed on international travel at the moment domestic travel had already become the bigger problem.

The discussion and limitations. The authors are measured about what they are and are not claiming. They are not claiming the virus had been circulating undetected in Connecticut since January; they are claiming that by early March the Connecticut outbreak was being seeded by multiple domestic introductions, with sequence evidence for at least one that passed through the West Coast. They are not claiming a specific source state for the Washington-linked cluster; they are claiming that undersampling of other United States states means they cannot resolve that. They are not claiming the travel model is quantitatively accurate — they are using 2019 passenger volumes because 2020 wasn't available, and they explicitly flag that general work-from-home and social-distancing responses had probably already reduced actual travel below the 2019 baseline. If anything, those limitations go in the direction of underestimating domestic flux, because they are also leaving out car and rail travel entirely — which, for the East Coast corridor between Boston, New York, and Washington D-C, is enormous. The paper's policy argument lands on this: travel restrictions targeting individual countries are not going to help once the virus has established within a connected national network, and preventing further intra-country spread requires surveillance and testing capacity at the state level. The sentence they end on is that controlling the pandemic in the United States in the absence of a unified national surveillance strategy would be, in their word, Sisyphean.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment. The specific claim that the Connecticut cluster carried a Washington genomic fingerprint largely held up as more sequence became available — the clade the paper identified is the one later work would call lineage A-one, the predominant early-United-States lineage. The broader argument about domestic travel outweighing international travel became obvious to the entire field within weeks. The ARTIC-plus-MinION-plus-Nextstrain methodology this paper demonstrated at nine-genome scale would, over the following years, produce millions of SARS-coronavirus-two genomes from laboratories around the world, and the Nextstrain tree you can look at today is a direct descendant of what the Connecticut team put up on their community page in March 2020.
