## Script

Welcome back to SARS-coronavirus-two: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-coronavirus-two literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time, in late April 2020, we had the Krogan-lab interactome paper — twenty-six viral proteins pulled through affinity-purification mass spectrometry to map which human proteins each one touches, with a drug-repurposing shortlist on top of it. Today we stay in the same week of the pandemic but move from the proteome back to the molecule the virus actually is: its R-N-A. The question this paper asks is deceptively simple. We have the genome sequence. We have an annotation that says there are eleven open reading frames and nine subgenomic transcripts. But is the annotation right? What does the virus actually make, in a cell, in detail — which R-N-As, in which proportions, with what modifications on them, with what lengths of poly-A tail?

Episode nine. The paper is "The Architecture of SARS-coronavirus-two Transcriptome," published in Cell on the twenty-third of April, 2020. The first author is Dongwan Kim, and the senior authors are V. Narry Kim at Seoul National University and the Institute for Basic Science in Korea, and Hyeshik Chang, also at Seoul National University, with collaborators at the Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Narry Kim is a very well-known R-N-A biologist — her lab works on microRNA biogenesis and on R-N-A turnover more generally — and Hyeshik Chang is a nanopore-sequencing specialist in her group. When the virus arrived, they already had the sequencing platform, the analysis software, and the biochemical expertise to go after the viral transcriptome in a way that very few labs in the world could.

Let me set the scene. End of April 2020. Three million confirmed cases worldwide, more than two hundred thousand reported deaths. Lockdowns are the main public-health lever in Europe and much of the Americas. The genome sequence from Wu, Zhao and Zhang back in Episode one has been in GenBank for three months. The annotation of that genome — which proteins it encodes, which non-coding features it has, where the open reading frames start and stop — is largely inherited from what the field already knew about SARS-coronavirus and other betacoronaviruses. One particular feature of that inherited annotation is a short open reading frame near the three-prime end of the genome called O-R-F-ten. It's thirty-eight codons long. It has no homolog of known function. Nobody has shown that it's actually translated. It's in the annotation because someone drew a box around a plausible-looking stretch of sequence. That is the kind of question this paper is in a position to answer. More broadly, the paper is a stake in the ground: before you can study the virus's biology in detail, you want a definitive map of what R-N-A molecules the virus produces.

Here is the headline. Kim and Chang and colleagues infect African green monkey Vero cells with a Korean isolate of the virus, extract the total R-N-A, and run two orthogonal sequencing experiments on it. The first is nanopore direct R-N-A sequencing — a long-read technology that reads the R-N-A molecule itself without converting it to D-N-A, which means you can see modifications on the bases. The second is D-N-B-seq — a short-read, high-accuracy technology that gives them very deep coverage of junction sites. From the combined data they confirm nine canonical subgenomic R-N-As plus the full-length genomic R-N-A, and show that O-R-F-ten is essentially never expressed — the annotation should be reconsidered. They discover a large and previously under-appreciated class of noncanonical fusion transcripts, where the viral polymerase appears to be jumping between distant parts of the genome at sites that are not the canonical switch points. They measure poly-A tail lengths on individual molecules and show the tails come in two populations. And they identify forty-one sites in the viral R-N-A that carry chemical modifications, enriched at a short "A-A-G-A-A"-like motif, with the striking observation that modified R-N-A molecules carry systematically shorter poly-A tails than unmodified ones.

Now the background, because to appreciate what's being measured you have to understand how a coronavirus actually reads out its genome. A coronavirus is a positive-sense R-N-A virus with one of the largest R-N-A genomes known — around thirty kilobases. Positive-sense means the genomic R-N-A can be directly translated by host ribosomes, as if it were a messenger R-N-A. The virus takes advantage of this: as soon as it enters the cell, ribosomes land on the five-prime end of the genomic R-N-A and translate the first very long open reading frame, which actually comes in two linked variants — O-R-F-one-a, and, via a programmed minus-one ribosomal frameshift just before the O-R-F-one-a stop codon, the longer O-R-F-one-a-b. Those produce two giant polyproteins that are chopped by two viral proteases into sixteen non-structural proteins — the machinery of replication and R-N-A synthesis.

But if the virus had to translate the whole genome every time it wanted to make, say, nucleocapsid protein, it would be hopelessly inefficient. The ribosome scans from the five-prime end and almost always initiates at the first start codon it encounters, which is in O-R-F-one-a. So the structural and accessory proteins, which sit further down the genome, need their own messages. The coronavirus solution is a mechanism called discontinuous transcription, which produces a set of nested shorter messenger R-N-As called subgenomic R-N-As. Here is roughly how it works. The viral R-N-A-dependent R-N-A polymerase, when it is copying the genome into a negative-sense intermediate, pauses at short conserved sequence motifs called transcription regulatory sequences — T-R-S for short. There is a T-R-S near the five-prime end of the genome, in what's called the leader region — the T-R-S-L. And there are T-R-S motifs scattered through the body of the genome just upstream of each of the other open reading frames — the T-R-S-Bs. When the polymerase pauses at one of these body T-R-Ss, it can disengage from the template and reattach at the leader T-R-S, continuing synthesis from there. The product is a negative-strand R-N-A whose three-prime end is fused to the leader sequence, and when that negative strand is subsequently copied back into positive-sense R-N-A, you get a short subgenomic messenger with the leader sequence at its five-prime end and the body of one particular downstream open reading frame following. Each subgenomic R-N-A starts with the first open reading frame in its body, which is now the first start codon the ribosome encounters, so that is what gets translated.

So in principle the virus should produce a nested set of these subgenomic messages — one for spike, one for the small envelope protein, one for membrane, one for nucleocapsid, one for each accessory protein — all sharing the same five-prime leader. Prior work on SARS-coronavirus and on mouse hepatitis virus, decades of it, worked out this scheme. What was missing for SARS-coronavirus-two was experimental validation at scale. Which subgenomic R-N-As are actually made? In what proportions? Are the junction sites exactly where the T-R-S annotation says they should be? Are there additional junction sites that don't fit the canonical model? And are the R-N-As decorated with chemical modifications, as some other coronaviruses had hints of?

The reason direct nanopore sequencing is well-matched to this problem takes a moment to explain. In a conventional short-read sequencing experiment — Illumina or D-N-B-seq — you fragment the R-N-A, convert it to D-N-A, amplify it, sequence small pieces, and reassemble. That gives you very high accuracy and very deep coverage, which is great for counting things, but it destroys the single-molecule picture. You cannot tell which modifications co-occur on the same R-N-A molecule, you cannot resolve exactly how a nested transcript looks end-to-end, and you cannot read the poly-A tail at the three-prime end. In nanopore direct R-N-A sequencing — commercialised by Oxford Nanopore and the thing Hyeshik Chang's group had been building tools for — a single R-N-A molecule is threaded through a protein nanopore embedded in a membrane under a voltage. As each base moves through the constriction, it changes the current across the pore in a sequence-specific way. You read the current trace, and from the trace you call the bases. Because the chemistry is reading the R-N-A molecule itself — no reverse transcription, no amplification — you get a long read corresponding to one actual R-N-A, you can measure its poly-A tail by looking for the stretch of flat current corresponding to a run of adenosines, and you can detect base modifications, because a modified base perturbs the current signature differently from the unmodified version. The downside is that nanopore basecalling is noisier than Illumina, around five to ten percent per-base, and it has a known three-prime bias — the enzyme that feeds the pore grips the three-prime end and sometimes doesn't manage to reel through the whole molecule. For a nested set of coronaviral messages whose three-prime ends are all the same, that three-prime bias means the shorter messages get represented more heavily than they would in truth. Which is exactly where D-N-B-seq comes in — short reads, high depth, high accuracy, no three-prime bias. The two platforms complement each other, and the paper uses both.

So, the experiment. They infect Vero cells with the Korean isolate at a low multiplicity, harvest at twenty-four hours post-infection, extract total R-N-A, and split it. One aliquot goes into nanopore direct R-N-A sequencing on a MinION; the other goes into D-N-B-seq on an M-G-I platform. The MinION run gives them about eight hundred and eighty thousand reads from infected cells, of which sixty-five percent map to the virus — a striking number, because it means that twenty-four hours in, the viral R-N-A population has essentially taken over the cell. The genome is almost fully covered — missing only the last twelve nucleotides at the five-prime end, which is a known limitation of the nanopore chemistry. On top of that, they do a parallel experiment with in-vitro-transcribed R-N-As covering the whole genome — unmodified, synthetic R-N-As — which they also run on the nanopore. These act as the critical control for the modification analysis. If you don't have an unmodified reference, you cannot tell whether a weird current signature at a given position is a modification or just the natural quirks of the basecalling algorithm.

Let me walk through the results. The first thing they do is use the coverage pattern itself as evidence of the subgenomic R-N-As. If you plot read depth across the genome, you see sharp step-downs at exactly the positions where the canonical T-R-S-Bs sit, because at each step more of the shorter messages drop out from the pool contributing to that position's coverage. Every one of the nine canonical subgenomic R-N-As — spike, O-R-F-three-a, envelope, membrane, O-R-F-six, O-R-F-seven-a, O-R-F-seven-b, O-R-F-eight, and nucleocapsid — is directly supported by reads that span the predicted leader-to-body junction. Then they use the D-N-B-seq data, which has three hundred million reads and allows them to make a two-dimensional heatmap of junction sites: for every read that spans a gap, where is the five-prime side of the gap and where is the three-prime side. The expected canonical pattern — all the five-prime sides at the leader T-R-S, three-prime sides at each of the body T-R-Ss — comes out clearly. Nucleocapsid is overwhelmingly the most abundant subgenomic R-N-A, followed by spike, then O-R-F-seven-a, then O-R-F-three-a. That ordering is consistent with what the virus needs — nucleocapsid wraps every new genome, so it has to be made in huge amounts.

But O-R-F-ten is absent. In the D-N-B-seq data, out of all the reads that span a canonical leader-to-body junction, exactly one read supports a message encoding O-R-F-ten. One. That is zero-point-zero-zero-zero-zero-zero-nine percent of the junction-spanning reads. In the direct R-N-A sequencing data, O-R-F-ten is not supported at all. The authors are blunt about what this means: O-R-F-ten is unlikely to be expressed, and the annotation should be reconsidered.

The second major finding is a large population of noncanonical fusion transcripts. These come in three groups. The first is where you have the canonical leader at the five-prime side of the junction, but the three-prime side lands somewhere unexpected — in the middle of an open reading frame rather than at a canonical T-R-S-B. The second is long-distance fusions that don't involve the leader at all — the five-prime side of the junction is somewhere in the body of O-R-F-one-a, the three-prime side is a long way downstream, and the junction skips over thousands of nucleotides. The third is more local fusions, also not involving the leader, that create smaller internal deletions. Taken together these noncanonical events are not individually abundant, but in aggregate the long-distance group, in particular, accounts for roughly twenty percent as many reads as the full-length genome itself. The authors verify a subset of them by R-T-P-C-R, and show that they produce the expected amplicons. Mechanistically, some of the junctions have short sequence similarity between the two sides, suggesting that the polymerase is using tiny homology stretches to template-switch, and some have no such feature at all, so another mechanism may be at play. Several of these noncanonical events are predicted to produce truncated or alternative proteins — a truncated form of O-R-F-seven-b, for instance, that would differ from the annotated one by missing the first twenty-three amino acids. Whether these alternative proteins are actually translated and have functional roles is a question the paper flags but cannot answer.

The third finding is about poly-A tails. The polymerase that makes coronaviral R-N-As also puts a poly-A tail on the three-prime end, and the length of that tail can affect R-N-A stability and translation. By measuring the time an R-N-A spends in the nanopore while the poly-A region is passing through — essentially, how long you have to wait before the next distinct base comes through — the group can estimate the tail length on each individual molecule. The viral tails are shorter than typical cellular messenger R-N-As, with a median of forty-seven nucleotides. More interestingly, the distribution for the subgenomic R-N-As is bimodal — there's one peak around forty-five nucleotides, and a smaller peak around thirty. The authors speculate, pointing to prior work on bovine coronavirus, that the shorter tails represent older R-N-A molecules whose tails are being chewed back by host deadenylases — the tail length may be a kind of molecular age marker.

The fourth and most intricate finding is on R-N-A modifications. Here is where the control experiment with unmodified in-vitro-transcribed R-N-As matters. The authors first try the standard, control-free approach — they run the viral R-N-A through a modification-detection tool called Tombo, which has a pre-trained model for predicting five-methyl-cytosine. The tool nominates many candidate sites. But when they run the same tool on the unmodified in-vitro transcripts, Tombo nominates nearly identical sites. In other words, those "modifications" were false positives — artefacts of the basecaller, not real chemistry on the R-N-A. This is a really important methodological point, and it undercuts a published analysis of another coronavirus that had done modification calling without this kind of unmodified control. Lesson: you cannot trust a modification call based on signal alone; you need an unmodified reference.

Having set that up, they re-do the analysis by directly comparing the current traces at each position between viral R-N-A and in-vitro-transcribed R-N-A. Any position where the two differ substantially is a candidate real modification. They identify forty-one such sites across the genome where more than twenty percent of the viral molecules show a deviant signal. The modifications are not evenly distributed across the different subgenomic R-N-As — some sites are heavily modified on, say, the spike message but barely modified on the nucleocapsid message, even though both carry the same underlying sequence. The sites cluster at a short motif, A-A-G-A-A and other purine-rich sequences, and they are concentrated in the three-prime end of the genome, between positions roughly twenty-eight-thousand-five-hundred and twenty-nine-thousand-five-hundred. The longer subgenomic R-N-As — the genomic R-N-A itself, and the ones for spike, O-R-F-three-a, envelope, and membrane — are more frequently modified than the shorter ones.

And here is the result the paper is proudest of. Because the direct R-N-A sequencing reads out multiple features of the same molecule, they can ask: within a given subgenomic R-N-A, do the heavily modified molecules have different poly-A tails from the lightly modified ones? And the answer is yes — the heavily modified molecules have systematically shorter poly-A tails, with statistically very small p-values. This connects two things that nobody had connected before: internal chemical modification of the viral R-N-A, and three-prime turnover. The authors are careful to call this an association, not a mechanism, but it's a striking one, and it immediately suggests that whatever these modifications are, they may be part of how the virus controls the lifespan of its own messages.

What they can say about the chemical identity of the modifications is largely by exclusion. The classic N-six-methyladenosine mark, deposited by the host enzyme M-E-T-T-L-three, has a well-known consensus sequence — R-R-A-C-H — and the A-A-G-A-A motif the virus is using does not match it. R-N-A editing by the host A-D-A-R enzymes would convert adenosines to inosines, which get read as guanosines; they do not see the corresponding sequence changes in their D-N-B-seq data, so A-D-A-R editing is also ruled out. One-methyladenosine, which is known to make reverse transcriptases stall, would show up as premature termination of the reads, and they do not see that either. So they have excluded three of the usual suspects, and they do not know what the actual chemistry is. That is a question they explicitly hand off to future work.

Now the discussion — significance and limitations. The paper's claims amount to three things. First, that we now have an experimentally validated map of what the virus's thirty-kilobase genome actually produces inside a cell: nine canonical subgenomic R-N-As plus the full-length genome, a substantial population of noncanonical fusion transcripts, and some proteins — like the hypothetical product of O-R-F-ten — that the annotation should probably not promise anymore. Second, that the virus's R-N-As carry chemical modifications that are enriched at specific motifs, differ across subgenomic R-N-A species, and are coupled to poly-A tail length. Third, that this coupling implicates a layer of regulation — tails, modifications, and turnover — that the field has not yet investigated for coronaviruses and that is likely important for how the virus controls its own gene expression and possibly how it evades host innate immunity.

The limitations they are honest about. All of this is done in Vero cells, which are an African green monkey kidney line, not a human respiratory cell. Vero cells famously have a crippled type-one interferon response — they can't make their own interferon in response to viral infection — which was convenient for growing the virus but means that any aspect of the viral R-N-A landscape that depends on an intact innate immune system may be missed or distorted here. The modification results are statistical, from current-trace deviations, and the actual chemistry remains unidentified. The noncanonical junctions are reproducible in their data but the authors cannot say which, if any, are functionally relevant versus which are errors by the viral polymerase that get caught in sequencing. And they are explicit that twenty-four-hours post-infection in a kidney cell line is one snapshot in time of one host — the picture in a human lung at different stages of infection could look different.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment. The transcriptome architecture laid out here largely held up: the nine canonical subgenomic R-N-As, the absence of O-R-F-ten expression, the existence of noncanonical fusion transcripts. The AAGAA modifications took longer to resolve — subsequent work from several labs over the next couple of years argued for a combination of two-prime-O-methylation and N-six-methyladenosine depending on the site, and the field is still refining the picture. The paper's broader methodological argument — that nanopore direct R-N-A sequencing with proper unmodified controls is the right approach for viral transcriptomes — became part of the standard playbook, applied to other R-N-A viruses in the years that followed.
