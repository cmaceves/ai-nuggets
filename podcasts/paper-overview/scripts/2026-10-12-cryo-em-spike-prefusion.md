## Script

Paper link: Wrapp et al., "Cryo-EM structure of the 2019-nCoV spike in the prefusion conformation." Science, 19 February 2020. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb2507

Welcome back to SARS-CoV-2: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-CoV-2 literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we covered the Shi lab paper in Nature — the second of the two companion reports that opened the field, which showed experimentally that the new virus uses the human A-C-E-two receptor. Today we leave genomics for structural biology, because the next big paper was a solved structure.

Episode three. The paper is "Cryo-E-M structure of the two-oh-one-nine n-C-o-V spike in the prefusion conformation," published in Science on the nineteenth of February, 2020. The senior author is Jason McLellan at the University of Texas at Austin. First author is Daniel Wrapp. The lab had been working on coronavirus spike structural biology for years, going back to MERS and the common-cold coronavirus H-K-U-one — which is why they were in a position to turn around a structure this quickly.

Let's set the scene. Mid-February 2020. The companion genomes we covered in the last two episodes are two weeks old. The virus is still provisionally called two-thousand-nineteen n-C-o-V — the name SARS-coronavirus-two gets assigned by the taxonomy committee a few days before this paper comes out, and the disease name COVID-nineteen is assigned by the W-H-O on the same day. There are around seventy-five-thousand reported cases, almost all in China, and the first large outbreak outside China — on the Diamond Princess cruise ship — is actively unfolding. The W-H-O has declared a public health emergency of international concern; the pandemic declaration is still three weeks away. Vaccine programs have begun on the strength of the genome sequence alone — Moderna, by this point, had already shipped a candidate vaccine antigen for a phase-one trial. But nobody has an atomic-resolution picture yet of what the spike protein actually looks like.

Here's the headline. The McLellan lab expressed a stabilized version of the two-oh-one-nine n-C-o-V spike protein, imaged it by cryo-electron microscopy, and solved its structure at a resolution of three-and-a-half angstroms — the first atomic-level view of the new coronavirus's spike in its prefusion shape. They showed the spike is a trimer of three identical subunits, and that at any given moment one of the three receptor-binding domains tends to be flipped up into a receptor-accessible position while the other two lie down. They measured how tightly the spike binds the human A-C-E-two receptor, and found it binds with roughly fifteen-nanomolar affinity — approximately ten to twenty times tighter than the original SARS-coronavirus spike binds the same receptor. And they tested three published monoclonal antibodies that neutralize SARS-coronavirus and found that none of them bind the new virus's receptor-binding domain, which told the field very early on that cross-reactive therapies from the SARS era were going to be in short supply.

Now the background, because the methodology of this paper only makes sense in the context of what coronavirus spike structural biology had learned the hard way over the previous decade. The spike is what's called a class-one viral fusion protein. It is a large, heavily-glycosylated trimer that sits on the viral surface — the coronavirus crown is just a dense forest of these trimers. Each of the three identical protomers has two functional halves, called S-one and S-two. S-one carries the receptor-binding domain — I'll call it the R-B-D — which is the part that physically grips the host receptor. S-two sits underneath and houses the fusion machinery that, when triggered, drives the viral and host membranes together. Here's the critical structural point. Class-one fusion proteins are spring-loaded. They start out in a high-energy state called the prefusion conformation, which is what sits on the virus particle. When they engage the receptor and get an activating protease cut, they snap irreversibly into a lower-energy state called the postfusion conformation, like a mousetrap going off, and that rearrangement is what pulls the membranes together. For vaccine design and for therapeutic antibody discovery, the prefusion state is what you want — because that is the shape of spike on an intact virion, and most of the surfaces that elicit potent neutralizing antibodies live only on the prefusion form. The problem is that purified prefusion spike is unstable. It falls apart. If you spit out spike protein from a cell line and purify it, by the time you look at it in the electron microscope a lot of it has already triggered into the postfusion state, which is the wrong target.

McLellan and his collaborators had, over the previous five years, developed a general trick for solving this problem. They had worked out that if you take a class-one fusion protein and introduce two proline residues at a specific hinge in what's called the central helix of the fusion machinery, you stabilise the prefusion state in place — the two prolines kink the backbone in a way that physically blocks the conformational change. They called this the two-P modification, two-P as in two prolines. It had been shown to work for the respiratory syncytial virus fusion protein, and McLellan's group had previously applied it to the MERS and H-K-U-one coronavirus spikes. So when the two-oh-one-nine n-C-o-V genome dropped in January 2020, they already had a template. They mapped the two-P sites onto the new virus's spike and could design a stabilized construct within days. The gap this paper fills, really, is turning that recipe into an actual atomic structure for the virus that was then spreading across the world.

So what did they do. The construct they expressed was the full spike ectodomain — residues one through twelve-oh-eight — with four engineered changes. First, the two-P stabilising mutations in the S-two central helix, as described. Second, they mutated the furin cleavage site at the junction between S-one and S-two. The two-oh-one-nine n-C-o-V spike has an unusual multi-arginine motif at this junction — the sequence R-R-A-R — that is cleaved by a host protease called furin. They replaced it with the four amino acids G-S-A-S, which is a neutral linker that can't be cut, to keep the S-one and S-two halves tethered together and prevent premature triggering. Third, they added a trimerisation motif at the C-terminal end — the T-four phage fibritin "foldon" — to help the three subunits assemble into a proper trimer. And fourth, they added purification tags. They expressed this construct in human embryonic kidney cells, purified it by affinity and size-exclusion chromatography, and recovered it at a modest yield of about half a milligram per litre of culture. Then they vitrified it onto cryo-E-M grids.

A quick primer on cryo-electron microscopy, since it's the beating heart of this paper. Cryo-E-M works by rapidly freezing a thin layer of your protein solution into a sheet of amorphous — glass-like — ice, so the molecules are preserved in whatever conformations they occupy in solution, in random orientations. You then image them in the electron microscope under very low electron dose, which gives you an enormous number of extremely noisy, two-dimensional projections of individual molecules. The computational step is where the resolution comes from. You classify those projections, figure out what three-dimensional orientation each one corresponds to, align them to each other, and average thousands or millions of them together to build a three-dimensional density map of the molecule. From that map, if the resolution is high enough — three-and-a-half angstroms, as in this paper — you can trace the protein's amino acid backbone and model the side chains, and get an atomic model.

For this spike, they collected over three thousand micrograph movies and processed about two-hundred thousand individual particles. One of the key observations came early: when they classified the particles, they could clearly see that the three receptor-binding domains in the trimer were not all doing the same thing. In most of the particles, two of the three R-B-Ds were tucked down flat against the body of the spike, in what they call the receptor-binding-domain-down conformation. The third R-B-D was rotated up away from the body of the spike, in what they call the receptor-binding-domain-up conformation. That asymmetric arrangement — two down, one up — turns out to be the predominant state of the spike in solution. To get the highest-resolution map, they reconstructed the trimer without imposing symmetry — because imposing three-fold symmetry would blur the up and down R-B-Ds together — and they got three-point-five angstroms overall. For the more rigid S-two region at the base of the molecule, they could impose three-fold symmetry across the symmetric parts and sharpened that part of the map further, to about three-point-two angstroms. The final atomic model covers residues twenty-seven through eleven-forty-six and resolves glycans at forty-four of the sixty-six potential glycosylation sites per trimer.

Why does the up-versus-down conformation matter. Because the receptor binding site on the R-B-D — the surface that actually contacts A-C-E-two — is physically occluded when the R-B-D is in the down position. It's buried against the body of the trimer and can't reach the receptor. The receptor-binding site is only exposed when the R-B-D flips up. So this hinge-like motion is a gating mechanism. The spike has to breathe — individual R-B-Ds have to transiently rotate up — in order for the virus to actually engage A-C-E-two. The authors directly observed this breathing in their data. They used a technique called three-dimensional variability analysis, which lets you resolve a continuum of conformations within a single data set rather than treating everything as a static average, and they saw the R-B-D hinging between up and down states.

Now on to the receptor-binding measurements. They expressed the human A-C-E-two ectodomain and asked how tightly the stabilized spike binds it, using surface plasmon resonance. Surface plasmon resonance works by immobilising one binding partner on a sensor chip and flowing the other across it in solution; the technique then measures, in real time, how the mass on the chip surface changes as molecules bind and dissociate, from which you can calculate the association rate, the dissociation rate, and the equilibrium binding constant. For the stabilized two-oh-one-nine n-C-o-V spike binding to human A-C-E-two, the equilibrium dissociation constant — the K-d — came out at about fifteen nanomolar. They then ran the same experiment with the SARS-coronavirus spike, which had been studied for years, and found that it bound the same human A-C-E-two with a K-d in the hundred-and-fifty to three-hundred-and-twenty nanomolar range — in other words, the two-oh-one-nine spike binds A-C-E-two about ten to twenty times tighter than the SARS spike does. That was a striking finding at the time. The high affinity was consistent with — but did not by itself prove — the clinical observation that the new virus seemed to spread efficiently between people. The authors are careful to say this: it is a plausible contributor to transmissibility, not a demonstration.

The last experimental piece is the cross-reactivity test. During and after the 2003 SARS outbreak, several neutralizing monoclonal antibodies had been characterised against the SARS-coronavirus R-B-D. The question for the new virus was: do any of those still work. The authors tested three well-characterised SARS-coronavirus R-B-D-directed antibodies — called S-two-three-zero, m-three-nine-six, and eighty-R — against the two-oh-one-nine n-C-o-V receptor-binding domain, using biolayer interferometry, which is another binding-kinetics technique. All three antibodies bound the SARS-coronavirus R-B-D strongly as expected. None of the three detectably bound the two-oh-one-nine R-B-D at the concentration tested. That negative result was important early-warning information: even though the two viruses are both sarbecoviruses and share the same receptor, their receptor-binding surfaces have diverged enough that a SARS-coronavirus antibody is not, by default, going to protect you from the new virus. Any monoclonal-antibody therapy was going to have to be developed afresh, from new antibodies raised against the new virus's spike.

They also made a structural comparison between the new virus's spike and the SARS-coronavirus spike. Overall, the two trimers are very similar. The structural root-mean-square deviation — a standard measure of how closely two structures superimpose — is a few angstroms over most of the protein, with the fusion-machinery S-two subunit most conserved and the S-one domains slightly more variable. The biggest structural difference is in the resting position of the down R-B-D: in the two-oh-one-nine spike the down R-B-D tilts a little further in toward the central cavity of the trimer than it does in SARS-coronavirus. That subtle geometric difference may be part of why the authors could not detect binding by SARS antibodies — the epitopes those antibodies target are presented slightly differently on the new spike.

Why did this matter. Three things. First, this paper handed the field a direct atomic template for vaccine antigen design. If you are designing an m-R-N-A vaccine, or a protein subunit vaccine, or a viral-vector vaccine, you want the antigen to adopt the native prefusion shape — because that is the shape the immune system needs to recognise to generate neutralizing antibodies against intact virions. The two-P stabilization strategy, together with the solved structure confirming that the construct really does adopt the prefusion fold, became the design blueprint for essentially every spike-targeting COVID vaccine that reached a phase-one trial. Second, it made the up-versus-down R-B-D dynamic a central feature of how everyone thought about the spike afterwards — both for drug design, because you need the R-B-D up to engage receptor, and for antibody characterisation, because some antibodies turn out to recognise R-B-D-up epitopes, some recognise R-B-D-down epitopes, and some recognise the quaternary structure that only exists when the R-B-D is down. Third, the apparent ten-to-twenty-fold higher receptor affinity provided a mechanistic hypothesis — not a proof — for the clinical observation of efficient human-to-human transmission.

What they were honest about. They are careful not to claim the high A-C-E-two affinity explains transmissibility; they note explicitly that additional studies are needed. They note that the stabilizing mutations and the furin-site swap mean this is not literally the wild-type protein, and that their measurements are of the stabilized construct. They note that forty-four of sixty-six glycosylation sites being resolved means a lot of the glycan shield is only partially visible. And they explicitly flag the possibility that other, as-yet-unidentified SARS-coronavirus antibodies — including antibodies not in the panel they tested — might cross-react.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment, briefly. The two-P stabilization strategy from this paper became the antigen in the Moderna, Pfizer-BioNTech, and Janssen COVID vaccines. The McLellan lab subsequently developed a further-stabilized construct called HexaPro, with six proline substitutions, that expresses better and is even more stable, and that construct became the preferred antigen for subsequent protein vaccines and research reagents. The up-versus-down R-B-D dynamic has been seen in every major variant studied since — including Alpha, Beta, Delta, and Omicron — and modulating that dynamic turns out to be one of the ways variants tune their transmissibility and immune profile. The lack of cross-reactivity with SARS-coronavirus antibodies was borne out: the therapeutic monoclonals that reached patients were all raised against the new virus's spike, not repurposed from the SARS era.

That's episode three. The paper reference and link are in the show notes. Next time we go back to genomics — "The proximal origin of SARS-CoV-2," by Andersen, Rambaut, Lipkin, Holmes, and Garry in Nature Medicine, published a month after this one.
