## Script

Paper link: Gordon et al., "A SARS-CoV-2 protein interaction map reveals targets for drug repurposing." Nature, 30 April 2020. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2286-9

Welcome back to SARS-coronavirus-two: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-coronavirus-two literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we were in Cell with Markus Hoffmann and Stefan Pöhlmann, pinning down the entry pathway — A-C-E-two as the receptor, T-M-P-R-S-S-two as the lung-relevant priming protease, and camostat as an already-approved drug that blocks the step. Today we zoom out one more notch. Rather than nominating a single host protein because of a particular piece of biology that was already understood, we ask a much more general question. Across all twenty-nine proteins that this virus makes, which human proteins does each of them touch? And of those human proteins, which ones already have drugs pointed at them?

Episode eight. The paper is "A SARS-coronavirus-two protein interaction map reveals targets for drug repurposing," published in Nature on the thirtieth of April, 2020. The first author is David Gordon, and the senior author is Nevan Krogan at the Quantitative Biosciences Institute at the University of California, San Francisco, with a sprawling collaboration of more than a hundred authors across U-C-S-F, the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, the Institut Pasteur in Paris, and the Gladstone Institutes. The Krogan group had spent the previous decade building protein–protein interaction maps for one virus after another — H-I-V, Ebola, dengue, Zika — using the same mass-spectrometry framework. When the new coronavirus arrived, they already had the pipeline. By the first week of March 2020, they had scoped the project. By the end of March, they had posted the first draft of the map on bioRxiv. And by the thirtieth of April, it was in Nature.

Let me set the scene. End of April 2020. The pandemic has gone fully global. The United States has become the single largest reservoir of cases, with New York City in the trough of its first wave and much of the rest of the country starting to climb. Most of Europe has been in some form of lockdown for six weeks. There are something on the order of three million confirmed cases worldwide and more than two hundred thousand reported deaths. On the therapeutic side, a few things are happening in parallel. Remdesivir, from the A-C-T-T-one trial, has just reported a modest reduction in time-to-recovery and will be granted emergency use authorisation at the start of May. Camostat trials — the ones that chase the Hoffmann and Pöhlmann result from Episode seven — are being spun up. Hydroxychloroquine is being prescribed and defended politically well out ahead of the clinical data, and the first controlled trials against it are reading out as negative or null. There are no approved antibodies and no vaccines. The field is desperate for therapies that can be deployed on timescales shorter than it takes to design a new drug — which is to say, for drugs that already exist in the clinic and can be aimed at this new virus. That is the problem this paper sets out to attack systematically.

Here is the headline. Gordon and Krogan and colleagues clone and tag twenty-six of the virus's twenty-nine proteins, express them one at a time in human cells, pull each one back out of the lysate on an affinity tag, and ask by mass spectrometry which human proteins came along for the ride. They identify three hundred and thirty-two high-confidence interactions between viral proteins and human proteins, covering an unusually broad slice of host biology — vesicle trafficking, nuclear transport, innate immune signalling, the ribosome and translation machinery, ubiquitin ligases, and chromatin regulators. They then overlay that interactome with the chemical landscape, matching those three hundred and thirty-two human proteins to known drugs and tool compounds, and come up with a shortlist of sixty-nine compounds — twenty-nine F-D-A-approved drugs, twelve already in clinical trials, and twenty-eight preclinical tool molecules — that engage sixty-six of those human proteins. They take a subset of these compounds, feed them to actual SARS-coronavirus-two in two independent containment labs, and show that two chemical classes consistently block viral replication. The first class are inhibitors of protein synthesis that act on the cap-binding complex at the start of mRNA translation. The second, more surprisingly, are small molecules whose common feature is that they bind a pair of poorly understood receptors called the sigma receptors.

Now the background, because the premise behind this kind of paper is not obvious. For a direct antiviral — one that targets a viral enzyme like a polymerase or a protease — the drug-design logic is simple. The virus mutates, and whenever a mutation changes a residue in the enzyme's active site in a way that affects drug binding, you have resistance. H-I-V taught the field this at scale in the nineteen-nineties. One answer is to target not the virus's own proteins but the host-cell proteins that the virus has to use to replicate — the receptors it binds, the proteases that cut its spike, the ribosomes that make its proteins, the vesicles that carry its R-N-A. Host factors are not under the virus's selective control; mutations that break them break the host long before they break the drug's binding pocket. The flip side is that if your drug blocks a protein the host needs to live, you have a toxicity problem rather than a resistance problem. The programme behind this paper rests on an older bet: for an acute viral infection in which treatment is a matter of days to weeks, hitting a host factor transiently is often tolerable, and the resistance headroom you gain is worth it. The Krogan group had been making the case for this approach, with viral interaction maps as the entry point, since the mid-two-thousand-tens.

The technical trick at the heart of the paper is called affinity-purification mass spectrometry — A-P-M-S. The idea is this. You take a single viral protein and fuse it to a short tag that binds a known affinity resin — in this paper, a tag called two-x-Strep, which sticks tightly to streptavidin beads. You transfect that construct into a line of human cells, in this case H-E-K-two-ninety-three-T cells, which are easy to express high levels of exogenous protein in. You let the viral protein find its partners in those cells. Then you lyse the cells, pour the lysate over the strep beads, wash, and elute. Whatever comes off the beads should be the tagged viral protein plus any human protein that was holding its hand. You digest everything into peptides, run the peptides through a mass spectrometer, and read out which human proteins were present and in what abundance. You do this once per viral protein, and in parallel do a battery of control pulldowns — an empty tag, an unrelated bait — so you can distinguish genuine partners from sticky background. Finally, you push the data through statistical scoring algorithms that look at how reproducibly a human protein came up in the tagged pulldowns relative to the controls and assign a confidence score. The ones above threshold are your high-confidence interactions.

The scale here is the news. SARS-coronavirus-two encodes twenty-nine proteins — four structural proteins including spike, nucleocapsid, membrane and envelope; sixteen so-called non-structural proteins that come out of the big polyprotein encoded by the first open reading frame of the genome; and nine accessory proteins, the little regulatory factors scattered through the rest of the genome. Gordon and colleagues cloned twenty-six of these twenty-nine. The remaining three were omitted for technical reasons. For each of the twenty-six, they did the pulldown in biological replicates in H-E-K-two-ninety-three-T cells, ran the mass spectrometer, and ran the scoring. Replicate-to-replicate correlations ranged from about point four six to point seven two, which is in the usual range for a well-executed A-P-M-S experiment. In the end they called three hundred and thirty-two high-confidence virus-to-human protein interactions, averaging about twelve human partners per viral bait, with substantial variation — some viral proteins touched one or two human proteins, others touched dozens.

Three hundred and thirty-two interactions is a lot of entries to make sense of. The authors do two things with them. The first is to ask, cell biologically, what the virus is reaching for. The strongest signals cluster into a handful of themes. Vesicle and endomembrane trafficking is the dominant one — roughly forty percent of the human partners sit on or around the Golgi apparatus, the endoplasmic reticulum, or the vesicular transport system. That makes sense: coronaviruses replicate inside membrane rearrangements that they induce from these compartments, and they bud out of them. Nuclear transport is another cluster — several viral non-structural proteins, and the accessory protein from open reading frame six, interact with components of the nuclear pore and with the mRNA export machinery, consistent with the virus trying to prevent the host's own transcripts, including interferon-response transcripts, from leaving the nucleus and being translated. Innate immunity comes up — several viral proteins touch factors in the interferon pathway and in the N-F-kappa-B signalling cascade. The ribosome and translation machinery come up — the nucleocapsid protein, the second non-structural protein, and the eighth non-structural protein each pull down ribosomal factors and translation initiation factors, which fits with the virus needing to redirect protein synthesis towards its own messages. Ubiquitin ligases come up — the small accessory protein from open reading frame ten pulls down a ubiquitin ligase complex involving the Cullin-two scaffold and a substrate adapter. Chromatin regulators come up — the envelope protein pulls down bromodomain proteins B-R-D-two and B-R-D-four, which read acetylated histones and are themselves the target of a well-known class of cancer drugs. Across the whole set, the human partners are enriched for genes that are highly expressed in the lung relative to the rest of the body — median expression about eightfold higher than the genome-wide median. That is reassuring: these interactions, which were measured in a kidney cell line, are made with proteins that in a human are plausibly present where the virus is actually replicating.

The second thing they do with the interactome is the drug-matching exercise. They walk through the three hundred and thirty-two human proteins and ask, for each one, whether there is a published small-molecule ligand — a drug, a clinical candidate, or a tool compound — that engages it. They draw on chemoinformatics databases and on the published medicinal chemistry literature. The output is sixty-nine compounds that collectively hit sixty-six of the human partners. Twenty-nine of those compounds are F-D-A-approved drugs for one indication or another. Twelve are in clinical trials for something else. Twenty-eight are preclinical tool compounds — things that work in cells but have not been developed as drugs. This is, at the time it was published, by some margin the largest systematically-generated repurposing shortlist pointed at this virus.

The last and most important step is to actually test these compounds against the virus. The authors picked forty-seven of the sixty-nine, roughly on the basis of availability and selectivity, and screened them in two independent biosafety-level-three laboratories using two different readouts. At the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York, they infected African green monkey Vero-E6 cells — a kidney line that supports SARS-coronavirus-two replication — with authentic virus and read out infection by immunofluorescence staining of the viral nucleocapsid protein. At the Institut Pasteur in Paris, they infected cells and read out infection by quantitative P-C-R on the viral R-N-A in the supernatant. Each lab tested in dose-response, and the authors looked for compounds that reduced viral signal consistently in both.

Two chemical classes stood out. The first is translation inhibitors — specifically, compounds that engage the eukaryotic initiation factor four-A, or e-I-F-four-A — one of the components of the cap-binding complex at the five-prime end of a eukaryotic mRNA, which is what unwinds secondary structure in the message so that ribosomes can scan to the start codon. The paper highlights two of them. One is a molecule called zotatifin, which was in clinical development as an anti-cancer agent and is a selective e-I-F-four-A inhibitor. The other is a natural-product-derived compound called ternatin-four, which inhibits a related step in translation elongation. Both reduced viral replication in a dose-dependent way in both labs. The mechanism being proposed is plausible: the virus has a positive-sense R-N-A genome that is translated like a cellular mRNA, and if you cripple cap-dependent translation, you cripple viral protein synthesis. There is also a secondary effect — e-I-F-four-A inhibitors can trigger the formation of stress granules, which are cytoplasmic condensates of untranslated mRNAs and R-N-A-binding proteins that themselves have antiviral activity.

The second class is more unexpected. The sigma receptors are two poorly characterised membrane-associated proteins, called sigma-one and sigma-two. They were first described decades ago as pharmacological binding sites for a hodgepodge of psychiatric and antihistamine drugs; sigma-two in particular had been identified, only recently to this paper, as a protein called T-M-E-M-ninety-seven. The viral protein that reaches for them in the interactome is the ninth non-structural protein, and the authors argue that these receptors sit at the endoplasmic-reticulum interface where the virus assembles its replication compartments. Several very different-looking drugs happen to be good sigma-receptor ligands, and the paper tests a number of them. A tool compound called P-B-twenty-eight, a sigma-one and sigma-two agonist, has strong antiviral activity. A sigma-two-selective tool compound called P-D-one-four-four-four-one-eight has activity. Clemastine, an over-the-counter antihistamine that also happens to engage sigma-one, has activity. And hydroxychloroquine and chloroquine, the molecules that at the moment this paper is being published are at the centre of a political argument about COVID therapy — those molecules too bind sigma receptors, and have modest antiviral activity in the same assays. The paper is explicit about what this implies, and the implication is important. The commonly invoked mechanism for chloroquine's antiviral activity in cell culture had been that it raises endosomal p-H and so blocks the cathepsin-dependent entry pathway. In the sigma-receptor framework, the authors are arguing that in the cell lines where chloroquine looks active, the active mechanism may well be sigma-receptor engagement rather than endosomal p-H effects. More sharply, they point out that hydroxychloroquine has roughly thirty-fold selectivity between the sigma receptors and the h-E-R-G cardiac potassium channel, whereas the dedicated sigma-receptor tool compounds like P-B-twenty-eight have five-hundred to five-thousand-fold selectivity. If you want to engage the sigma receptors therapeutically, hydroxychloroquine is the wrong tool for the job — it is more likely to hit h-E-R-G and cause cardiac arrhythmias at concentrations where it would meaningfully engage its intended target. There is one further and rather neat control: dextromethorphan, the cough suppressant, is a sigma-one antagonist, where everything else they are testing is an agonist. Dextromethorphan does the opposite of the others — it increases viral titre in the assay. The direction switch is consistent with sigma-one agonism being the antiviral mechanism, and strengthens the authors' confidence that they are reading the sigma signal correctly rather than catching some unrelated off-target effect.

Rounding out the hits, the paper flags a few more compounds that come out of other arms of the interactome. Selinexor, a drug approved for multiple myeloma, inhibits the nuclear export factor exportin-one, and the authors argue it should block the open-reading-frame-six accessory protein's hijacking of nuclear export. Silmitasertib, an inhibitor of casein kinase two, which the nucleocapsid pulls down. P-S-three-oh-six-one, a tool inhibitor of the Sec-sixty-one translocon — the channel through which secretory proteins enter the endoplasmic reticulum, which several viral proteins engage.

Now the discussion. The authors make three main claims. First, that a protein interaction map is a productive scaffold for drug discovery against a pandemic virus — in a matter of weeks you can go from "virus arrived" to "drug shortlist tested in biosafety-level-three", if the technology is already in hand. Second, that host-directed antivirals are a worthwhile complement to direct-acting antivirals like remdesivir — resistance pressure is applied to the host, not the virus, and the risk of selecting for resistance mutations is correspondingly lower. Third, that two particular host processes — cap-dependent translation and sigma-receptor-mediated signalling at the endoplasmic reticulum — look like promising hubs that current chemistry can already engage, with specific compounds in each class worth taking into animal models.

What they are honest about. These experiments are done in H-E-K-two-ninety-three-T cells, which are kidney not lung and are a transformed line. The interactome is measured one viral protein at a time, out of the context of an actual infection — the real network during replication is unlikely to be exactly the sum of twenty-six single-bait pulldowns. The antiviral assays are in Vero-E6 cells, which are a monkey kidney line and use predominantly the endosomal entry route the Hoffmann paper described, meaning results here may or may not transfer to a cell that relies on T-M-P-R-S-S-two. Forty-seven of the sixty-nine candidate compounds were tested, leaving a long tail unscreened. And they are careful to note that activity in a dish is not evidence of clinical benefit — these are starting points for programmes, not endpoints.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment. The sigma-receptor arm aged unevenly: when the same compounds were tested in human lung cell lines over the next year, several of the hits, including hydroxychloroquine, did not reproduce — suggesting that at least part of the Vero-E6 signal was tied to that cell line's heavy reliance on the endosomal entry route. The translation-inhibitor arm aged better: zotatifin went on into COVID clinical trials. And the paper's broader point — that fast, systematic interactome-plus-chemistry pipelines are a legitimate pandemic response — became part of the standard playbook over the following two years.
