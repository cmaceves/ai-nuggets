## Script

Paper link: Zhou et al., "A pneumonia outbreak associated with a new coronavirus of probable bat origin." Nature, 3 February 2020. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2012-7

Welcome back to SARS-CoV-2: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-CoV-2 literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we covered the Wu, Zhang and Holmes paper in Nature — one of the two reports that opened the field. Today we cover the second of that pair.

Episode two. The paper is "A pneumonia outbreak associated with a new coronavirus of probable bat origin," published in Nature on the third of February, 2020. The senior author is Zheng-Li Shi at the Wuhan Institute of Virology — her group had spent the previous decade sampling SARS-like coronaviruses from bats across China, which turns out to matter a great deal for how this paper unfolds. First authors are Peng Zhou, Xing-Lou Yang, and Xian-Guang Wang.

Let's set the scene. Early February 2020. The paper reports that, as of the twenty-sixth of January, the Wuhan outbreak had grown to about two-thousand seven-hundred and sixty-one cases in China, eighty deaths, and thirty-three cases in ten other countries. Human-to-human transmission has been confirmed. The provisional name is still two-thousand-nineteen novel coronavirus — written as two-oh-one-nine dash n dash C-o-V. The first-generation diagnostic P-C-R assays are being developed and distributed. The genome from the companion Wu paper, which we covered last time, has been released to GenBank. And a second lab — the group at the Wuhan Institute of Virology that specialises in bat coronaviruses — has independently been sequencing patient samples. This is their report.

Here's the headline. The Shi lab took bronchoalveolar lavage and swab samples from seven ICU patients at the Wuhan Jin Yin-Tan Hospital — six of whom were sellers or deliverymen from the local seafood market — and recovered five full-length genomes of this new coronavirus. They showed the virus is a sarbecovirus related to, but clearly distinct from, SARS-coronavirus — about eighty percent genome identity. Then they pulled a bat coronavirus called R-A-T-G-thirteen out of their own archived samples from a cave in Yunnan province, sequenced it in full, and found it was ninety-six-point-two percent identical to the new virus across the whole genome — the closest known relative. They isolated the virus in cell culture, confirmed by electron microscopy and immunofluorescence that it was a coronavirus. And they showed experimentally — by expressing different A-C-E-two proteins on cells that normally resist infection and asking whether the virus could then enter — that the new virus uses human A-C-E-two as its receptor, which is the same receptor used by the original SARS virus. That receptor result is the one most people cite from this paper.

Now the background. Why was the Shi lab the right lab to do this, and why does the methodology look the way it does. Shi's group had been doing bat coronavirus surveillance in Chinese caves for over a decade following the original SARS outbreak of two-thousand-three. They had built up a large collection of bat sarbecoviruses — SARS-related coronaviruses — along with the diagnostic tools to detect them. So when a severe pneumonia started appearing in Wuhan in late December, with a cluster around a market, the shape of the problem was immediately familiar: respiratory, winter, market, animal contact. The first move the paper describes is simple and consequential — they used what are called pan-coronavirus P-C-R primers on the patient samples. "Pan-coronavirus" means the primers are designed against a region of the viral genome that is conserved across the whole coronavirus family, so they can detect a coronavirus you've never seen before, as long as it has coronavirus-like genes. Five of the seven patient samples came back positive. That's the opening data point of the paper, and it's what directs the whole rest of the investigation toward a coronavirus genome.

So what did they do. One of the P-C-R-positive samples was a bronchoalveolar lavage — fluid flushed into and recovered from the deep lung — from a patient they call ICU-oh-six. They put that sample through next-generation sequencing in an unbiased, metagenomic mode. The idea is the same as in the Wu paper we covered last time: extract everything, sequence everything, let the computer find the virus. The scale here is instructive. They got about ten million total sequencing reads out of the sample. After filtering out reads that mapped to the human genome, only about one-and-a-half thousand reads were left — the vast majority of what you sequence from a lung sample is human. Of those non-human reads, about eighty-seven percent matched known SARS-related coronaviruses. They assembled those reads de novo — meaning without using a reference genome as a scaffold — closed the gaps with targeted P-C-R, and ended up with a twenty-nine-thousand eight-hundred ninety-one base-pair coronavirus genome. That genome shared about seventy-nine-point-six percent nucleotide identity with the original SARS-coronavirus reference. That's related — same subgenus, sarbecovirus — but it's a distinct virus. By the standard species-definition rule for coronaviruses, which looks at the conserved replicase domains at the amino-acid level, the identity to SARS-coronavirus was about ninety-four percent, high enough that the two viruses are classified as the same species. They tentatively named the new virus two-thousand-nineteen n-C-o-V, following the W-H-O's provisional name.

They then went back and got four more full-length genomes from four additional patient samples, using a mix of next-generation sequencing and conventional P-C-R. All five genomes were more than ninety-nine-point-nine percent identical to one another, which is the signature of a recent single-source outbreak — the virus had not been in people long enough to accumulate significant diversity between these cases.

Then comes the bat part, which is where the Shi lab's archive paid off. They searched their own records of partial coronavirus sequences from bat samples and found a short fragment of the R-N-A-dependent R-N-A polymerase gene — call it the Rd-R-p — from a coronavirus they had previously detected in a horseshoe bat of the species Rhinolophus affinis, sampled in Yunnan province. That partial fragment was a close match to the new virus. So they went back to the archived R-N-A sample, which was called R-A-T-G-thirteen, and sequenced its full genome for the first time. The full genome of R-A-T-G-thirteen turned out to be ninety-six-point-two percent identical to two-oh-one-nine n-C-o-V, across the full roughly thirty-thousand nucleotides. Across the panel of coronaviruses in their phylogenetic analysis, R-A-T-G-thirteen was the closest known relative — but the two viruses still form a distinct lineage together, separate from other known SARS-related bat coronaviruses. The authors tested formally for recombination within the new virus's genome using the aligned sequences and found no evidence of a recent recombination event in this virus's history.

They then zoomed in on the spike protein — the surface protein that lets the virus bind and enter cells — and particularly on the region of spike that physically grips the host receptor. The spike gene of the new virus and of R-A-T-G-thirteen is longer than other SARS-related coronaviruses, because of three short insertions in what's called the N-terminal domain. More importantly for predicting what the virus might do: in the receptor-binding motif — the short stretch of amino acids that makes direct contact with the host receptor — four out of five of the key residues that SARS-coronavirus uses to grip A-C-E-two are different in the new virus. That difference is important two ways. It warned the field that antibodies raised against SARS-coronavirus spike might not neutralize this one, which is a problem for repurposing anything. And it set up the question that the back half of the paper actually answers experimentally: does this virus still use A-C-E-two despite those substitutions.

To answer that, they ran a receptor-usage experiment. The method is worth taking slowly because it's a classic entry-biology design. They used HeLa cells, which are a human cell line that doesn't naturally express A-C-E-two and is not readily infected by SARS-like coronaviruses. They transfected the HeLa cells with plasmids encoding A-C-E-two from five different species — human, Chinese horseshoe bat, civet, pig, and mouse — or left them untransfected as a negative control. Then they tried to infect the cells with the new virus. The read-out is immunofluorescence: viral protein staining shows up red in cells where the virus successfully got in and replicated, and the A-C-E-two protein being expressed shows up green. The result was clean. Red staining appeared in cells expressing human, bat, civet, or pig A-C-E-two. Cells expressing mouse A-C-E-two were not infected. Untransfected HeLa cells were not infected. As a counter-check, they also tested the other two main coronavirus receptors known in humans — aminopeptidase N, which is the receptor for human coronavirus two-two-nine-E, and dipeptidyl peptidase four, which is the receptor for MERS-coronavirus — and the new virus could not use either of those. So the new virus uses A-C-E-two, it uses several species' versions of A-C-E-two, and the mouse version is specifically a poor fit — which, as a practical matter, meant that laboratory mice would need to be engineered to carry a human A-C-E-two before they could be used as a model.

A few other pieces of the paper. They developed a q-P-C-R diagnostic assay targeting a variable region of the spike gene, and showed it discriminates the new virus from a panel of other coronaviruses including the closely related bat virus W-I-V-one. They used an existing nucleocapsid antigen — the N protein from a bat SARS-related coronavirus called R-p-three, which shares about ninety-two percent identity with the N protein of the new virus — to build an in-house I-g-G and I-g-M ELISA. In the one patient they could track across multiple time points, both I-g-G and I-g-M antibody levels rose over the course of infection, which is textbook seroconversion. In five of the seven patients tested about twenty days after symptom onset, all were strongly I-g-G-positive. They isolated the virus itself from the lung-wash sample in Vero E-six and Huh-seven cells, saw clear cytopathic effects — meaning the cells visibly died — confirmed the identity of the virus by immunofluorescence and metagenomic resequencing of the culture supernatant, and imaged the cultured virus by electron microscopy. The electron micrographs show particles with the fringe of surface spikes that gives the family its name — the coronavirus crown — in intracellular vesicles, exactly as you'd expect.

Why did this matter. Three things. First, independent confirmation. The companion Wu paper reported one genome from one patient. This paper reported five genomes from five patients in a different lab, with the sequences almost identical to each other and to the Wu paper's genome. That triangulation is what let the field be confident, very quickly, that a single new virus was behind the outbreak, rather than a mixed-aetiology cluster. Second, the receptor result. Everyone had suspected A-C-E-two based on sequence homology with SARS, including the Wu paper's prediction, but this paper actually ran the entry assay and showed it. That is the moment the field pivoted — vaccine designers, monoclonal-antibody developers, antiviral programs — onto spike and A-C-E-two as the entry pathway to target. Third, the ancestral context. By pulling R-A-T-G-thirteen out of their bat archive and showing it was the closest known relative at ninety-six percent genome identity, this paper established the bat sarbecoviruses as the natural reservoir context for the new virus. "Of probable bat origin," as the title puts it.

The authors are explicit about what they haven't done. They note that association between the virus and the disease has not been formally proved by animal infection experiments — Koch's postulates are not fulfilled here. They note they don't yet know the transmission route among hosts, only that human-to-human transmission has clearly begun. They warn — correctly — that the virus should be monitored for evolution toward higher transmissibility or virulence. They raise but don't resolve the question of an intermediate host between bats and humans, which was presumed likely by analogy to SARS and MERS. R-A-T-G-thirteen is close, but a six-point-eight percent nucleotide divergence corresponds to decades of evolutionary separation, not a direct spillover. And they are careful about the spike's N-terminal insertions — they note the open question of whether those insertions give the new virus the ability to bind sialic acids on cell surfaces, as MERS does, but they do not claim to have tested it.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment, briefly. The A-C-E-two receptor result became the single most actionable finding of this paper — essentially every COVID vaccine and monoclonal antibody is aimed at the spike-A-C-E-two interaction, and that targeting dates to right here. R-A-T-G-thirteen became one of the most litigated sequences in the subsequent debate over the origin of the pandemic, with questions raised about whether other viruses in the Shi lab's archive were even closer relatives, how and when they had been sampled, and what to make of a six percent evolutionary gap between the closest known bat virus and the human outbreak. The specific question of an intermediate host, and the role of the seafood market, remained open for years and we'll come back to those arguments when we reach the papers that take them up directly.

That's episode two. The paper reference and link are in the show notes. Next time we move from genomics to structural biology — the first cryo-electron microscopy structure of the spike protein in its prefusion state, from the McLellan lab, published in Science in March 2020.
