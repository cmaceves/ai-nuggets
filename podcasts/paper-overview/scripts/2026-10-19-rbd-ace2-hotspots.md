## Script

Paper link: Shang et al., "Structural basis of receptor recognition by SARS-CoV-2." Nature, 30 March 2020. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2179-y

Welcome back to SARS-CoV-2: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-CoV-2 literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we were in Science with the Zhou lab's cryo-electron microscopy picture of the full-length A-C-E-two receptor with a piece of the virus docked on it. Today we move three and a half weeks forward in the calendar to a Nature paper that attacks the same virus-receptor interface from a different direction — not with cryo-electron microscopy but with X-ray crystallography — and asks a question the earlier structural papers hadn't yet asked explicitly: which specific amino-acid changes, residue by residue, actually make this new virus better at grabbing a human receptor than the original SARS coronavirus was.

Episode six. The paper is "Structural basis of receptor recognition by SARS-coronavirus-two," published in Nature on the thirtieth of March, 2020. The senior author is Fang Li at the University of Minnesota, and the first author is Jian Shang. This group has an important backstory for what you're about to hear: they have been working on how SARS-coronavirus engages A-C-E-two since 2005. The vocabulary for talking about the interface — the very idea of hotspots on the receptor — comes largely out of their earlier work. When the new coronavirus emerged, this was the lab that was best positioned to translate between the old virus and the new one.

Let's set the scene. Late March 2020. The World Health Organization has declared COVID-nineteen a pandemic nineteen days earlier. New York City is becoming the global epicenter — the Javits Center is being converted into a field hospital, Central Park is receiving tents. Italy's Lombardy region has had thousands of deaths; Spain is in strict lockdown. The United Kingdom has gone into its first lockdown four days before this paper publishes. The field has, in the span of less than two months, gone from "we have a new sequence" to "the virus binds through the spike receptor binding domain to A-C-E-two" to "here are near-atomic-resolution structures of the pieces." Today's paper closes the first chapter of the receptor-recognition story.

Here's the headline. The Fang Li group solved a crystal structure, at two-point-six-eight angstroms resolution, of the receptor binding domain of the new coronavirus bound to the enzyme head of human A-C-E-two. Three things fell out of that structure. First, they pointed to a specific small loop at the tip of the receptor binding domain that has reconfigured, relative to the original SARS coronavirus, into a more compact shape that pushes further into the receptor — and they identified the residue changes that enable it. Second, by comparing the new structure against their earlier structures of SARS-coronavirus with A-C-E-two, they could show that two long-known weak spots on the receptor — spots they had labelled hotspot-thirty-one and hotspot-three-five-three — are both held in productive, binding-competent shapes by two specific residues on the new virus's receptor binding domain. Third, they also tested a bat coronavirus called RaTG-thirteen, the closest known relative of the new virus, and showed that it, too, can use human A-C-E-two to enter cells. And then they ran the comparison in reverse: by introducing just three amino-acid substitutions that put the new virus's hotspot residues into the RaTG-thirteen receptor binding domain, RaTG-thirteen became much better at engaging human A-C-E-two.

Now the background, because the hotspot vocabulary is really the key to reading this paper, and the hotspot vocabulary is older than the pandemic. Back in 2005, the Li group had solved the crystal structure of SARS-coronavirus's receptor binding domain bound to human A-C-E-two. In that structure, two particular places on the A-C-E-two surface stood out as the critical determinants of whether a given coronavirus could bind the human receptor at all. The first is a lysine at position thirty-one of A-C-E-two, which in isolation would form a salt bridge with a glutamate at position thirty-five — a buried charge pair sitting in a hydrophobic environment. That is energetically unfavourable: buried charges want partners, and if the virus supplies a hydrogen-bond network that stabilises the pair, the interaction is strong; if not, the whole interface pays a penalty. They called this hotspot-thirty-one. The second is a lysine at position three-five-three of A-C-E-two, which likewise forms a salt bridge with an aspartate at position thirty-eight — again a buried charged pair asking for stabilisation. They called this hotspot-three-five-three. The remarkable thing about these two hotspots is that they are the critical nodes: whether a coronavirus can use human A-C-E-two is largely determined by what the virus's receptor binding domain presents at these two sites. If the virus stabilises the hotspots, it binds; if it clashes with them or leaves them unsatisfied, it does not. This framework, built up over fifteen years, is what the authors bring to the new structure.

Why do a crystal structure here, rather than cryo-electron microscopy. Both are complementary. Cryo-electron microscopy, as we saw in the last episode, lets you capture a large, fragile complex — like full-length A-C-E-two sitting in the membrane together with its chaperone — in roughly its native state. X-ray crystallography requires you to coax your protein into forming an ordered crystal, which is harder, but the payoff is extremely sharp maps of exactly how specific side chains are arranged relative to each other at an interface. For a question like "which single-residue changes between the two coronaviruses actually matter at the interface," crystallography is the sharper tool. You get down to the exact rotamer each amino acid is in, and the exact hydrogen bond lengths. The trade-off is that you cannot study the whole, membrane-embedded receptor the way cryo-electron microscopy can.

So what did they do, in detail. They expressed the receptor binding domain of the new coronavirus — the piece of the spike that actually contacts A-C-E-two — in insect cells. Insect cell expression is a standard trick for viral glycoproteins: it folds large eukaryotic proteins reliably, and it attaches sugars, though the sugars are a little simpler than human ones. In parallel, they expressed the peptidase domain of human A-C-E-two — the enzyme head, without the membrane anchor — the same construct the field had been using for structural work for years. They mixed the two proteins together, purified the complex by size, and set it down in crystallisation trials. One important wrinkle: the full receptor binding domain of the new virus did not readily give usable crystals, so they built a chimeric version. They took the structural scaffold of SARS-coronavirus's receptor binding domain — which they knew crystallised well, because they had done it before — and replaced only the business end of it, the small ridge of residues that actually contacts the receptor, with the equivalent piece from the new virus. The ridge is called the receptor binding motif, and in the paper and in this script, it is just the tip of the receptor binding domain, the part that touches A-C-E-two. The chimeric construct keeps the new virus's business end intact, and it crystallised. Crystallography people call this trick scaffold grafting, and it is a reasonable move when a target protein is uncooperative with crystal packing — the contacts between neighbouring molecules in the crystal are made by the better-behaved scaffold, while the interface of interest is left exactly as it is in the new virus.

A short primer on X-ray crystallography. You grow a crystal of your protein, which is a highly ordered three-dimensional lattice with your molecule as the repeating unit. You shoot the crystal with a beam of X-rays, usually at a synchrotron. The X-rays diffract off the electrons in the crystal, producing a pattern of spots on a detector. You collect many of these patterns from different crystal orientations, and from the intensities and positions of the spots you can — with enough work and the right software — compute the three-dimensional distribution of electron density inside the repeating unit of the crystal. If the resolution is good enough, you can trace the protein backbone and model side chains into that density. The resolution is set by how well the crystal is ordered and how far out from the centre of the diffraction pattern the usable spots extend. Two-point-six-eight angstroms, as here, is sharp enough to see side-chain orientation and short hydrogen bonds, which is exactly what you want for the atomic-level interface analysis they are about to do.

Now the results. The chimeric receptor binding domain of the new coronavirus sits down on A-C-E-two in overall the same place and the same orientation as the SARS-coronavirus receptor binding domain had, back in 2005. The ridge of the receptor binding domain lies across the top of the alpha-one helix of A-C-E-two. But look closely, and the ridge is reshaped. In SARS-coronavirus, the equivalent piece of the ridge contains the three amino acids proline-proline-alanine. In the new virus, that stretch has been replaced by a four-amino-acid motif, glycine-valine-glutamate-glycine, running from position four-eight-two to position four-eight-five. Glycines are the most flexible amino acid — the backbone can adopt angles no other side chain allows — and having two of them bracketing a short loop means the ridge can tuck down and curl into a more compact conformation. The result is that the ridge no longer bulges away from the receptor the way it did in the original SARS; it folds down and makes closer, better contacts along the length of the alpha-one helix. A single change downstream reinforces this effect: at position four-eight-six, where SARS-coronavirus had a leucine, the new virus has a phenylalanine. Phenylalanine has a bigger, flatter aromatic ring, and it inserts straight into a hydrophobic pocket on the receptor formed by methionine eighty-two, leucine seventy-nine, and tyrosine eighty-three. Hydrophobic packing of this kind — oily group into oily cavity — is quietly one of the strongest contributions to a protein-protein interface, and this one swap exploits the pocket far better than the original SARS did.

Then the hotspot story. Hotspot-thirty-one — that buried salt bridge between lysine thirty-one and glutamate thirty-five on A-C-E-two — is engaged, in the new structure, by a glutamine of the virus at position four-nine-three. The glutamine side chain is long enough to reach both the lysine and the glutamate, and it donates and accepts hydrogen bonds that lock the two A-C-E-two residues into a productive geometry. In SARS-coronavirus, the equivalent position carried a shorter asparagine, which provided weaker stabilisation. Hotspot-three-five-three — the lysine-three-five-three to aspartate-thirty-eight salt bridge — is engaged by an asparagine of the virus at position five-oh-one. Asparagine five-oh-one slides in and makes a hydrogen-bond network, both through its side chain and its main chain, that holds that salt bridge in place and packs well against the main chain of A-C-E-two. In SARS-coronavirus the equivalent position had a threonine, which did not provide the same stabilisation. In other words: the two hotspots the Li group had been staring at for fifteen years are, in the new virus, both stabilised a little better than they were in SARS-coronavirus — once by position four-nine-three, once by position five-oh-one. These are the two residues to remember from this paper.

To test the significance of these interactions functionally, the authors did two kinds of experiments. First, by surface plasmon resonance — a technique that measures how tightly two purified proteins stick to each other by watching binding and release in real time on a sensor surface — they showed that the new virus's receptor binding domain binds human A-C-E-two significantly more tightly than the SARS-coronavirus receptor binding domain does. Second, by protein pull-down — a simpler assay where you immobilise one protein and see which versions of the other protein co-purify with it — they tested specific mutants. If you take the compact ridge of the new virus and swap the glycine-valine-glutamate-glycine motif back to the SARS proline-proline-alanine, binding goes down. If you mutate position four-nine-three or position five-oh-one to residues that would destabilise the hotspots, binding goes down. The structural interpretation is backed up by biochemistry.

Now the RaTG-thirteen experiment, which is the one most people remember from this paper. RaTG-thirteen is the bat coronavirus discovered by the Wuhan Institute of Virology group, which we talked about in episode two, and which is the closest known relative of the new virus at the whole-genome level — about ninety-six percent identical. But the receptor binding domain, where the virus actually contacts a potential host receptor, is more divergent, and the field in early 2020 was asking whether RaTG-thirteen could directly use human A-C-E-two or whether it would need an intermediate adaptation. The Shang paper tests this directly. They expressed the RaTG-thirteen receptor binding domain, mixed it with human A-C-E-two, and did the same binding assays. The answer: yes, RaTG-thirteen binds human A-C-E-two, though substantially less well than the new coronavirus does. The weaker binding is traceable to specific residues at the hotspot positions. RaTG-thirteen has a leucine at the position where the new virus has phenylalanine four-eight-six — so it does not fill that hydrophobic pocket as well. It has a tyrosine at position four-nine-three instead of a glutamine — so it stabilises hotspot-thirty-one less well. And it has an aspartate at position five-oh-one instead of an asparagine — so hotspot-three-five-three is engaged differently. Then they ran the reverse experiment. They introduced those three substitutions — leucine-to-phenylalanine at four-eight-six, tyrosine-to-glutamine at four-nine-three, and aspartate-to-asparagine at five-oh-one — into the RaTG-thirteen receptor binding domain, converting those three positions to the new virus's identities. Human A-C-E-two binding went up substantially. In other words, you can walk the bat sequence toward the pandemic sequence, residue by residue, and watch the human-receptor affinity climb. This does not prove that the new virus's spike arose directly from RaTG-thirteen; the two viruses are too distant for that. But it does show that a very small number of mutations at the hotspot-interacting positions can convert a bat spike into one that binds human A-C-E-two efficiently.

Now the discussion. The paper is a residue-by-residue atlas of why this virus is good at grabbing a human receptor, delivered in the vocabulary that this group had built for the field over fifteen years of pre-pandemic work. For therapy, it names specific positions: block the hotspot-engaging residues on the virus, or shield the hotspots on the receptor, and you blunt entry. For evolution and host range, it says something subtle. On the one hand, the new virus's recipe for high human-A-C-E-two affinity is not identical to SARS-coronavirus's — it has rebuilt the interface, with changes at the ridge, at hotspot-thirty-one, and at hotspot-three-five-three, each contributing a little. That is a lot of coherent improvements piled up at the same interface. On the other hand, the RaTG-thirteen result shows that even the bat sequence has non-trivial human-A-C-E-two affinity to begin with, and that only a handful of changes are needed to improve it to the new virus's level. That observation lands in the middle of the origin discussion we were in two episodes ago, with the proximal-origin paper: it means a bat sarbecovirus of the RaTG-thirteen type does not have to pass through a particular intermediate host to acquire the ability to bind the human receptor, and it also means the raw material for a cross-species jump is probably more widespread in the bat reservoir than people had assumed before 2020.

What they were honest about. The structure is of a chimeric construct — the new virus's receptor binding motif grafted onto the SARS-coronavirus core. The grafting was done in a region far from the interface they were analysing, and the authors argue, carefully, that the business end's local structure should be unaffected. But it is still a chimera, not the native domain. The affinity measurements by surface plasmon resonance are done with the receptor binding domain alone, not with the whole trimeric spike in the context of a virion, and other factors — spike oligomerisation, membrane anchoring, host protease cleavage — modulate how receptor binding translates into actual infection. And binding to a receptor in a cell-free assay is necessary but not sufficient for infection; the virus also needs to be processed, fused, and the viral RNA released into the cytoplasm, each of which can be rate-limiting in a different host.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment. The two hotspot-interacting residues identified in this paper — glutamine four-nine-three and asparagine five-oh-one — would both become famous names over the next two years. Asparagine five-oh-one is where the asparagine-to-tyrosine substitution, N-five-oh-one-Y, lands — a change shared across the Alpha, Beta, Gamma and Omicron variants, and which the hotspot analysis in this paper correctly frames as a change that strengthens hotspot-three-five-three engagement. Glutamine four-nine-three is where the glutamine-to-arginine substitution, Q-four-nine-three-R, first appears in Omicron, and which, on later analysis, further stabilises hotspot-thirty-one. In both cases, the mutational re-visits of these positions can be read directly off the hotspot framework that this paper, and the Li group's earlier SARS-coronavirus work, laid down.
