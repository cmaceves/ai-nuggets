## Script

Welcome back to SARS-coronavirus-two: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-coronavirus-two literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we spent the episode on the Pango nomenclature proposal — the vocabulary the field would use to name and talk about lineages for the next several years. Today we step back about six weeks, from mid-July to late May 2020, and walk into one of the most ferocious outbreaks the virus had produced anywhere in the world. The paper is eighty-four patients sequenced at a single New York City hospital system during the first eighteen days of the local epidemic, and it is the first genomic reconstruction of how the virus actually arrived in the city.

Episode twelve. The paper is "Introductions and early spread of SARS-coronavirus-two in the New York City area," published in Science on the twenty-ninth of May, 2020. The first author is Ana Silvia Gonzalez-Reiche at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. The senior author is Harm van Bakel, also at Mount Sinai. The author list runs more than thirty people, which gives you a sense of what sequencing a clinical cohort inside a working hospital in a disaster zone actually takes — clinicians collecting swabs, a surveillance program accessioning them, laboratory staff extracting R-N-A and running next-generation sequencing on two different platforms, bioinformaticians assembling genomes, and phylogeneticists building trees against a global background. Several names on the list recur in later work: Florian Krammer and Adolfo García-Sastre, who ran serology and virology efforts at Mount Sinai throughout the pandemic; Viviana Simon and Emilia Mia Sordillo, who were among the senior authors and would lead Mount Sinai's genomic surveillance for years afterward. Marta Luksza at Mount Sinai did much of the phylogenetic analysis — she is one of the people who built the computational infrastructure that the field used to make sense of trees like this one.

Let me set the scene. By the end of May 2020, when the paper came out, New York state had reported more than three hundred and twelve thousand confirmed COVID-nineteen cases, and over half of them — about a hundred and seventy-two thousand — were in New York City. There had already been more than thirteen thousand three hundred deaths in the metropolitan area. The city was in lockdown. Hospitals were running out of ventilators. Refrigerated trucks were sitting outside emergency rooms. By any measure, New York was the first American epicenter of the pandemic, and it had happened fast — the first confirmed case in New York state was identified on the twenty-ninth of February. Three weeks later the hospitals were overwhelmed. The scientific question underneath the human catastrophe was: where did it actually come from? The federal government's travel-restriction timeline was built around China first, then Iran, then continental Europe on the thirteenth of March, then the United Kingdom and Ireland on the sixteenth. Those restrictions were visible, loud, and politically central. Had they worked? And if not, which routes had the virus actually taken into the city?

The headline. The group sequenced ninety SARS-coronavirus-two genomes from eighty-four patients seen at the Mount Sinai Health System between the twenty-ninth of February and the eighteenth of March — the first eighteen days of the New York outbreak. When they placed those eighty-four distinct patient isolates on a phylogenetic tree together with roughly twenty-three hundred genomes available worldwide at the time, the New York sequences fell throughout the tree, not into a single clade — direct evidence of multiple independent introductions into the city. The great majority — eighty-seven percent of the inferred-origin isolates — fell into a clade dominated by European sequences, suggesting that most of the New York virus had arrived from Europe rather than from Asia. A smaller fraction were clearly of domestic United States origin, including a cluster whose closest relatives were on the West Coast. And buried inside the European-origin clade, the authors identified two small local transmission clusters that were almost exclusively made up of New York sequences — direct genomic evidence of community spread already happening in the first two weeks of March. The take, in a sentence: the New York outbreak was not seeded from China, and it was not seeded from a single source — it was dozens of unrelated introductions, mostly from Europe, mostly before any travel restriction on Europe existed, and community spread was underway before most of these cases were even detected.

Now the background, because the real teachable content here is how you reconstruct the origin of an outbreak from a few dozen viral genomes, and that method matters more than any one city's story. Phylogenetic epidemiology — the field this paper sits in — uses the fact that R-N-A viruses mutate as they replicate. Each round of replication introduces a small number of substitutions into the viral genome, essentially at random, and those substitutions are inherited by descendant viruses. SARS-coronavirus-two mutates slowly for an R-N-A virus, roughly two substitutions per month across its roughly thirty-kilobase genome, but it does mutate. So if two patient samples carry the same unusual substitution, it is evidence their viruses share a recent common ancestor. If a well-supported group of samples all share several substitutions, that's a clade — a transmission chain or a set of related chains. A tree is just the structure that encodes all those shared and unique mutations as ancestor-to-descendant relationships. Overlay geography on top: if all the samples in one clade come from one place, the outbreak there probably came from a single introduction. If samples from one place are scattered across the whole tree, that place was seeded many times from many sources.

The paper is explicit about the vocabulary it uses, and this matters for the historical arc of the show. In May 2020 the Pango system we discussed last episode had not yet been proposed — the paper we are discussing today was being written while Rambaut's group was still drafting the Pango proposal. So Gonzalez-Reiche and colleagues use the clade naming scheme from Nextstrain, which was the dominant pre-Pango vocabulary. Nextstrain's clades, at the time, were named with letter-and-number labels like A1a, A2a, B, B1, and B4. The letters here are not Pango lineages — they are Nextstrain clades, defined by shared sets of amino acid and nucleotide substitutions, assigned on the basis of statistical support from both maximum-likelihood and Bayesian tree reconstruction. The two systems overlap in some places and diverge in others; the field would, over the next year, converge largely onto Pango, but at the moment this paper was written, Nextstrain clades were the common currency.

Here is how they did the work. Mount Sinai had something called the Pathogen Surveillance Program — an in-house infrastructure for near-real-time genomic analysis of pathogens showing up in the hospital's clinical samples. When COVID started, they repointed that infrastructure at SARS-coronavirus-two. They took residual nasopharyngeal swabs from patients who had tested positive by P-C-R, extracted R-N-A, and sequenced the viral genomes on two platforms in parallel — Illumina for short-read coverage and Pacific Biosciences for longer reads. Of the samples that passed quality control, they generated seventy-two complete genomes and eighteen partial but better-than-ninety-five-percent genomes, from eighty-four distinct patients. Those patients were drawn from twenty-one neighborhoods across four of the five New York City boroughs — Manhattan, the Bronx, Queens, and Brooklyn — plus two towns in Westchester County. Demographically it is roughly representative of who was ending up in a Mount Sinai bed that month: about half men, half women; ages spread from twenty into the eighties, with the heaviest representation in the forty-five-to-seventy-nine range. Eighty percent of the patients with available visit information were admitted to the hospital. These are, by selection, the sicker end of the spectrum of early New York cases.

The phylogenetic analysis is the heart of the paper. They aligned the eighty-four Mount Sinai genomes with roughly twenty-three hundred SARS-coronavirus-two sequences deposited in GISAID — the global sharing database — up to the first of April, 2020. Then they built a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree. Maximum likelihood, briefly: you specify a model of how nucleotides substitute over time, and you find the tree topology that makes the observed sequence data most probable under that model. Then they ran a Bayesian time tree under a strict molecular clock — meaning the virus is assumed to accumulate mutations at a fixed rate per unit time, in this case eight-tenths of a substitution per thousand base pairs per year — which lets you turn branch lengths into estimated calendar time, so you can date the common ancestors in the tree. The method here is standard genomic epidemiology — the same conceptual toolkit the Grubaugh group used on Connecticut in the paper we did a couple of episodes ago, run against a much larger dataset. Two of the New York cases had documented international travel history and were excluded from the inference, because the point of the exercise was to understand the un-traced community cases, not the ones already known to be imported.

For each New York isolate, the authors assigned it to one of the main Nextstrain clades based on where it sat on the tree. They then classified each clade placement as evidence of a particular kind of event — a direct introduction with onward spread, a possible period of untraced global transmission, a possible domestic versus international origin, and so on — and summarized that in a table.

Now the results, which are the most striking part of the paper. First and most important: the New York sequences fall throughout the global tree. They are not concentrated in one clade. If a single introduction had seeded the entire New York outbreak, you would expect all the samples to cluster together, with each New York sample being either the ancestor or a descendant of some common New York root. That is not what the data showed. New York samples appear in at least four or five different Nextstrain clades, and within some clades they are scattered across multiple sub-branches. This is phylogenetically what multiple independent introductions look like.

Second: the dominant signal is European. Of the inferred-origin New York isolates, about eighty-seven percent fell into clade A2a. Clade A2a, in the April 2020 global dataset, was dominated by sequences from European countries — seventy-two percent of all A2a sequences in GISAID at the time were from Europe, with the earliest samples coming from Italy, Finland, Spain, France, and the United Kingdom in late February. A smaller fraction of New York isolates — about six percent — fell into clade A1a, which was also overwhelmingly European in origin, more than eighty percent. The clear implication is that most of New York's virus had traveled from Europe. The authors date the period of untraced global transmission that fed clade A2a to roughly late January through mid-February — a window of several weeks in which the virus was circulating widely in Europe before anyone was testing for it comprehensively.

Third: there is a domestic signal too, but it is smaller. A handful of New York isolates fell into clades B, B1, and B4. Clade B1 is the lineage that had seeded the major outbreak in Washington state, which Trevor Bedford's group at Fred Hutchinson had reconstructed in parallel work earlier in the spring. Two of the New York introductions appear to have come from the Pacific Northwest by way of this domestic clade. One isolate in clade B4 traces back to an inferred period of transmission in an unknown location during February, with its closest known relatives in Australia and China. So there is a measurable but minority contribution from within the United States.

Fourth, and this is subtle: within the dominant European-origin A2a clade, the authors looked for sub-clades that were almost exclusively made up of New York sequences, because those would be the smoking gun of local community transmission. They found two of them. The larger of the two included seventeen New York isolates — twenty-three percent of the A2a sub-dataset and twenty percent of the paper's total sequences — all distinguished by a specific amino acid substitution, A-eighteen-forty-four-V in the ORF-one-b gene, meaning the eighteen-hundred-forty-fourth amino acid in that gene had changed from alanine to valine. The cases carrying this signature were spread across five New York counties, including one patient from New Rochelle in Westchester, which was the location of the first documented cluster of community-acquired infections in New York state back on the third of March. The paper is explicit that it cannot say for sure whether this cluster represents the New Rochelle outbreak itself or a related chain that spread elsewhere in parallel — but the genomic fingerprint is consistent with sustained local transmission from a single introduction. The second cluster was smaller — four Manhattan cases and one Queens case, in three different Manhattan neighborhoods — which again looked like local spread rather than independent importations.

Fifth, the geography. When they plotted the sequenced cases onto a map of the city, the clades were distributed across all four boroughs and twenty-one neighborhoods. There was no single ground zero. The city was being seeded essentially everywhere at once. This is consistent with the picture of dozens of unrelated introductions, each initiating a local chain that was already spreading by the time testing caught up.

The discussion. The authors are careful about what they are and are not claiming. They are not claiming the virus had been circulating in New York since January; they are claiming that by late February and early March the city was being seeded from Europe and, to a smaller extent, from domestic sources, and that community transmission was already underway in those first weeks. They are also pointing out something important about public-health policy: the two documented travel-history cases that opened the Mount Sinai cohort — the ones with Middle East exposure and Europe exposure — did not seed the larger community clusters. Those index cases were caught, quarantined, and hospitalized, and the chains ran into dead ends. The community clusters came from different introductions, undetected, that were spreading before anyone was looking.

The limitations are real and the authors flag them. The global dataset in early April 2020 was small — about twenty-three hundred sequences from a pandemic that already had more than a million reported cases. Sequencing effort was heavily uneven by country; some countries were producing dozens of genomes a week, others essentially none. That unevenness means the geographic attribution of a clade is only as good as the sampling of that geography. If France was sequencing heavily and Spain was not, European sequences in the tree would look French when the underlying virus might have been circulating equally in Spain. The authors are explicit that the dated most-recent-common-ancestor estimates come with uncertainty — different molecular clock models and different Bayesian priors give different numbers, and the paper reports a range. And they could not match viral sequence back to detailed travel-history or contact data for most of the patients, so the attribution of periods of untracked transmission to specific regions is inference, not direct observation.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment. Two other groups published in parallel on the same question — Trevor Bedford and colleagues on Washington state, which seeded the domestic signal here; and a separate group at NYU Grossman led by Adriana Heguy, who sequenced a different New York hospital cohort over an overlapping window and reached broadly compatible conclusions, which this paper cites. The convergent finding across all of them — that the New York outbreak was predominantly European-origin and seeded by many independent introductions before travel from Europe was restricted — became one of the most durable results from the first wave of United States genomic surveillance. It is also one of the clearest early demonstrations of why travel restrictions targeted at single countries fail against a pathogen that has already spread widely elsewhere: by the time the border was closed to one source, the virus was arriving from several others. The methodological point — that a few dozen genomes from one hospital, placed against a global background, could resolve the origins of a city-sized outbreak — is one that the field spent the next several years scaling up to cover entire countries continuously.
