## Script

Paper link: Hoffmann et al., "SARS-CoV-2 Cell Entry Depends on ACE2 and TMPRSS2 and Is Blocked by a Clinically Proven Protease Inhibitor." Cell, 5 March 2020. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2020.02.052

Welcome back to SARS-CoV-2: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-CoV-2 literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we were in Nature with the Fang Li lab's crystal structure of the receptor binding domain docked onto A-C-E-two, cataloguing residue by residue why this new virus is good at grabbing the human receptor. Today we zoom out from the virus-receptor interface itself and ask a different question — one the entry story in late February 2020 had not yet nailed down. Even if the spike grabs A-C-E-two, that is not enough to get the virus inside a cell. Something has to cleave the spike at the right moment, to allow the fusion machinery to spring forward. Which host cell protease does that cleaving, and can we block it with a drug we already have.

Episode seven. The paper is "SARS-coronavirus-two cell entry depends on A-C-E-two and T-M-P-R-S-S-two and is blocked by a clinically proven protease inhibitor," published in Cell on the fifth of March, 2020. The first author is Markus Hoffmann and the senior author is Stefan Pöhlmann at the German Primate Center in Göttingen, with Christian Drosten at the Charité in Berlin as a co-senior author. This is the lab that spent most of the previous decade dissecting how SARS-coronavirus and MERS-coronavirus get into cells — the lineage of papers that identified T-M-P-R-S-S-two as the protease that primes those viruses in the lung. When the new coronavirus emerged, this group had both the reagents and the mental model ready.

Let me set the scene. Early March 2020. The paper is accepted on the twenty-seventh of February and published online on the fifth of March, six days before the World Health Organization declares the pandemic. Globally there are something like a hundred thousand confirmed cases, concentrated in China but climbing fast in Italy, Iran and South Korea. The United States is beginning to realise what has been happening unobserved — the Washington state nursing-home cluster is being traced, Seattle hospitals are reporting severe pneumonia cases, and the first community transmission has been formally acknowledged. There are no vaccines, no monoclonal antibodies, no approved antivirals. There is one and only one approach to therapy that could, in principle, be deployed in weeks rather than months: repurpose a drug that is already approved for something else. That premise is baked into the title of this paper.

Here is the headline. The Hoffmann and Pöhlmann labs show four things, each with implications for public health in that moment. First, SARS-coronavirus-two uses the same cell-surface receptor that SARS-coronavirus used in 2003 — angiotensin-converting enzyme two, or A-C-E-two. Second, the spike of the new virus, like the SARS spike, needs to be cut, or primed, by a human protease at the cell surface — specifically the transmembrane serine protease T-M-P-R-S-S-two. Third, an existing serine protease inhibitor called camostat mesylate, which is already approved in Japan for pancreatitis, blocks T-M-P-R-S-S-two and in turn blocks the new virus from infecting human lung cells and primary airway epithelial cells. Fourth, antibodies from patients who recovered from SARS in 2003 can partially cross-neutralise the new virus. The first three findings between them hand the field both a conceptual model of entry and a specific drug candidate for immediate off-label use.

Now the background, because to see what is new in this paper you have to see what the Pöhlmann and Drosten groups had already worked out for the original SARS virus. Coronavirus entry has two steps. First, the spike glycoprotein — which decorates the surface of the virion in a trimeric crown — binds a receptor on the host cell. The spike is organised into two subunits called S-one and S-two. S-one carries the receptor binding domain and does the gripping; S-two is the fusion machinery that drives the viral membrane and the host membrane to merge. Second, before the fusion machinery can spring, the spike has to be cleaved — in two places, actually. Once at the junction between S-one and S-two, called the S-one-S-two cleavage site, which separates the receptor binding half from the fusion half, and once a little further down inside S-two, at the S-two-prime site, which releases a short internal fusion peptide that then inserts into the host membrane. Only after these cuts can S-two refold into its post-fusion conformation and drag the two membranes together. Where the cuts happen depends on which proteases the virus can recruit. There are, broadly, two options. One is a surface serine protease — in the respiratory epithelium, that is T-M-P-R-S-S-two, which sits on the plasma membrane of airway cells. The other is a pair of endosomal cysteine proteases called cathepsin B and cathepsin L, which live inside the acidic compartments the cell uses for internalising receptors. SARS-coronavirus had been shown by this same group and others to be able to use either route in cell culture, but in a living animal only the T-M-P-R-S-S-two route actually mattered for disease — knockout mice lacking T-M-P-R-S-S-two were largely protected from SARS, while loss of cathepsin activity did not spare them. The gap this new paper sets out to fill is whether the same model holds for the brand-new coronavirus. Receptor usage first; then which protease; then whether that protease can be drugged.

Let's talk methodology. The core assay in this paper is a V-S-V pseudotype assay, so it's worth explaining what that is because almost every entry result in the paper rides on it. Vesicular stomatitis virus — V-S-V — is a well-behaved, well-characterised rhabdovirus. Working with it is routine in a basic biosafety containment lab. You take a crippled V-S-V vector in which the gene for its own surface glycoprotein has been replaced with a reporter gene — in this case firefly luciferase, so you can read out successful infection as a glow in the lysate. In its place you supply the coronavirus spike in trans, from a separate expression plasmid in the producer cell. The virion particles that come out of those producer cells carry the coronavirus spike on their surface, but inside they carry a V-S-V genome that cannot propagate past one round. These are called pseudotyped particles, or pseudotypes. The practical point of pseudotypes is this: entry — binding to the receptor, protease priming, membrane fusion — is governed entirely by the surface glycoprotein, which is the coronavirus spike. Everything downstream, from genome replication onwards, is governed by V-S-V. So you can quantitatively measure how well the coronavirus spike gets into a cell by counting luciferase in a plate reader, without ever handling live SARS-coronavirus-two in a biosafety-level-three facility. For a result in early 2020, when nearly nobody had patient isolates in hand, this is exactly the right tool. The limitation is that pseudotypes do not fully recapitulate real virus infection — spike density on the particle is different, there is no second cycle of replication, and some host factors that would matter in a real infection are not probed. So wherever the pseudotype pointed at an important result, the authors backed it up with the authentic virus — a patient isolate from Munich — in appropriate containment.

With that assay in hand, here is what they did. First, they confirmed that the spike of the new virus is actually proteolytically processed in human cells. They expressed a tagged version of the spike in human kidney cells and ran a Western blot. They saw a band at the size of the uncut spike, and also a band at the size of the S-two fragment — meaning the S-one-S-two cut had happened. That cut did not happen efficiently for the old SARS spike in the same cells. The explanation sits right in the sequence: at the S-one-S-two junction, the new virus has a stretch of several arginine residues — a so-called multibasic cleavage site — that is not present in SARS-coronavirus. Multibasic sites are the preferred substrates of a different cellular protease, called furin, which lives in the trans-Golgi network of the producer cell and cuts proteins as they are being secreted. The practical consequence is that the spike comes out already pre-cut, with S-one and S-two held together only by non-covalent contacts, which is thought to accelerate the entry steps that follow. Keep that detail in mind — it comes back in the discussion.

Second, receptor usage. They took their new spike pseudotypes and tested which cell lines they could infect, side by side with pseudotypes carrying the old SARS spike. The two covered an essentially identical panel of cell lines across humans, bats, pigs, cows, dogs and non-human primates, which was already a strong hint that they share a receptor. To nail it down, they turned to a cell line called B-H-K-twenty-one — baby hamster kidney cells — which is not naturally susceptible. By transfecting it with a plasmid encoding human A-C-E-two, they rendered it susceptible to both new and old SARS pseudotypes. Transfecting it with human D-P-P-four, which is the MERS receptor, or with human A-P-N, which is the receptor for an unrelated common-cold coronavirus, did not. An antibody raised against human A-C-E-two blocked pseudotype entry at concentrations where a control antibody did not. And finally, they infected the A-C-E-two-expressing cells with authentic SARS-coronavirus-two from the Munich patient isolate and showed by quantitative P-C-R that viral R-N-A multiplied in those cells but not in the parental B-H-K-twenty-one without A-C-E-two. Taken together: A-C-E-two is sufficient to turn a non-susceptible cell into a susceptible one. The receptor is settled, and it is the SARS-coronavirus receptor.

Third, the protease experiment, which is the heart of the paper. They first asked whether the endosomal cathepsin route is used by the new virus. They treated cells with ammonium chloride, which raises the pH inside endosomes and so blocks the cathepsin proteases from working. In human kidney cells, which have little surface T-M-P-R-S-S-two, ammonium chloride strongly suppressed entry of both the new and the old SARS pseudotypes. So in a cell forced to rely on the endosomal route, the new virus can use the cathepsin route. They confirmed this with a direct cathepsin inhibitor, E-sixty-four-d, which also blocked entry into those cells. Then they asked about T-M-P-R-S-S-two. They used camostat mesylate — a small-molecule serine protease inhibitor that is approved in Japan for an unrelated indication and that targets T-M-P-R-S-S-two in cell culture at reasonable concentrations. On its own, camostat only partially blocked entry into cells that have both routes available. But combine camostat with E-sixty-four-d, and entry was essentially abolished. In cells like human kidney cells that lack T-M-P-R-S-S-two, camostat does nothing. If you force T-M-P-R-S-S-two expression in those cells by transfecting it, you can rescue entry even when cathepsin is blocked. In other words: both routes exist, both can prime the spike, and the question is which one dominates in a cell that gets to choose.

Fourth, which route matters in the lung. Camostat on its own, in a human lung cell line called Calu-three, substantially reduced entry by the new spike. Not just the pseudotype — camostat also reduced infection by authentic SARS-coronavirus-two, measured by quantitative P-C-R on the supernatant. And most compellingly, they moved from a cell line to primary human airway epithelial cells, cultured directly from bronchial tissue from pulmonary resection patients. Camostat blocked entry of the new virus's spike into those primary cells as well. The implication: in cells that resemble the real target tissue in the human airway, T-M-P-R-S-S-two is the operative protease, and inhibiting it inhibits the virus. The dual pathway seen in simple cell lines appears to collapse, in physiologically relevant cells, to one dominant path.

The fifth and final experiment is about antibody cross-reactivity. Sera from three patients who had recovered from SARS back in 2003, plus sera from rabbits immunised with the S-one subunit of the SARS spike, could neutralise the new coronavirus's spike pseudotype. The cross-neutralising activity was weaker than the activity against the old SARS spike itself — which is to be expected, given that the two spikes are only about seventy-six percent identical — but it was unambiguously there. The take-home at the time: whatever is going to be built as a vaccine against the new virus, it will not be building from zero. The immune responses that SARS vaccine candidates had generated in the 2000s and 2010s had been largely parked after the first SARS outbreak receded, but they would at least cross-react to a meaningful degree.

Now the discussion, and the significance. On the entry pathway side, the paper hands the field a model. The new virus, like SARS-coronavirus before it, docks on A-C-E-two, gets primed by surface T-M-P-R-S-S-two in the airway, and fuses at the plasma membrane. In cell culture it can also take the back door through the endosome and be primed by cathepsins, but the lung-relevant route is T-M-P-R-S-S-two. This model is important not just for drug development but for making sense of the tissue tropism of the virus. A-C-E-two is expressed on many cell types outside the lung — the gut, the kidney, the heart. T-M-P-R-S-S-two is also widely expressed. Where the two coincide, the virus has an opening.

On the therapeutic side, the implication is unusually sharp. Camostat mesylate is not a new molecule. It is an established oral drug with decades of safety data in Japanese patients, used for chronic pancreatitis and reflux. Here it blocks the key host-protease step in the entry of a pandemic virus. The authors are careful not to oversell — they do not claim to have shown that camostat will work in a patient — but the inference is laid out plainly: this compound, or newer serine protease inhibitors with greater potency, should be considered for off-label use and formal evaluation.

What they are honest about. The pseudotype system is a model system: it measures a single round of entry and does not touch replication, genome packaging or spread. Cell lines, even lung cell lines, are not airway tissue. The multibasic cleavage site at S-one-S-two is new to this virus relative to the sampled bat relatives, and the paper flags that it may matter for entry efficiency and zoonotic potential, but it does not yet dissect how. They do not claim to measure spike-A-C-E-two affinity in physical units — they leave that to the structural papers we have just come through. And they are explicit that camostat only partially blocks infection of the Calu-three lung cell line by authentic virus, consistent with residual spike priming by cathepsin even in that context — meaning a real therapy may need to hit both routes together.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment. This paper went on to be one of the most-cited of the pandemic. The entry model it specifies — A-C-E-two engagement, T-M-P-R-S-S-two priming, plasma-membrane fusion — became the textbook description, and the finding immediately spawned clinical trials of camostat mesylate for COVID-nineteen. Those trials, which read out over the following two years, were mixed and ultimately disappointing — camostat did not translate into a clear clinical benefit at the doses tested, for reasons the field is still debating, probably including pharmacokinetics in the human airway and the virus's ability to lean more heavily on the endosomal cathepsin route than the Calu-three experiments had suggested. The multibasic cleavage site that the paper notices in passing turned out to be more central than the authors could have known in early March 2020 — it became a focal point for understanding why the new virus is more transmissible than SARS was and, once the Omicron variant arrived in late 2021, the balance between the T-M-P-R-S-S-two and the endosomal routes shifted again, with later variants showing a greater reliance on the endosomal pathway. None of that was knowable in March 2020. What was knowable, and what this paper locked down, was the model.
