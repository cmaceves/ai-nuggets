## Script

Paper link: Yan et al., "Structural basis for the recognition of SARS-CoV-2 by full-length human ACE2." Science, 4 March 2020. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abb2762

Welcome back to SARS-CoV-2: A History Run-Through. I'm Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-CoV-2 literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports forward. Last time we were in Nature Medicine with the proximal-origin perspective — the piece arguing from genomic data that the new virus wasn't engineered. Today we go back one step chronologically to a structural paper that answers the companion question to the one Wrapp and McLellan asked a couple of weeks earlier: if the spike is the key, this paper is the structural picture of the lock.

Episode five. The paper is "Structural basis for the recognition of SARS-coronavirus-two by full-length human A-C-E-two," published in Science on the fourth of March, 2020. The senior author is Qiang Zhou at Westlake University in Hangzhou. The first author is Renhong Yan. This group had made their name on membrane protein cryo-electron microscopy — in particular on amino-acid transporters — and that prior expertise turns out to be exactly what unlocks the structure here.

Let's set the scene. Early March 2020. The W-H-O has not yet declared a pandemic — that comes on March eleventh, a week after this paper publishes. Italy is in the middle of its first wave; Iran is seeing explosive spread; the first community-transmission cases in the United States and the United Kingdom are being reported. The Wrapp lab's cryo-electron microscopy structure of the spike ectodomain in the prefusion conformation, which we covered in the last episode, had come out two weeks earlier. The same group that produced today's paper had also just reported, in the same month, a structure of the isolated receptor binding domain of SARS-coronavirus-two bound to the isolated peptidase domain of A-C-E-two — the fragment-on-fragment view. What was still missing was the full picture: what does the whole A-C-E-two protein look like, in a cell membrane, with everything attached, when it is being gripped by the virus. That is what this paper delivers.

Here's the headline. The Zhou lab solved the first cryo-electron microscopy structure of full-length human A-C-E-two, at an overall resolution of two-point-nine angstroms, and then solved a second structure of the SARS-coronavirus-two receptor binding domain bound to that same full-length A-C-E-two, also at two-point-nine angstroms. To get there they had to co-express A-C-E-two with another membrane protein, an amino acid transporter called B-zero-A-T-one, because A-C-E-two by itself has only a single transmembrane helix and is too floppy for high-resolution reconstruction. Three things fell out of the structure. First, full-length A-C-E-two is an obligate dimer — two copies locked together through a previously-unseen neck domain, and the dimer can open and close like a pair of jaws. Second, the structure captures two spike receptor binding domains docked onto one A-C-E-two dimer, which implies that two spike trimers can engage one receptor dimer at the same time. And third, by comparing the new virus's receptor binding domain contacts against the equivalent contacts for SARS-coronavirus, they could point to specific amino-acid changes that likely strengthen the grip on human A-C-E-two, and others that may weaken it.

Now the background, because the methodology here really only makes sense in the context of what A-C-E-two actually is and why it had resisted structural work for so long. A-C-E-two stands for angiotensin-converting enzyme two. It is a membrane-anchored enzyme whose day job has nothing to do with viruses: it clips a hormone called angiotensin, trimming angiotensin-one into a shorter peptide and converting angiotensin-two into another shorter peptide, and those products help regulate blood pressure, fluid balance, and inflammatory tone. It is expressed in the lung, the heart, the kidneys, and the intestine. Structurally, each A-C-E-two molecule has an extracellular enzyme head — called the peptidase domain, or P-D — tethered to the membrane by a short piece called the collectrin-like domain, which ends in a single transmembrane helix and a short tail inside the cell. For nearly twenty years, every published A-C-E-two structure had been of the extracellular enzyme head alone. Nobody had seen the full-length molecule, with its membrane anchor, in a near-native state. The reason is that single-pass membrane proteins — proteins that cross the membrane just once — are notoriously hard to image at high resolution by cryo-electron microscopy. There isn't enough mass down in the membrane region to pin down the molecule's orientation during image processing, and the whole particle wobbles around that single thin anchor. That is the technical wall this paper walks up to.

The way around that wall is the clever part. The Zhou group had previously shown, in work on amino acid transport, that A-C-E-two does not only serve as an angiotensin-chopping enzyme — it also acts as a chaperone for a second membrane protein, an amino acid transporter called B-zero-A-T-one, encoded by the gene S-L-C-six-A-nineteen. B-zero-A-T-one brings neutral amino acids into intestinal cells, and it needs A-C-E-two in order to get to the cell surface in the first place. Mechanically, B-zero-A-T-one has many transmembrane helices — it is a large, bulky integral membrane protein — and A-C-E-two sits alongside it, almost like a passenger clinging to a boulder. If you co-express A-C-E-two with B-zero-A-T-one and purify them together as a complex, suddenly you have a much larger assembly, with much more mass in and around the membrane, that is far better suited to cryo-electron microscopy. The authors had used an analogous trick before, on a different pair — a small amino-acid transporter called L-A-T-one needing a chaperone called four-F-two-h-c — and they borrowed that playbook here. Importantly, B-zero-A-T-one is not involved in viral biology at all. It is being used as a structural scaffold — it stabilises A-C-E-two for the microscope. The biology of interest is still A-C-E-two.

So what did they do, in detail. They cloned full-length human A-C-E-two and full-length human B-zero-A-T-one into expression vectors, added small protein tags to each so they could purify them, and co-expressed both proteins in human embryonic kidney cells grown in suspension — a standard eukaryotic expression system that gets you properly-folded human membrane proteins with human-style glycosylation. They solubilised the membrane complex using mild detergent, pulled it through two rounds of affinity chromatography — one tag at a time — and then ran size-exclusion chromatography, which separates particles by physical size. The complex came off as a single sharp peak, which means it was clean and uniform in size — a necessary condition for good cryo-electron microscopy. They vitrified this sample onto microscope grids, collected images, and reconstructed a three-dimensional map. From over four hundred thousand particle images they got a two-point-nine-angstrom reconstruction of the A-C-E-two–B-zero-A-T-one complex. That is high enough resolution to assign amino-acid side chains across the whole structure.

A short primer on cryo-electron microscopy in case you joined the show in the last episode. You flash-freeze a thin layer of your purified protein into a sheet of amorphous ice, so the molecules are preserved in their native solution shapes in random orientations. You then image them in the electron microscope with a very low electron dose, which gives you an enormous library of extremely noisy two-dimensional projections of individual molecules. Software then sorts those projections by orientation, aligns them, and averages hundreds of thousands of them to reconstruct a three-dimensional density map. If the resolution is good enough — two-point-nine angstroms, as here — you can trace the backbone and model side chains.

Then they did it again with the virus added. They bought a purified version of the SARS-coronavirus-two receptor binding domain tagged with a mouse antibody fragment to help it behave on the grid, mixed it with their already-purified A-C-E-two–B-zero-A-T-one complex at roughly a one-to-one ratio, and prepared fresh cryo-electron microscopy grids of that ternary complex. From roughly half a million particle images they built a second reconstruction, also at two-point-nine angstroms overall. The peripheral receptor binding domains were a bit less rigid than the central A-C-E-two–B-zero-A-T-one core, so they applied a technique called focused refinement — which lets you locally sharpen one region of the map at a time — and got the receptor binding domain interface up to about three-and-a-half angstroms. That is sharp enough to see exactly which amino-acid side chains on the virus's receptor binding domain are reaching across to contact which side chains on A-C-E-two.

Now the results. First the A-C-E-two–B-zero-A-T-one complex on its own. The structure is a dimer of heterodimers — in plain terms, two copies of A-C-E-two sandwiched between two copies of B-zero-A-T-one, with the two A-C-E-two molecules dimerising directly with each other down the middle. All the dimerisation contacts are made by A-C-E-two itself; B-zero-A-T-one is along for the ride. This matters, because it means A-C-E-two very likely exists as a dimer in a cell membrane even in tissues where B-zero-A-T-one is not expressed — the dimerisation does not require the transporter. The dimer is held together primarily by a region of A-C-E-two that nobody had seen before, which the authors call the neck domain — a small compact fold just outside the membrane that bridges the enzyme head and the transmembrane helix. That neck is where the real glue is: a dense network of hydrogen bonds and salt bridges between the two neck domains clamps the dimer together.

There is also a weaker interaction up at the top of the molecule, between the two peptidase domains of the dimer. And here comes the second surprise. In their data, the authors could sort particles into two classes. In one class — the closed state — the two peptidase domains are touching each other at the top, with that weak interface in place. In the other class — the open state — the two peptidase domains have rotated apart by about twenty-five angstroms, like a mouth opening, while the neck domains stay clamped together at the bottom. In other words, the dimer breathes. Those two conformations of the receptor are coexisting in the sample at the same time, and they interconvert through a hinge-like motion.

Now the virus. When they add in the receptor binding domain, only the closed state is seen. The receptor binding domain lands on the outer surface of each peptidase domain — one virus piece per A-C-E-two monomer, so two per dimer. The physical contact is beautifully specific. The receptor binding domain presents an extended ridge that lies across the top of a long helix in A-C-E-two called the alpha-one helix, with additional contacts to the alpha-two helix and to a short loop between two strands labelled beta-three and beta-four. The authors describe three clusters of contacts: one at each end of the ridge, and one in the middle. At one end, three virus residues — glutamine four-nine-eight, threonine five hundred, and asparagine five-oh-one — build a network of hydrogen bonds with four A-C-E-two residues. In the middle, lysine four-one-seven of the virus pairs with aspartate thirty of A-C-E-two to form a salt bridge, and tyrosine four-five-three of the virus packs against histidine thirty-four of A-C-E-two. At the other end, glutamine four-seven-four of the virus is hydrogen-bonded to glutamine twenty-four of A-C-E-two, and phenylalanine four-eight-six of the virus pushes into a hydrophobic pocket formed by methionine eighty-two. These are the atomic details of how the virus grabs on. Several of those residue numbers — four-one-seven, four-five-three, four-eight-four and nearby positions, four-eight-six, five-oh-one — are worth remembering, because they come back later in the story. But in March 2020 they are just interface residues.

The authors then do the comparison that the field was most interested in. They superimpose the SARS-coronavirus receptor binding domain, which had been solved in complex with the A-C-E-two peptidase domain back in 2005, onto the new structure, and look residue-by-residue at the interface. The overall shape is very similar between the two viruses — the backbones align well — but a number of positions on the receptor binding domain are different in SARS-coronavirus-two, and some of those changes look likely to strengthen the grip on A-C-E-two. The clearest example is the salt bridge they just identified, between lysine four-one-seven of the virus and aspartate thirty of A-C-E-two: at the equivalent position in SARS-coronavirus there is a valine, which cannot form a salt bridge at all. So SARS-coronavirus-two gains a stable charge-charge interaction at that site. Another change, from leucine four-seven-two in SARS-coronavirus to phenylalanine four-eight-six in SARS-coronavirus-two, enlarges the hydrophobic contact at the other end of the ridge. On the other side of the ledger, a change from arginine four-two-six in SARS-coronavirus to asparagine four-three-nine in SARS-coronavirus-two removes a salt bridge with another A-C-E-two residue. The take-home is that the new virus has not simply pasted the SARS binding solution onto its own spike. It has rebuilt the interface, with some residues gaining strength and others losing it. The net effect — the ten-to-twenty-fold higher binding affinity that Wrapp and colleagues had measured in the previous episode — is explained by the sum of these local changes, not by any single switch.

Finally, the authors fit the ternary complex into the spike trimer structure that Wrapp and McLellan had just published, by aligning the receptor binding domain to its position in the trimer. The question they wanted to answer was: can an A-C-E-two dimer actually bind two spike trimers at the same time, or does the geometry forbid it. They find that if the trimer's receptor binding domain is in the down position — tucked against the body of the spike — then docking it onto A-C-E-two creates clashes with the rest of the trimer. If instead the receptor binding domain is in the up position, there are no clashes. So the hinging motion that Wrapp and colleagues observed, the up-and-down breathing of the receptor binding domain, is not just a feature of spike dynamics — it is a prerequisite for productive A-C-E-two engagement. And geometrically, there is enough room for a receptor dimer to accommodate two separate spike trimers, each contributing one up receptor binding domain, at the same time.

Now the discussion. Why did this matter in early March 2020. A few things. First, this was the native-state picture of the receptor. Up to now every structural analysis of A-C-E-two had used only the enzyme head, in isolation, with no membrane context and no information about whether A-C-E-two was a monomer or an oligomer at the cell surface. This paper shows it is a dimer, with a specific dimer interface in a part of the molecule that had never been seen. That has consequences for how we think about receptor clustering on infected cells, and about how multivalent therapeutics — soluble A-C-E-two decoys, for example, or antibodies that engage two spike protomers at once — might be designed. Second, it gave the field a shared mental model for how the virus actually docks. The receptor binding domain lands on the alpha-one helix of A-C-E-two, mostly through polar contacts; the surface area and residue list are not large, which is one reason why antibodies targeting the receptor binding tip, or small decoy proteins, could in principle compete effectively. Third, it provided a residue-level account of why the new virus binds a human receptor so tightly, which is one of the key factors underlying the clinical observation of efficient human-to-human transmission. The genome had already told the field, within days of the sequence release, that A-C-E-two was the probable receptor; this paper tells us why that pairing is a particularly good fit.

What they were honest about. The structure of the ternary complex was obtained with the receptor binding domain fragment, not the full spike trimer, so some of the biology that depends on spike quaternary context is necessarily absent. The two conformations of the dimer — open and closed — are intriguing but their biological role is not established in this paper; the authors flag it as something to investigate. They also note that the C-terminal region of A-C-E-two is a known cleavage site for the host protease T-M-P-R-S-S-two, which another group — Hoffmann and Pöhlmann in Cell — had just shown to be important for spike cleavage and viral entry. In this structure, that cleavable region sits right at the dimer interface, which raises the question of whether being in a dimer, or being bound to B-zero-A-T-one, might modulate T-M-P-R-S-S-two access. The authors do not resolve this, but they put it on the table.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment, briefly. Several of the interface residues identified in this paper would turn into famous names over the next two years as the virus evolved. The lysine at position four-one-seven becomes mutated — to asparagine, in the Beta variant, and to threonine, in the Gamma variant — in ways that specifically trade off receptor affinity against antibody escape. The asparagine at position five-oh-one is the N-five-oh-one-Y change — asparagine to tyrosine at position five-oh-one — that is shared across Alpha, Beta, Gamma and Omicron and that tightens the receptor grip. The glutamate at position four-eight-four, right at the edge of the contact patch, is where many antibody-escape mutations land. None of this is in the paper we just covered. In March 2020, these were just residues at a protein-protein interface. The paper gave the field the structural atlas; the mutational re-visits of each of those positions would come later.
