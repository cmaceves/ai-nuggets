## Script

Paper link: Wu et al., "A new coronavirus associated with human respiratory disease in China." Nature, 3 February 2020. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2008-3

Welcome to SARS-CoV-2: A History Run-Through. I'm your host, Nigel. This show walks through the SARS-CoV-2 literature in historical order, one paper per episode, from the first 2020 reports up to the present. The idea is to experience the pandemic the way the field did — not knowing yet what the next variant would do, watching the tools get built, watching ideas get revised. We're starting at the beginning.

Episode one. The paper is "A new coronavirus associated with human respiratory disease in China," published in Nature on the third of February, 2020, by Fan Wu, Yong-Zhen Zhang, Edward Holmes, and colleagues at the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center, Fudan University, and the University of Sydney.

Let's set the scene. Early February 2020. In Wuhan, a cluster of pneumonia cases of unknown cause had been reported to the World Health Organization at the very end of December. By late January, cases had been reported outside China. The virus had just been given its provisional name, two-thousand-nineteen novel coronavirus. The world was watching, but the thing itself — what exactly was causing this illness, where it came from, how it got into people — was still only partially pinned down. This paper is one of the first two reports in Nature that answers the first of those questions.

Here's the headline. The authors took a single bronchoalveolar lavage sample from one hospitalized pneumonia patient in Wuhan, ran it through unbiased deep sequencing, and recovered the complete genome of a previously-unknown coronavirus. They showed it belonged to the same subgenus as the original SARS virus from two-thousand-three. They showed its receptor-binding machinery looked a lot like the SARS virus — meaning it was very plausibly able to use the human A-C-E-two receptor to get into cells, which is a technical way of saying this thing was built to infect humans. They named their isolate W-H-Human-one coronavirus, and they released the full genome sequence. Note added in proof: by the time the paper went to press, the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses had renamed the virus SARS-CoV-two, and the World Health Organization had named the disease it causes COVID-nineteen.

Now the background. Why did this matter and why was this the right experiment to run. Coronaviruses are a large family of enveloped R-N-A viruses. Four of them circulate endemically in humans and cause mostly mild colds. Two of them — the original SARS coronavirus in two-thousand-three, and MERS coronavirus starting in twenty-twelve — had jumped from animals and caused severe outbreaks. Both of those earlier outbreaks were ultimately traced back to bats, with intermediate mammalian hosts in between. So by late December twenty-nineteen, when a cluster of severe pneumonia started appearing in Wuhan, and the usual culprits — influenza, chlamydia, mycoplasma, adenovirus — all came back negative, a new coronavirus of animal origin was high on the list of hypotheses. But hypothesis is not proof. You need a genome. And ideally you don't want to use P-C-R primers that assume what the answer is, because if you assume wrong you miss. That's where unbiased meta-transcriptomic sequencing comes in, and that's the central methodological choice of this paper.

So what did they do. The patient was a forty-one-year-old man who had been admitted to the Central Hospital of Wuhan on the twenty-sixth of December, twenty-nineteen, six days after symptom onset. He had fever, cough, chest tightness, abnormal chest imaging consistent with pneumonia, and he had worked at a local indoor seafood market that also sold live wild animals, including hedgehogs, badgers, snakes, and birds — but not bats. During admission, the clinicians collected bronchoalveolar lavage fluid — that's fluid flushed into and recovered from the deep lung — and the authors took that sample into a biosafety-level-three laboratory.

The sequencing approach is worth taking slowly, because it's the engine that makes the rest of the paper possible. Meta-transcriptomic sequencing means: pull out all the R-N-A in the sample, without pre-selecting for any particular organism, and sequence everything. You get reads from human cells, from any bacteria that happen to be in the airway, and critically, from any R-N-A virus present — including viruses nobody has sequenced before. The authors extracted total R-N-A, depleted ribosomal R-N-A so it wouldn't drown out the rest, built a sequencing library, and ran it on an Illumina MiniSeq, generating about fifty-six-and-a-half million paired-end reads at one-hundred-fifty bases per read. Then — and this is the key move — they ran de novo assembly. De novo means they didn't try to map reads back to any known reference genome; they let the assembler stitch overlapping reads into longer contiguous sequences, called contigs, without prejudice about what organism those contigs would belong to. They used two assemblers, Megahit and Trinity, and compared results. The longest contig Megahit produced was about thirty-thousand four-hundred nucleotides long, which is right in the size range you expect for a coronavirus genome. When they searched that contig against the public sequence databases, its closest match was a SARS-like coronavirus previously isolated from a bat in China — call it bat SL-CoV-Z-C-forty-five — at about eighty-nine percent nucleotide identity. Eighty-nine percent means related, same ballpark, but definitely a different virus. They then nailed down the exact ends of the genome with a technique called rapid amplification of c-D-N-A ends, confirmed the sequence with targeted P-C-R, and remapped all the reads back to the finished genome to check coverage. The final genome was twenty-nine-thousand nine-hundred three nucleotides, with ninety-nine-point-nine-nine percent coverage. They uploaded it to GenBank, and the viral load in that lung-wash sample was high — on the order of four hundred million copies per milliliter.

With a genome in hand, the next question is: what is this thing and where does it fit on the family tree. They built maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees — a maximum-likelihood tree is a statistical reconstruction of evolutionary relationships that finds the tree topology under which the observed sequence differences are most probable. They built trees based on the whole genome, and also based on individual genes: the two big replicase genes O-R-F-one-a and O-R-F-one-b, the spike gene, and the structural genes for envelope, membrane, and nucleocapsid proteins. Across all those trees, the new virus landed inside the subgenus Sarbecovirus — the same group that contains the original SARS virus and a number of SARS-like coronaviruses previously sampled from bats. But — and this is interesting — the new virus didn't sit in the same place on every tree. In the spike-gene tree it clustered closest to that bat virus from Z-C-forty-five. In the O-R-F-one-b tree it fell in a basal position, further out. When different genes give you different trees from the same organism, the standard interpretation is historical recombination. Coronaviruses recombine readily when two viruses co-infect the same cell, and the authors confirmed, using recombination-detection software called R-D-P-four and similarity-plot analysis, that there were signatures of past recombination within the spike gene among sarbecoviruses — though no clean evidence that any one recombination event is what gave rise to this particular new virus.

Then they went in on the spike protein, and specifically on the receptor binding domain — the part of the spike that physically grips a host-cell surface receptor. For SARS-coronavirus, that receptor is A-C-E-two on human cells. The authors aligned the receptor binding domain of the new virus against the equivalent region in SARS-coronavirus and in a panel of bat SARS-like coronaviruses. The sequence identity to SARS-coronavirus was in the seventy-three to seventy-five percent range at the amino-acid level — and crucially, the new virus did not carry the deletions in that region that characterize the bat sarbecoviruses that are known to be unable to bind human A-C-E-two. They also built a predicted three-dimensional structure of the new virus's receptor binding domain by homology modeling, and it looked like the SARS-coronavirus binding domain. The authors stopped short of claiming this proves human A-C-E-two usage — this paper does not include a binding assay — but they laid out a very specific prediction: this virus likely enters human cells through A-C-E-two, and if so, it is capable of human-to-human transmission.

Why did this matter. First, the simple answer: when a new respiratory pathogen is causing severe disease in a growing cluster of patients, the single most actionable thing public health can do is get the genome, release it, and let the world build diagnostics. The authors released the complete sequence, GenBank accession M-N-nine-oh-eight-nine-four-seven. Within days, labs around the world were using it to design P-C-R diagnostics, which is why we had tests at all. Second, by placing the virus phylogenetically in the sarbecovirus group with a bat relative, they corroborated the hypothesis that this was a zoonotic spillover, probably of ultimate bat origin, consistent with how the previous SARS and MERS outbreaks had gone. Third, the receptor binding domain analysis gave the field an early, well-founded prediction about the molecular entry mechanism, which is where a huge amount of subsequent biology — vaccines, monoclonals, antivirals — would be aimed.

The authors are very clear about the limitations. This is one patient and one virus isolate. They cannot formally conclude from a single sample that this virus is causing the outbreak — Koch's postulates aren't met here. They note that an independent paper, in parallel, corroborated the finding in additional patients. They flag that the natural reservoir has not been established — bats are a plausible host, there is high sequence similarity to bat sarbecoviruses, but the virus they recovered is not identical to any known bat virus, and whether an intermediate host was involved was unknown at the time of writing. The seafood market was a possible site of exposure, but the paper is careful not to overclaim what role the market played, because the patient could have been exposed through contact with animals there without those animals being the ultimate source.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment, briefly. The genome released here, M-N-nine-oh-eight-nine-four-seven, became the reference sequence that essentially the entire first year of the pandemic response was built on top of — diagnostics, vaccines, variant surveillance. The receptor binding domain prediction was quickly confirmed: this virus does use human A-C-E-two, and the spike protein became the target of nearly every COVID vaccine. The question of ultimate origin — the identity of the specific bat reservoir, whether there was an intermediate host, the role of the market — remained open and contested well beyond this paper, and we'll come back to those arguments in later episodes.

That's episode one. The paper reference and link are in the show notes. Next time, the companion paper from the same week — Zhou and colleagues, from the Wuhan Institute of Virology — which independently sequenced the virus from additional patients and dug further into the bat connection.
