## Script

Paper link: Andersen et al., "The proximal origin of SARS-CoV-2." Nature Medicine, 17 March 2020. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-020-0820-9

Welcome back to SARS-CoV-2: A History Run-Through. I'm your host, and today we're looking at a paper published in Nature Medicine on the seventeenth of March, 2020 — "The proximal origin of SARS-CoV-2," by Kristian Andersen at Scripps, Andrew Rambaut at Edinburgh, Ian Lipkin at Columbia, Eddie Holmes at Sydney, and Robert Garry at Tulane. Five of the most senior figures in viral evolution and emerging infectious disease, putting their names together on a single short piece.

To situate this: it's mid-March 2020. The World Health Organization declared COVID-19 a pandemic on the eleventh of March, less than a week before this paper came out. The authors note that by that date there were about 121,000 confirmed cases across more than 110 countries and about 4,400 deaths. Italy's hospitals are in crisis. The United States is three days away from a national emergency declaration. And in parallel with the public-health emergency, a very loud, very public argument has started about where the virus came from — whether it jumped from an animal, or whether it leaked, or was engineered, in a laboratory. This paper is the virology community's attempt to lay out, in a formal venue, what the genomic evidence can and can't say about that question.

So — the headline. Unlike the other papers we've looked at so far, this isn't a primary research report. It's a Perspective. The authors do not generate new sequence data; they review what's already in the public record — the SARS-CoV-2 genome itself, the closest bat and pangolin coronaviruses that had been sequenced, structural work on the spike protein, and the general literature on coronavirus evolution. They argue two things. First, that the genomic data are inconsistent with SARS-CoV-2 being a purposefully engineered virus, and inconsistent, in their view, with it being the product of cell-culture or animal passage in a lab. Second, that two natural scenarios can plausibly explain its emergence: either it adapted to humans in an animal host before jumping, or it jumped to humans in a cruder form and adapted during a period of undetected human-to-human spread. They say the current data don't let them decide between those two.

Let's back up and set the stage for why this paper exists. In February and early March of 2020, the sequence of SARS-CoV-2 was already out — Episode 1 and Episode 2 of this show covered the first two Nature papers that reported it. From those sequences and the structural work in Episode 3 on the spike, several things were obvious. One, this was a sarbecovirus — a sub-genus of betacoronaviruses that includes SARS-CoV, the virus behind the 2003 outbreak, and a sprawling diversity of bat coronaviruses. Two, the closest known relative was a bat virus called RaTG13, sampled from a horseshoe bat in Yunnan, which Shi Zheng-Li's group at the Wuhan Institute of Virology had archived years earlier. RaTG13 is about ninety-six percent identical to SARS-CoV-2 across the genome. Three, the spike protein — the surface protein that binds the human receptor — had features that fit human infection suspiciously well. And four, nobody had yet found an animal virus close enough to be the direct ancestor.

Into that gap, questions had rushed in. Was the spike engineered? Was it adapted in a lab by passaging a bat virus through human cells until it bound well? Did it escape from the Wuhan Institute of Virology, which is a few kilometers from the Huanan market where many early cases clustered? Andersen and colleagues set out to say what the genome itself constrains in that debate.

To follow their argument you need two concepts, so let me define them.

The first is the receptor binding domain, which I'll call the receptor binding domain or just the R-B-D. Coronaviruses have a large spike protein on their surface, and inside that spike there's a specific sub-region whose job is to physically touch and grip the host cell's receptor protein. For SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 that receptor is a human cell-surface enzyme called ACE2 — angiotensin-converting enzyme 2. The receptor binding domain is the lock-and-key interface. Six individual amino-acid positions inside it are known to determine, in SARS-like coronaviruses, how well the spike grips ACE2 from a given species. Five of those six positions differ between SARS-CoV-2 and the original 2003 SARS-CoV. So SARS-CoV-2 has effectively a different solution to the same problem.

The second concept is the furin cleavage site. The spike has to be cut by a host protease into two pieces, called S1 and S2, before it can drive fusion of the viral envelope with the cell membrane. In SARS-CoV-2 the junction between S1 and S2 contains a short run of basic amino acids — the sequence arginine, arginine, alanine, arginine — which is a motif recognised by a human enzyme called furin. Furin is extremely widely expressed, so having a furin cleavage site means the virus can be activated by proteases in many tissues rather than being restricted to tissues that express a specific one. That's a classic virulence feature; it's well known in influenza, where the acquisition of a polybasic cleavage site in the hemagglutinin protein is what turns a low-pathogenicity avian flu into a highly pathogenic one. Importantly, no other sarbecovirus — no previously described bat or pangolin SARS-like coronavirus — had been seen with a polybasic cleavage site at this position. And in SARS-CoV-2 the cleavage site is a twelve-nucleotide insertion relative to its closest relatives, not a one-letter mutation. There are also three predicted O-linked glycans — sugar attachments to the protein backbone — right around the cleavage site, which are also unique to this virus.

Those two features — the receptor binding domain that grips human ACE2, and the brand-new furin cleavage site — are the pieces of the puzzle the paper is really working with.

Now, their methods, such as they are. There are no bench experiments in this paper. The analysis is a comparative-genomics argument. The authors line up the SARS-CoV-2 spike against a panel of related sarbecoviruses — RaTG13, SARS-CoV, and sequences from Malayan pangolins that had been illegally imported into Guangdong province, which had been shown earlier that year to carry SARS-like coronaviruses. They compare the six key receptor-binding residues one by one across those viruses. They then look at the S1/S2 junction across the same panel to establish that the polybasic cleavage site is unique to SARS-CoV-2 within the sarbecovirus clade. And they cross-reference against structural and biochemical work from other labs — including the spike cryo-EM structure we covered last episode — to characterise how good the SARS-CoV-2 spike actually is at binding human ACE2.

With that setup, here's what the comparison showed. The RaTG13 bat virus has the overall closest genome, but its receptor binding domain diverges from SARS-CoV-2 at exactly those critical contact residues, which means RaTG13 itself is predicted to bind human ACE2 poorly. The pangolin coronaviruses are different: at the whole-genome level they're more distant than RaTG13, but inside the receptor binding domain some pangolin viruses are strikingly similar to SARS-CoV-2 — all six of the key ACE2 contact residues match. That pattern — closest relative in one region of the genome, closest relative in a different region elsewhere — is the fingerprint of a mosaic genome produced by recombination between related viruses co-circulating in their natural hosts. On the furin cleavage site: it's not present in any sampled bat or pangolin sarbecovirus, including RaTG13.

That's the data. Here's what they argue it implies.

Their first conclusion is that the virus wasn't deliberately engineered. The argument runs like this. Years of structural work had been done, before SARS-CoV-2, on what an ideal set of contact residues for binding human ACE2 would look like. SARS-CoV-2's receptor binding domain is not that ideal set. It's a different solution — one that binds human ACE2 tightly, but through a configuration that computational predictions from before the pandemic would not have identified as optimal. If someone were engineering a spike to bind human ACE2, the authors argue, they would almost certainly have used the previously-predicted optimal residues; they wouldn't have landed on a novel configuration that nobody had predicted would work. A novel, counter-intuitive, high-affinity binding solution looks much more like something that fell out of natural selection in a real host than something hand-designed from the existing literature. On top of that, there are several well-established reverse-genetics systems for betacoronaviruses, and the SARS-CoV-2 genome has no discernible trace of any of those backbones. If someone built it, they didn't start from any virus anyone has published a method for building from.

Their second conclusion is that it wasn't produced by passaging a bat virus through cell culture or animal models until it adapted — the softer version of the lab-origin story. The pangolin receptor binding domain gives a cleaner, more parsimonious explanation of the spike residues than cell-culture adaptation would — basically, those residues already exist in nature, in a close relative, and recombination with such a host-adapted lineage is a well-documented mechanism in coronaviruses. And the furin cleavage site is the harder piece: polybasic cleavage sites have been generated by extended cell-culture or animal passage only in avian influenza, and only after very prolonged in vitro or in vivo passage of a low-pathogenicity progenitor. No one had reported anything equivalent for coronaviruses, and no one had published having a progenitor virus close enough to SARS-CoV-2 to begin such passage experiments from. The predicted O-linked glycans around the cleavage site are an additional problem for a culture-origin story: those glycan patterns are suggestive of selection in a host with an immune system, not in an immortalised cell line.

So — the lab-origin side having been pushed off the table by the evidence as they read it — the paper lays out two natural scenarios and declines to pick between them.

Scenario one is natural selection in an animal host before the virus jumped to humans. In this version, both the ACE2-adapted receptor binding domain and the furin cleavage site arose in some non-human population — plausibly through recombination in bats, pangolins or some other mammal with a high enough population density to let selection work and with an ACE2 receptor similar enough to the human one that adaptation to that host would also be, incidentally, adaptation to us. The virus then jumped into humans already loaded with the features it needed to spread.

Scenario two is natural selection in humans after an earlier, cruder zoonotic jump. In this version, something like the pangolin-receptor-binding-domain configuration jumps into people — because the pangolin virus data suggests that residue combination was probably already present in whatever jumped — but the furin cleavage site arises later, during an initial period of undetected human-to-human transmission, before the outbreak was large enough to be noticed. They note that estimates of the most recent common ancestor of all sequenced SARS-CoV-2 genomes point to late November or early December 2019, which is consistent with such a period of cryptic spread occurring before the Wuhan cluster was recognised. They draw an analogy to MERS, where every human case is a fresh jump from camels producing short, self-limiting chains — the point being that short, invisible human transmission chains can accumulate before a virus crosses the threshold into pandemic potential.

A few things worth being honest about in what this paper does and doesn't do. It is a Perspective, not a primary report. It presents no new sequence, no new experiment, no new structure. Its argument against engineering rests partly on an aesthetic — that the SARS-CoV-2 receptor binding domain doesn't match what pre-pandemic computational models said an engineered one should look like — and the authors acknowledge that their position doesn't formally rule the engineering scenario out, only makes it unlikely in their reading. Its argument against cell-culture passage rests on the absence of a published progenitor virus and the absence of equivalent passage results in coronaviruses, which is an argument from the state of the literature rather than from the virus itself. And the two natural-origin scenarios are proposed, not demonstrated — the paper is quite open that distinguishing between them would require data that doesn't yet exist, in particular the identification of an intermediate animal host, a progenitor virus with partial features, or sequences from very early human cases.

The authors are also explicit about why answering this question matters beyond the public argument. If the virus pre-adapted in another species, then that species is still out there, and the same genetic preconditions could produce another spillover. If instead the adaptation happened in people after a crude initial jump, then repeated zoonotic transfers from an animal reservoir aren't going to easily reproduce the pandemic, because they'd each have to re-evolve the same adaptations. The two scenarios have genuinely different implications for surveillance strategy and for assessing re-emergence risk.

Taken on its own terms, in mid-March 2020, this paper is doing something quite specific. It's a short piece in a high-visibility venue, co-authored by five people the field takes seriously, saying essentially: here is what the genome tells us, here is what it doesn't tell us, and the lab-origin scenarios are not where the data point. It became one of the most-read and most-cited pieces of pandemic-era virology, and it also became politically contested in a way few scientific publications ever are.

Stepping outside the paper for a moment, briefly. Over the years that followed, additional data arrived that both parts of this picture had to be revisited in light of. Sarbecoviruses closer to SARS-CoV-2 than RaTG13 were found in bats in Laos. Further analyses of the Huanan market early-case geography were published. The authors' private correspondence from the period preceding this paper was later released and widely re-examined. The debate over whether a laboratory incident can be ruled out has continued, and reasonable scientists disagree. None of that is in the paper we just discussed, and the point of this show is to meet each paper where it stood when it was published. What was on the table in March 2020 was two Nature papers of sequence data, a cryo-EM structure, a handful of related bat and pangolin coronaviruses, and the arguments laid out here. That was the evidence Andersen and colleagues were working from, and that's the evidence we've walked through today.
