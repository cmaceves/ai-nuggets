## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Tuesday, August eleventh, twenty twenty six. Today: two emerging tick-borne viruses that most listeners have probably never heard of just got sequenced from Nebraska ticks for the first time, and the phylogenetics tell you exactly where they came from and how fast they're moving. Then, on the business side, Jazz Pharmaceuticals just paid up to one point three billion dollars for a tiny biotech developing a first-in-class ion-channel drug for a devastating childhood epilepsy that affects about twenty-five hundred kids in the U S — and the mechanism is a genuinely interesting piece of neuroscience. In A I for medicine, Google published its most ambitious clinical A I to date: an agent that runs a video visit with a patient, does the interview, does the physical, and delivers a diagnosis and a management plan — and their randomized head-to-head against primary care doctors is worth walking through. And to close: a lovely paper in the journal Evolution that answers a question you may have never thought to ask — why were there no mouse-sized dinosaurs?

Let's get into it.

Pathogen watch, first. A team at the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services, the Nebraska Public Health Laboratory, and the University of Nebraska Medical Center posted a preprint on medRxiv this morning reporting the first-ever detections of two emerging tick-borne viruses — Heartland virus and Bourbon virus — from ticks collected in eastern Nebraska. And crucially, they didn't just find them; they sequenced complete genomes and did a phylogenetic analysis that tells a very concrete story about how these viruses are moving across the American Midwest. A note up front: I'm going off the abstract for this one — the preprint went up this morning and the full body hasn't rendered yet — but the phylogenetic result is the take, and the abstract is quite specific about it.

First, the setup. Heartland virus and Bourbon virus are both what's called *emerging* tick-borne arboviruses — arbovirus is short for arthropod-borne virus, meaning a virus whose main transmission cycle runs through a blood-feeding arthropod like a tick or a mosquito. Both of these are carried by the same vector: the lone star tick, *Amblyomma americanum*, which is the small tick with the single white spot on the back of the adult female that most people in the southeastern and south-central U S know from summer hikes. Heartland virus is a bunyavirus — the same viral family as Rift Valley fever and Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever, though far less dangerous than either — and Bourbon virus is a thogotovirus, related to influenza but in a separate genus that circulates mostly in ticks. Both were only described in the last fifteen years — Heartland in twenty twelve from a Missouri farmer, Bourbon in twenty fourteen from a Kansas man who died — and both have caused dozens of confirmed human cases, most with fever, low white counts and low platelets, and a small but real number of deaths.

Until very recently, both viruses were geographically anchored to the Missouri-Kansas-Tennessee-Arkansas area — which is also where lone star tick populations were densest. That's changing. Lone star ticks have been expanding north and west for decades, driven by warming winters and deer population changes, and the concern for public health people has been: does the virus expansion track the tick expansion? Now we have an answer for one edge of the range.

Here's what the Nebraska team did. Starting in twenty twenty four, the state health lab added Heartland and Bourbon virus screening to its ongoing tick-pathogen surveillance program. They collected lone star ticks from across eastern Nebraska, pooled them, and screened by P C R. Ticks from two counties in the eastern part of the state came back positive for Heartland; a single pool came back positive for Bourbon. Then — and this is the important methodological piece — rather than stopping at "positive P C R," they developed a new amplicon-based whole-genome sequencing strategy, essentially a set of overlapping P C R primers that tile the entire viral genome, and used it to pull complete genome sequences off those three Heartland-positive and one Bourbon-positive pools. Whole genomes let you do proper phylogenetics — you can place the Nebraska viruses on an evolutionary tree with all the other publicly available sequences and ask, in effect, "which population did these come from, and when did they diverge?"

Here's the finding. The three Nebraska Heartland genomes form what phylogeneticists call a monophyletic cluster — a tight little clade, all descended from a single common ancestor to the exclusion of everything else on the tree — and that common ancestor time-calibrates to viruses that were circulating in Missouri in the early two thousands. Which means the Nebraska Heartland virus is essentially a Missouri lineage that has been quietly hitchhiking with expanding tick populations for twenty-plus years, and only now got caught because someone thought to look. The Bourbon virus story is different — the single Nebraska genome sits on a long branch, meaning it's genetically distant from anything sequenced before, and probably diverged from the previously sampled Bourbon genomes in the early twenty tens. Long branches on a phylogeny are a diagnostic of undersampling — they usually mean there's a lot of unsampled diversity out there in the tick population that no one has ever sequenced, not that this one virus mutated at a wild rate.

Why does this matter? Three things. One: this is the western edge of the lone star tick range meeting a virus that's been silently expanding with it. It's a good example of how genomic surveillance can pick up an ecological range shift before you see the first human case — and Nebraska has not yet reported a confirmed human Heartland or Bourbon infection, so this is preemptive. Two: the amplicon-based whole-genome sequencing method they built is portable. Other state labs can now adopt it and start generating comparable genomes, which is what you'd need to actually trace this thing in real time as it continues to expand. And three, on the clinical side: for a physician in the middle of the country seeing a patient with an unexplained summer fever, low platelets, low white count, and a recent tick bite, Heartland and Bourbon should now be on the differential a lot further west than the old maps say. Doxycycline, the standard empiric antibiotic for tick-borne bacterial diseases, does absolutely nothing for these viruses — so if a patient isn't responding, that's the tell.

On the business side. Yesterday morning, Jazz Pharmaceuticals — the Irish specialty-pharma company best known for the sleep drug Xywav and the pediatric-epilepsy cannabidiol drug Epidiolex — announced it's acquiring a private San Diego biotech called Actio Biosciences for eight hundred twenty million dollars up front, and up to another five hundred million in contingent payments tied to approval and sales milestones. So potentially about one point three billion dollars for a company most listeners have never heard of. What Jazz is really buying is a single drug — a Phase one-b, two-a asset called A B S twelve-thirty — and it's worth understanding what that drug is, because it's a nice example of what modern precision-medicine deal-making actually looks like.

Let's start with the disease. KCNT one epilepsy — or, if you prefer, K C N T one epilepsy — is a rare genetic developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. About twenty-five hundred children in the United States are affected. Eighty percent of those children have their first seizures in infancy — often in the first six months of life. And the seizure burden, unlike most epilepsies, is catastrophic: dozens to hundreds of seizures a day, resistant to every standard antiepileptic drug that gets tried. On top of the seizures, these children have severe developmental delays; life expectancy is significantly shortened. There is, right now, no approved treatment.

The gene at the center of this is KCNT one, which encodes a potassium channel called Slack — a sodium-activated potassium channel that sits in the membrane of certain brain neurons and lets potassium ions flow out when the cell has just been electrically active. Potassium leaving the cell is what *ends* an action potential — it's how a neuron quiets itself back down and gets ready to fire again. In KCNT one epilepsy, the mutation is what geneticists call a "gain of function" — the mutant channel opens more easily and lets more potassium out. Which, at first, sounds like it should be *anti*-epileptic — quieter neurons, less firing, right?

Wrong, and this is the interesting bit. The neurons that most rely on the Slack channel are inhibitory interneurons — the fast-spiking cells whose job is to *suppress* the excitatory neurons around them. If you over-quiet the inhibitory neurons, you've removed the brakes from the network, and the excitatory neurons run away. That runaway is the seizure. So the paradox resolves cleanly at the circuit level: a hyperactive potassium channel silences the wrong cells and lets the network burn.

Which tells you exactly what a good drug should do. Block the mutant channel. Restore the balance. That's A B S twelve-thirty. It's a small molecule, taken orally, designed as a first-in-class KCNT one channel inhibitor. In the preclinical work, Actio showed the compound inhibits KCNT one across all the pathogenic mutations they could test — which matters because there are many different point mutations that cause this disease, and you need one drug that works against the whole spectrum. An early proof-of-concept trial in children showed what Actio describes as meaningful seizure reductions, and the drug is now in the KYRON trial — a fifty-five-patient Phase one-b, two-a study that F D A has told Actio can be used to support a registrational filing. It has Fast Track, Rare Pediatric Disease, and Orphan Drug designations, and it's been admitted to the F D A's Rare Disease Evidence Principles program — a relatively new pathway designed to accept smaller trials when the disease population is small and the unmet need is severe.

Now the strategic layer. Jazz already dominates the rare-epilepsy space with Epidiolex. Adding a second, mechanistically different, first-in-class rare epilepsy drug to that portfolio is exactly the shape of expansion that makes sense for Jazz — same specialty physicians, same rare-disease infrastructure, complementary rather than competing. On Actio's side: a small biotech with one lead asset that was probably years from being able to run its own commercial launch just skipped that whole risk and monetized. And the deal terms are worth reading closely — up-front money is a slim majority of the total, and the rest is contingent on the drug actually getting approved and selling. That's the shape of a modern deal for a de-risked-but-not-approved asset. Expect to see more of this: mid-cap pharma buying single-asset rare-disease biotechs at the Phase one-b, two-a inflection point, once the compound has shown a real signal in humans but before the compound has to survive a commercial launch on its own.

Now, A I in biomedicine. Google Research and Google DeepMind posted a paper to arXiv yesterday describing a system called A M I E — that's the Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer — in its newest form, which they're calling A M I E Video. Everything up to this point about A M I E has been text-based: A M I E would take a written patient description and generate a differential diagnosis and management plan, and Google had published results showing it could match primary care physicians in a text-based interview. This new work is the multimodal version: A M I E runs the whole visit — takes the history, watches the patient through a live video feed, guides them through a physical exam, and then produces a diagnosis and management plan. And they benchmarked it head-to-head against thirty primary care physicians.

The setup is worth understanding. It's a randomized O S C E — that's an Objective Structured Clinical Examination, the standardized-patient exam that every medical student takes for licensure. Fifteen trained patient actors, each playing one of a hundred clinical scenarios that span both physical-exam-heavy and history-heavy presentations. Each scenario is run twice, in random order: once with A M I E, once with a primary care physician conducting the visit by video. The visits are recorded. Then a separate panel of clinical evaluators, blinded to which visit was which, scores every visit on four axes — quality of the history, accuracy of the diagnosis, appropriateness of the management plan, and quality of the physical observation and exam. Separately, the patient actors themselves rated the two experiences for satisfaction and rapport.

Here's what they found. On history-taking, on diagnosis, on management, and on physical observation and examination, blinded clinical evaluators rated A M I E as *on par with or better than* the primary care physicians. On patient satisfaction, the patient actors preferred A M I E for the quality of its explanations — how well it explained what it was thinking and what it was recommending — but preferred the human physicians for rapport-building and for handling emotional beats in the interview. The paper is quite candid about the remaining gaps: fine anatomical detail is still hard for the vision system, subtle emotional cues are still missed, and rapid movement — a tremor, a gait — is still imperfectly captured.

Why does this matter? Two things. One, this is the first study I've seen where a general-purpose language model system, with a vision-and-audio front end, has been *evaluated in the actual format that human physicians are certified in* — the O S C E — and matched them on the clinical-quality axes that matter. That's a real bar. Two, and more important for practice: the piece where A M I E did well and the piece where it didn't split cleanly along the technical-versus-human axis. Diagnostic reasoning, differential generation, communication clarity — technical work that can be trained on huge amounts of medical text — matched or beat human doctors. Rapport, emotional attunement — the parts of medicine that are actually about being with another person — the humans still owned. That's a hint of what the near-term division of labor might look like in telemedicine: A I as the clinician's diagnostic partner or as the first-contact triage layer, with human physicians doing the parts of medicine that are intrinsically about being human.

The paper is a research paper, not a product — this is not something you can log into today, and every result is on standardized patient actors, not real patients — but it moves the needle.

And finally — animals. A short but very fun paper out this week in the journal *Evolution*, from Stephanie Lechki at Princeton and Roger Benson at the American Museum of Natural History. The question they went after is one of those questions that sounds strange until someone points it out and then sounds obvious. Here it is: why were there no tiny dinosaurs?

Think about that. The dinosaurs ran the terrestrial ecosystems of the planet for roughly a hundred and sixty million years. And in all that time, across every lineage they radiated into, the smallest known non-avian dinosaur weighed about four hundred fifty grams — around one pound, about the size of a large rabbit. Meanwhile, if you look at modern mammals — descended from the same-era ancestors — about seventy-five percent of living species are *smaller* than that. Ninety percent of birds are smaller. There are mammals — the Etruscan shrew — and birds — the bee hummingbird — that weigh less than two grams. There are dwarf geckos that weigh a fifth of a gram. But there was, apparently, never a dinosaur small enough to fit in your hand.

So Lechki and Benson built a mathematical model. It's a body-size evolution model — you start with some ancestral size and let the model tell you where selection should push a lineage over time, based on very basic physiology: how much energy an animal can take in, how much energy it needs to reproduce, how those tradeoffs scale with body size. Then they fit that model to living groups — mammals, birds, turtles — as a sanity check. It captures those size distributions well. Then they applied it to the non-avian dinosaurs. And it does not capture them. Even after they allowed the physiological parameters to vary across a very wide range, the model kept predicting that dinosaurs *should* have evolved smaller forms — much smaller than they actually did — because those smaller sizes are, in principle, energetically favorable.

Which means: something outside the physiology was preventing it. The authors' hypothesis is competition — specifically, competition from early mammals. Mammals had been small, nocturnal, and quietly numerous for the entire span of the dinosaur era, and they had *already colonized* the ecological niches that mouse-sized dinosaurs would have needed to occupy. Benson has a nice line about the symmetry of the argument: for years the story about mammals has been that dinosaurs prevented mammals from evolving into large bodies until the asteroid cleared the deck. Their finding is that the reverse was equally true — mammals, quietly, from below, prevented dinosaurs from evolving into small bodies. Two whole clades of vertebrates locking each other out of half of the size spectrum, for over a hundred million years. And the moment one of them exited stage left, the mammals radiated into everything from bats to blue whales.

The final wrinkle. Birds — which *are* dinosaurs, technically — escaped this constraint dramatically. There are lots of tiny birds. And the authors argue that powered flight is what did it: taking to the air opened up whole new ecological niches — insect hawking, nectar feeding, canopy foraging — where mammals hadn't been. That escape route is why we have hummingbirds.
