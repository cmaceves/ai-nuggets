## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Wednesday, August nineteenth, twenty twenty six. Today: a preprint out of Paul Bieniasz's lab at Rockefeller identifies a viral enzyme that drives a rare but powerful class of mutations in coronaviruses — insertions — and argues that this normal, everyday mutation process is plausibly how the SARS cov two spike protein acquired its furin cleavage site in the first place. That's a specific answer to one of the most-litigated origin questions of the last five years. On the business side, Merck and Moderna announced this morning that their personalized mRNA cancer vaccine hit both of its Phase three endpoints in patients with completely resected melanoma — the first Phase three win for an individualized neoantigen therapy, and a genuinely landmark result. And Amylyx yesterday reported a very clean Phase three win for avexitide in post-bariatric hypoglycemia, a debilitating condition with no approved treatments. In AI for biomedicine, a group at Zhejiang University and Tencent shows you can teach a large language model to reason about cell biology by pointing it at existing gene-perturbation experiments and letting reinforcement learning do the rest. And to close: a new fossil study argues that in Middle Miocene South America, the animal at the top of the food chain wasn't a sabertoothed cat or a terror bird — it was a twenty-three-foot caiman relative.

Let's get into it.

First, pathogen watch.

The Bieniasz lab at Rockefeller University, working with the Hatziioannou lab there, posted a preprint two days ago that I think is going to end up widely discussed. It proposes a mechanistic answer to one of the most nagging questions in coronavirus evolution — how does a coronavirus, in the normal course of replicating in its host, generate the kind of large, functionally consequential insertion mutations that we've seen change the trajectory of these viruses? I'll flag upfront that I'm working from the abstract here — the full body isn't yet rendered on bioRxiv — but the mechanism they describe is clear enough that even the abstract is worth walking through in detail.

Here is the context. When a virus replicates, its polymerase — the enzyme that copies the viral genome — makes occasional errors. It substitutes one nucleotide for another. That is the primary source of the point mutations that drive most of the evolution we care about, including the amino acid substitutions in SARS cov two spike that let each new variant escape antibodies. That mechanism is well understood.

What is not well understood is where insertion mutations come from. Insertions are qualitatively different from point substitutions. Instead of one letter changing, you drop a chunk of new sequence — sometimes a few nucleotides, sometimes twelve or fifteen — into the middle of an existing gene. Because that chunk shifts everything downstream by however many letters it added, insertions often break the gene entirely — but occasionally they do the opposite. Occasionally they create something new and functionally advantageous. And the one everybody thinks about is the furin cleavage site at the S1-S2 junction of the SARS cov two spike protein.

Let me explain why that particular site matters. The spike protein of a coronavirus is a single, elongated protein that has to get cut into two pieces before it can fuse the virus to a human cell. Those two pieces are called S1 and S2 — S1 does the binding to the receptor, S2 does the actual membrane fusion. Where the cut happens, and by which host enzyme, controls a lot about how the virus behaves — which tissues it can infect, how fast it can spread, how transmissible it is. The most closely related coronaviruses to SARS cov two — the ones found in bats and pangolins — have to be cut at that junction by a protease called TMPRSS two, and that protease is enriched in the lower respiratory tract. SARS cov two has an extra four amino acids inserted right at that junction — proline, arginine, arginine, alanine — and those four amino acids create a recognition sequence for a completely different, much more widely expressed enzyme called furin. Furin is everywhere, including the upper airway. That insertion is why SARS cov two can efficiently infect the upper respiratory tract, why it spreads before symptoms, and, most people would say, a substantial part of why the pandemic happened. It is not present in the immediate bat and pangolin relatives.

Where that four-amino-acid insertion came from has been contested for the entire pandemic. What Bieniasz's group did is set up an experimental system to actually observe the process by which coronaviruses generate insertions in the lab. They built a version of a common cold coronavirus — HCoV OC forty three — that fluorescently reports whenever a rare insertion mutation happens. Then they screened for viral proteins whose activity was required to produce those insertions.

The answer they landed on is a coronavirus enzyme called NSP fifteen. NSP fifteen is a nuclease — an enzyme that cuts RNA — and it's encoded by every coronavirus. It has been known for years, and it's understood to be involved in dampening the host innate immune response by cleaving certain RNA structures that would otherwise trigger antiviral signaling. What is new in this preprint is the finding that NSP fifteen also drives a specific mutational process. When it cuts the viral RNA, the little oligonucleotides that fall out — short RNA fragments a handful of nucleotides long — can be reinserted at distant locations elsewhere in the genome during the next round of replication. It is, essentially, a cut-and-paste mutagenesis machine that the virus has been carrying around all along.

They confirmed this pattern in both HCoV OC forty three and SARS cov two, by ultra-deep sequencing of viral populations and hunting for insertion mutants. When they analyzed thousands of insertion mutants, the sequences of the inserts matched fragments elsewhere in the same viral genome — exactly what you would expect if NSP fifteen were doing the cutting and the polymerase were doing the pasting. Insertions in these two coronaviruses were common enough — more than one per thousand genomes — that in any real-world viral population of the size that circulates in nature, they would be happening constantly.

And then the payoff. They looked, in that same background of insertion mutants, for the specific class of insertions that would create or replace a furin cleavage site. They found many examples of exactly that — coronaviruses, replicating normally, generating furin cleavage site inserts as a matter of routine. Which is to say: the specific mutation that made SARS cov two the virus we know is not an exotic or unusual event. It is the kind of thing that an NSP fifteen nuclease and a normal replicative polymerase will produce, on their own, in any coronavirus population big enough for the roll of the dice to come up right.

Why this matters. Three reasons. First, it identifies a specific mechanism — a specific enzyme, a specific mutational process — that generates the class of mutations that has produced the most consequential single change in this pandemic virus. That's satisfying science on its own. Second, because NSP fifteen is conserved across all coronaviruses, this same mechanism is presumably operating in every coronavirus circulating in every animal reservoir right now — including the SARS-related coronaviruses in bats and the MERS-related coronaviruses in camels. Furin cleavage site acquisition, when it happens, is a natural, mechanistically explicable event in the ordinary life of a coronavirus. That is a real update to how we should think about pandemic risk. Third, and this is a point the authors are careful about, it directly reframes the "how did SARS cov two get its furin cleavage site" debate, because we now have a fully natural, fully mechanistic explanation for that specific class of insertion. It does not by itself resolve every origins question — but it removes one significant piece of scaffolding from the argument that this insertion was extraordinary.

On the business side.

Two Phase three biotech readouts landed in the last twenty-four hours, and they are both worth understanding in some depth. I'll take the biggest one first.

Merck and Moderna announced this morning positive topline data from a Phase three trial called INTerpath dash zero zero one, which tested Moderna's personalized mRNA cancer vaccine — the drug you may know as mRNA dash forty one fifty seven, now given the generic name intismeran autogene — in combination with Keytruda, Merck's flagship anti-PD-one immunotherapy, versus Keytruda alone, in patients with completely resected stage two B, two C, three, or four melanoma. Eleven hundred and thirty seven patients randomized two-to-one to the combination arm. The trial hit its primary endpoint of recurrence-free survival — the time from surgery to melanoma coming back — and also hit its key secondary endpoint of distant metastasis-free survival, at a pre-specified interim analysis. No new safety signals. The specific hazard ratios haven't been released yet — those will come at a medical meeting — but for context, the Phase two version of this trial, KEYNOTE nine forty two, showed a forty-nine percent reduction in the risk of recurrence and a fifty-nine percent reduction in the risk of distant metastasis, so we know the effect size in the Phase two was very large.

Let me explain why this is a genuinely landmark result, and then why the mechanism is interesting.

Here's the significance. The idea of a personalized cancer vaccine — a vaccine whose contents are custom-manufactured for each patient's individual tumor — has been the north star of a specific line of cancer immunotherapy research for at least fifteen years. Companies have tried this over and over with different platforms — peptide vaccines, DNA vaccines, dendritic cell vaccines — and the Phase three data has never cleanly worked. INTerpath dash zero zero one is the first Phase three trial of a personalized neoantigen vaccine ever to hit its primary endpoint. If it holds up on peer review, it is a template for a whole new modality.

The mechanism. When a tumor is surgically removed, that tumor is genetically unique to the patient — it carries a specific catalog of mutations, tens to hundreds of them, that arose over the tumor's evolution. Most of those mutations are junk from the immune system's perspective, but a subset generate short protein fragments — peptides — that look different from anything the person's healthy cells produce. Those are called neoantigens, and they are, in principle, the ideal target for the immune system, because there is no healthy tissue that carries them. The catch is that different patients' tumors have completely different neoantigens.

The intismeran autogene approach is to take the tumor after surgery, sequence its DNA, computationally identify the roughly thirty most promising neoantigen candidates based on which peptides will be presented well on the patient's own major histocompatibility complex molecules, then synthesize a single mRNA molecule that encodes up to thirty-four of those neoantigens strung together, package it into a lipid nanoparticle, and inject it back into the patient about eight to nine weeks after surgery. The patient's cells translate the mRNA, present the encoded neoantigens on their surface, and — the theory goes — recruit T cells specific for those exact tumor-mutation-derived peptides. When any residual tumor cells anywhere in the body express the same mutated proteins, those primed T cells recognize and kill them.

Keytruda's job in the combination is to release the brakes on those T cells so they can actually do the work. This is why the combination is expected to outperform Keytruda alone — the vaccine expands and directs the T cell repertoire, and Keytruda keeps that expanded repertoire from being suppressed.

Why it matters. Two reasons. First, high-risk resected melanoma — patients whose tumor was successfully removed but who are at real risk of relapse — is a population where the current standard is a year of adjuvant PD-one blockade, and the recurrence rate is still substantial. A therapy that shifts both recurrence-free and distant metastasis-free survival on top of that is a meaningful clinical improvement. Second, and this is the bigger point, if the personalized neoantigen mRNA vaccine works cleanly here, it opens the door for the same platform in other resectable cancers where you can get tumor tissue and sequence it — pancreatic, non-small-cell lung, kidney, urothelial. Moderna and Merck have Phase three studies running in several of those already. If the melanoma result generalizes, the "post-op infusion visit" is going to look very different across a lot of oncology in the next few years.

The other biotech readout, briefly.

Amylyx yesterday reported positive Phase three data on a drug called avexitide in a condition called post-bariatric hypoglycemia. Post-bariatric hypoglycemia — usually shortened to PBH — is a condition that affects about eight percent of people who have had bariatric surgery, especially Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. When people with this condition eat a meal, particularly a carbohydrate-containing meal, their gut releases exaggerated amounts of a hormone called GLP one — glucagon-like peptide one, the same hormone the Ozempic-class drugs mimic. In normal physiology, GLP one signals the pancreas to release insulin, and everything balances out. In PBH, the GLP one release is so extreme that the pancreas releases far too much insulin, and then blood sugar collapses. People with this condition can develop severe hypoglycemia — confusion, cognitive impairment, unconsciousness, seizures — sometimes multiple times a week. There is no approved therapy for it today.

Avexitide is a GLP one receptor antagonist. Instead of mimicking GLP one, which the Ozempic-class drugs do, it competitively blocks the GLP one receptor on the pancreatic beta cell. It sits in the receptor without activating it, so the exaggerated meal-triggered insulin release is dampened. The Phase three trial, called LUCIDITY, was small — seventy-eight patients — but the effect was striking. Fifty-five percent reduction, versus placebo, in the composite rate of clinically important hypoglycemic events, with a p value of three times ten to the minus six. Every secondary endpoint hit. Safety was clean, no serious adverse events attributable to the drug. Amylyx says they will file an NDA with the FDA by the end of this year.

Why it matters. This is a mechanism-of-action-appropriate drug for a mechanism-defined disease with no current therapy. Also, it's worth noting that the GLP one antagonist idea is a relatively lightly-explored corner of a receptor that everybody else in the field has been agonizing on. Blocking a GLP one receptor is the therapeutic opposite of what Ozempic does, but for a condition that is essentially GLP one signaling turned to eleven, that's exactly the right direction to move it in.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A group at Zhejiang University and Tencent posted a preprint on Monday about a system they call PertMind. It's a nice, concrete example of a bigger idea about where the "biological reasoning" capabilities of large language models can come from — and I think it's worth unpacking because the trick is generalizable.

Here's the context. When you talk to a state-of-the-art large language model about biology, it can produce fluent, mechanistically-plausible-sounding text — but if you actually stress it on a specific gene, a specific pathway, a specific perturbation, it often gets the answer wrong or wanders. The natural fix is to fine-tune the model with curated examples of good biological reasoning. But building a curated dataset of thousands of correct, mechanistic biology answers is enormously labor-intensive — you need domain experts to write the answers, and it doesn't scale.

What the PertMind authors ask is: what if, instead of asking humans to hand-curate reasoning traces, you used existing cellular perturbation experiments as the reward signal for reinforcement learning? A perturbation experiment is a fairly well-defined thing: you take a cell line, you knock out or activate a specific gene, and you measure what happens to the expression of every other gene in that cell — thousands of measurements per perturbation, from RNA sequencing. Across many labs, there are now atlases of hundreds of thousands of such perturbations. The result of any given perturbation is not a matter of expert opinion; it is a measurement. That means it is, in the machine-learning sense, a computable reward — you can ask a language model "what happens to gene expression if you knock down gene X in cell type Y" and check its answer against the actual measurement.

The PertMind pipeline does exactly that. They first do a supervised warm-up phase, where the model is initialized on a set of trusted reasoning trajectories. Then they switch to reinforcement learning, with three levels of reward. The first is at the individual gene level: is the model's predicted change for gene A close to what the experiment measured? The second is at the pathway level: is the model getting the broader shape of the response right — turning up the right pathways, turning down the right pathways? The third is a format reward, which just makes sure the model is producing well-structured outputs the rest of the pipeline can parse. The system, called GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization — is the reinforcement learning workhorse behind a lot of modern reasoning models.

The result they report is that a model trained only on forward-direction perturbation prediction — "here's the perturbation, predict the response" — transfers, without any additional task-specific fine-tuning, to a bunch of related tasks. It can do the reverse: given the response, identify the perturbation. It can reason about combinations of perturbations. It can prioritize hits from phenotypic screens. It can produce embeddings — biological profiles — that are competitive on a variety of downstream cell-biology tasks.

Why this matters, beyond the specific model. This is a template that generalizes. Any biomedical domain where the ground truth is measured, not annotated, is a candidate for the same trick — mass spectrometry proteomics, chemical screening, patient-outcome data from EHRs. If the field can systematically convert measurement into reward, and use existing experimental atlases as the training environment, then we do not need armies of PhDs sitting at keyboards writing reasoning traces to make LLMs better at biology. It's the same intuition that made AlphaGo different from earlier chess engines — reward from the game itself, not from hand-labeled human moves. The PertMind team has released the code and the trained model publicly; I would expect a lot of follow-on work along these lines over the next twelve months.

And finally, animals.

A research team led out of the University of Helsinki, with a postdoc named Oscar Wilson as the first author, published a paper this month in the Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology arguing that in Middle Miocene South America — roughly ten to sixteen million years ago — the animal at the top of the food chain was not a mammal, and not a bird. It was a giant crocodilian.

Here is the setting. During the Middle Miocene, a large swath of northern South America — including much of what is now the Amazon basin — was covered by an enormous inland freshwater lake system known as the Pebas mega-wetland. Around it walked one of the most striking predator assemblages in the fossil record: sabertoothed marsupials, terror birds, giant flightless carnivores, and, in the water, a caiman relative called Purussaurus neivensis. Purussaurus was seven meters long — twenty-three feet — and weighed around eighteen hundred kilograms. For scale, a large adult Nile crocodile today is around five meters and eight hundred kilograms. It is genuinely one of the largest crocodilians ever to have existed.

For a long time, the assumption in the field has been that the giant crocodilians were around, but that the mammalian predators — the sabertoothed marsupial Thylacosmilus, in particular — were the primary killers of the big mammalian herbivores that also lived there. The Helsinki team went at that assumption in a very direct way. Bite marks and tooth marks on herbivore bones are the single most direct evidence you have of a predator having actually killed and eaten a specific prey animal. So they went through the Miocene fossil collections and counted bite marks on ungulate — hoofed mammal — fossils from Colombia, dating between ten and a half and sixteen million years ago. What they found is that the majority of the diagnostic bite marks are consistent with Purussaurus, not with the mammalian predators.

Their read is that there was so much herbivore prey available that the mammalian predators just could not have eaten it all — the giant crocodilians, waiting at the water's edge as the big herbivores came down to drink, were doing most of the actual work at the top of the food chain. Which is a striking mental image, and, I think, a useful reminder that the modern intuition — mammals on top, reptiles subordinate — is a very recent inheritance in evolutionary time. For most of the Cenozoic era in most of the world, that was not remotely true.
