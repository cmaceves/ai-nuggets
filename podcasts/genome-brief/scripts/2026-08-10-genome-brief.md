## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Monday, August tenth, twenty twenty six. Today we look at a study out of Johns Hopkins that finds antibody evidence of H five N one exposure not just in poultry and swine workers, but in their neighbors — people who live near industrial livestock operations in North Carolina without ever setting foot in one. Then, on the business side, a new heart drug developer just banked a very large I P O to challenge Bristol Myers Squibb's blockbuster Camzyos. In A I for biology, a clever little trick that gets AlphaFold three and Boltz two to actually show you the alternative shapes a protein can take, instead of the one confident answer they usually return. And to close: what a C T scan of one of only two complete dodo skulls in the world tells us about how the bird actually experienced the world before it went extinct.

Let's get into it.

Pathogen watch, first. A team led by Nora Pisanic at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, working with Andrew Pekosz and the Center for Health Security's Gigi Gronvall, posted a new medRxiv preprint this morning that quietly matters a great deal. The question they went after is simple to state, and it has been sitting unanswered in the middle of the H five N one dairy-cattle situation: living near intensive livestock operations — not working in them, just living near them — does that raise your risk of being exposed to zoonotic flu? A quick note here: I'm going off the abstract for this one, as the preprint just hit the server this morning and the full text hasn't rendered yet. But the numbers in the abstract are enough to walk you through what they found.

First, the setup. H five N one — that's H five N one highly pathogenic avian influenza — is the same virus that's been circulating in U S dairy cattle since twenty twenty four, jumping into farm workers here and there, and giving public-health authorities heartburn about a possible mammalian-adapted strain. North Carolina is an interesting place to ask this question because poultry and swine production are extremely concentrated there, often geographically layered on top of each other, and the samples in this study go back to twenty twenty one through twenty twenty two — so this is baseline data from before the dairy outbreak, essentially asking the pre-existing question of "how leaky are these facilities under normal conditions?"

The method is elegant. Rather than drawing blood, they used saliva samples, which are painless, easy to collect at scale, and give you mucosal antibodies — the antibodies that sit at your mouth and nose and airway lining, which is exactly where a respiratory virus would first encounter you. They measured immunoglobulin A and immunoglobulin G against the hemagglutinin protein — the H in H five N one — for three flu subtypes: H five N one, seasonal H one N one, and seasonal H three N two. Three groups were compared: workers at industrial livestock operations, their neighbors, and people from metro areas as controls. They also — and this is the clever bit — measured a swine-specific fecal contamination marker called Pig-two-Bac D N A on household surfaces, which is basically a molecular test for "has swine waste physically ended up in your house?"

Here's the finding. Anti-H five mucosal I g A was elevated with every axis of exposure they looked at. Workers had more of it than metro controls. Neighbors — again, no occupational livestock contact — had more of it than metro controls. And most strikingly, the more concentrated the poultry and swine production in your zip code, the more anti-H five I g A they measured in your saliva. Every ten-fold increase in poultry production intensity was associated with a measurable bump in anti-H five antibodies, and the swine metric moved in the same direction. And when Pig-two-Bac D N A was detectable on your household surfaces — when swine waste was, physically, in your home environment — the antibody signal jumped again on top of that.

So why does this matter? A few reasons. One, antibodies in the mucosa are a footprint of exposure to the actual antigen. That doesn't necessarily mean full infection — you can get mucosal I g A from repeated low-dose exposure to viral protein without a productive infection — but it means the virus is getting close enough to people to be seen by their immune system. Two, it tells you the exposure surface for H five N one isn't just the occupational cohort we've been focused on. The community around these facilities — people who don't have farm jobs, who have not agreed to be biological monitors of an industry — is being lightly, chronically exposed. And three, it hands surveillance a lever: you can look for mucosal I g A in saliva from community members and get an ambient readout of what's leaking off of nearby operations. That's a tool that could, in principle, detect a shift in exposure risk before you see cases in the E R.

The caveats. This is a nineteen-month window, twenty twenty one to twenty twenty two, before the current dairy H five N one wave took hold. Cross-reactivity between H five and seasonal H one hemagglutinin can happen, and the abstract flags that both moved together, which they read as evidence of shared exposure rather than pure H five specificity. And "elevated antibodies" is not "infection," let alone "onward transmission." But as a piece of the surveillance picture, the fact that the intensity of nearby livestock production shows up in the neighbor's saliva is a striking demonstration that the boundary between an industrial facility and the community around it, for a virus, is a lot more porous than the property line.

On the business side. On Wednesday last week, a start-up called Braveheart Bio priced its I P O on the Nasdaq at eighteen dollars a share, twenty one point two million shares, for a total raise of three hundred eighty two and a half million dollars. Ticker is B R V E. Braveheart is a two-year-old Cambridge biotech, founded by former H I dash Bio executives, and it exists to develop one drug: B H B one eight nine three, an oral cardiac myosin inhibitor for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Let me unpack what any of that means. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — H C M — is a genetic heart-muscle disease. The heart muscle, the myocardium, thickens abnormally, most often the wall between the two ventricles, which then obstructs the outflow of blood from the left ventricle. You get shortness of breath, chest pain, and sometimes sudden cardiac death in young athletes — H C M is the classic cause of that "collapsed on the basketball court" scenario. The underlying biology is that the sarcomere — the little contractile motor inside each heart muscle cell — is hyperactive, generating too much force, and the muscle remodels itself in response. So you fix it by dialing the motor down.

Cardiac myosin inhibitors do exactly that. Myosin is the protein that pulls on actin to generate contraction; if you inhibit it partially, you reduce hypercontractility without stopping the heart. Bristol Myers Squibb's Camzyos — the generic name is mavacamten — was the first drug approved in this class, back in twenty twenty two. Cytokinetics has a competitor called aficamten, sold as Myqorzo. Both are huge commercial stories: pill-for-life chronic disease, on a genetic condition that has been under-diagnosed and now has good testing. Braveheart is arguing it can beat both of them with better pharmacokinetics — a simpler dosing regimen and a wider therapeutic window, without the black-box safety monitoring that Camzyos has to carry because of its potential to reduce ejection fraction too far.

The drug itself, B H B one eight nine three, isn't home-grown; it was licensed from Jiangsu Hengrui in China, which is running Phase three trials in the obstructive form of H C M there. Braveheart's global Phase threes — in both obstructive and non-obstructive H C M — start later this year and into early twenty twenty seven. The interesting business context is that Braveheart is the seventeenth biotech I P O of twenty twenty six, in a year that has now blown past the last several years combined on I P O count and dollar volume. This is a real, structural reopening of the biotech capital markets, not a one-off, and it's happening on very late-stage clinical assets — meaning the market is paying for de-risked programs that just need money to get to a Phase three readout. Braveheart with a China-licensed asset is a template we're going to keep seeing.

Now, A I in biomedicine. A short but very elegant methods paper on bioRxiv from Shosuke Suzuki and Toshiyuki Amagasa at the University of Tsukuba in Japan — the third version dropped yesterday. Again, this is abstract-only for now — the body hasn't rendered — but the abstract lays out the idea cleanly enough to explain.

The problem they're solving. Deep-learning protein structure predictors — AlphaFold three from DeepMind, and Boltz two, the open successor from the Mohammed Bin Zayed group and collaborators — will, given an amino acid sequence, return a single high-confidence three-D structure. That's phenomenally useful. But real proteins aren't a single structure. Enzymes flex between open and closed states. Membrane transporters cycle between conformations facing inside and outside the cell. Ion channels open and close. If your model only ever hands you one shape, you're missing biology that matters for how the protein actually works — and for how you'd design a drug against it.

Existing tricks for extracting more than one conformation from these models are heavy: run inference many times with perturbed multiple sequence alignments, or with subsampled sequences, or with different random seeds — expensive, and mostly heuristic. Suzuki and Amagasa's proposal is startlingly simple. Inside AlphaFold three and Boltz two, there's an internal representation of the protein called the pair representation — think of it as a big grid where each cell says "how confident am I that these two residues are near each other in three-D space?" Their idea is: at inference time, right before the network's core reasoning module — the Pairformer — reads that grid, multiply every entry by a single scalar number. That's it. One knob. No retraining, no second network, no repeated forward passes.

When they turn that scalar down, the model becomes less certain about its own encoded distance information and is willing to sample structures further from its default answer. Across eighty six proteins with two known biological states — including domain-motion enzymes and membrane transporters — this simple scaling broadens the conformational ensemble both models produce, and it recovers the alternative state that default inference misses. The effect is largest in AlphaFold three, and it works even on proteins deposited after the training cutoff, which rules out a pure memorization explanation. It approaches — not beats, but approaches — the recovery you'd get with the expensive alignment-based sampling methods, at a tiny fraction of the compute.

Why should you care? Because for structure-based drug design and mechanistic biology, "give me the other state" is one of the most valuable questions you can ask a structure predictor, and this makes it a one-line change. And conceptually, it's a nice reminder that these models are not oracles reading structure off amino acids — they are learned probability distributions over structures, and if you know where the confidence lives inside the network, you can tune it. The pair representation is where the model stores its bet, and turning down the volume on that bet is enough to let the alternative state come out.

And finally — animals. If you have ever heard the word "dodo," it has probably come attached to the phrase "as stupid as a." A new paper in the Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society, out this month, is here to gently correct the record. A team led by the University of Lethbridge in Canada, working with the Natural History Museum of Denmark, took high-resolution C T scans of three dodo skulls — including the only two complete dodo skulls in the world, one at the Natural History Museum in Copenhagen and one at Oxford's University Museum of Natural History. From those scans they digitally reconstructed the brain cavity — the endocast, which is the imprint the brain leaves on the inside of the skull — and compared it to modern pigeons and doves, which are the dodo's closest living relatives.

Two things fall out. First: no evidence of diminished cognition. Relative brain size and the organization of the different processing regions look about as you'd expect from a pigeon. So the "stupid" story is, at minimum, unsupported by the endocast. Second, and more interesting: the dodo appears to have had an enhanced olfactory system compared to modern pigeons, and a large, tactilely sensitive upper beak — the kind of enlarged olfactory bulbs and beak-nerve infrastructure that suggests it was hunting for food by smell and touch, not just vision. And the visual regions of the brain suggest it may have been active at dawn and dusk, not just in broad daylight. In other words: a pigeon-descended, flightless island bird that had leaned into smelling out fallen fruit and rooting through leaf litter, at low light — a specialization, not a deficit.

The story most people know — that dodos were dumb, tame, and just walked up to sailors to be eaten — is basically an artifact of first-contact accounts written by people who had no idea what they were looking at. The endocast tells a different one, which is that the dodo was a highly specialized foraging pigeon on an island with no predators, and the traits that got it killed were adaptations to a world that no longer existed the moment humans stepped onto Mauritius.
