## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Saturday, August eighth, twenty-twenty-six. Four items today. First, on pathogen watch — a preprint out this week on bioRxiv from Raiees Andrabi and Dennis Burton at Scripps, with Ralph Baric at U-N-C, Drew Weissman at Penn, and Darrell Irvine at M-I-T, reports the first non-human primate proof-of-concept for a pan-betacoronavirus vaccine. The idea is to focus the immune response on a small conserved patch on the spike protein that essentially every human-relevant coronavirus shares — and the vaccinated monkeys ended up making antibodies that neutralize SARS cov two, its variants, other bat sarbecoviruses, and MERS cov, and are protected in-vivo against both SARS cov two and MERS. Second, on the business side — the F-D-A this week granted accelerated approval to Replimune's Tudriqev, an oncolytic herpes virus, in combination with the P-D-1 blocker nivolumab, for patients with advanced melanoma who have already failed a checkpoint inhibitor. It is the first oncolytic viral therapy approved in the U-S in a decade, and it happened on the third try — the F-D-A had rejected it twice before, in July twenty-twenty-five and again this past April. Third, on AI in biomedicine — a preprint out this week from a group at Northwestern Polytechnical University in China introduces a tool called Vir3D that uses ESMFold-derived three-dimensional structures of the flu hemagglutinin protein to predict, from sequence alone, how antigenically similar two flu strains are. In a ten-year retrospective replay, its picks for the vaccine strain gave better coverage of what actually circulated than the World Health Organization's picks did. And crucially, applied to the H five N one currently sweeping through U-S dairy cattle, it predicts that the cattle virus is still antigenically close enough to the existing clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b vaccine strains that those stockpiled vaccines should work — and they wet-lab confirmed that prediction. And finally, animals — Denmark's Natural History Museum has quietly rediscovered a set of eleven-million-year-old Megalodon vertebrae that had been thought destroyed thirty-seven years ago, and the biggest one is the largest Megalodon vertebra ever measured. On with the show.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Coronaviruses have caused three major human outbreaks in twenty-three years — SARS in two-thousand-two, MERS starting in twenty-twelve, and SARS cov two in twenty-nineteen — and all three came from the same family, called *betacoronaviruses*. Bats are known to carry a huge reservoir of related viruses, so the underlying question every pandemic preparedness program has been asking since twenty-twenty is: can we make a single vaccine now that would work against whatever betacoronavirus jumps into humans next?

The obstacle is that current coronavirus vaccines are targeted at the tip of the spike protein — a region called the *receptor-binding domain*, or R-B-D. R-B-D is the part of the spike that grabs onto the receptor on a human cell and lets the virus get inside. It is the most exposed, most immunogenic part of the spike, which is why antibody responses are naturally dominated by it. But it is also the most variable part — different coronaviruses use different receptors, and even within SARS cov two the R-B-D drifts rapidly, which is why we have needed variant-updated boosters every year. So R-B-D-focused vaccines are highly effective but narrowly specific to one virus at a time.

The paper posted this week on bioRxiv, from Raiees Andrabi and Dennis Burton at Scripps with a large collaboration that includes Ralph Baric at U-N-C, Drew Weissman at Penn, Andrew Ward at Scripps, and Darrell Irvine at M-I-T, takes a different anatomical target: a short segment further down the spike, in the stalk region called *S-two*, and specifically a piece called the *stem-helix*. S-two is the machinery the virus uses after binding, to fuse its membrane with the human cell membrane — the actual entry step. And that fusion machinery has to be structurally and mechanically conserved across coronaviruses, so parts of it have been under selective pressure for a long time to stay the same. The stem-helix in particular is virtually identical across sarbecoviruses — the group that includes SARS cov two and the original SARS — merbecoviruses, which includes MERS cov, and embecoviruses, which includes the common-cold coronaviruses O-C forty-three and H-K-U one. If you can get antibodies that recognize that patch, in principle those antibodies neutralize any betacoronavirus.

The reason nobody has done this before is that the stem-helix is *occluded*, meaning it is buried underneath other parts of the spike on the intact virus and the immune system essentially doesn't see it during natural infection. Rare individuals do sometimes make antibodies against it, but the response is quiet. The Burton lab has been working for years on how to design an immunogen that would rip that patch out of its normal context, present it to the immune system as though it were a stand-alone target, and get a strong focused response.

Their approach in this paper is to build what is called an *epitope-focused nanoparticle immunogen*. Imagine a small, self-assembling protein sphere — a nanoparticle — with many copies of just the stem-helix segment displayed on its outer surface. There is no R-B-D anywhere on it. The immune system sees only the stem-helix, so the antibodies it makes are forced to target that one patch. They then immunize rhesus macaques with this nanoparticle, either alone or followed by a boost of an mRNA vaccine encoding the full BA-point-one SARS cov two spike — this is where Drew Weissman's group comes in, since he shared the Nobel with Katalin Karikó for the mRNA vaccine platform.

Here is what the vaccinated macaques do. Their sera contain antibodies that cross-react against sarbecoviruses, merbecoviruses, and embecoviruses — that is, the whole betacoronavirus family, not just SARS cov two. Those sera neutralize SARS cov two itself, multiple SARS cov two variants, additional bat sarbecoviruses that have never infected humans but are considered pandemic-risk candidates, and MERS cov, which is a genuinely different virus. When they clone out individual monoclonal antibodies from these animals, the antibodies show broad in-vitro neutralization. And, most importantly, when they challenge the vaccinated animals in vivo — actually infect them — with SARS cov two and separately with MERS cov, both infections are controlled. One vaccine, protection against two viruses from different subgenera of coronavirus that don't even share a receptor.

They also solve the structures of the antibodies from the monkeys and compare them to the rare human stem-helix broadly neutralizing antibodies people have found in COVID convalescent patients. The two are structurally very similar — same binding geometry, same key contacts — which suggests the human immune system, given the right immunogen, would make the same kind of antibody the monkeys did. That is the translational bridge.

Two things worth taking from this. First, going off the abstract here — the full preprint text is not yet rendered on bioRxiv so I have not read the wet-lab detail in full. But even at the abstract level, this is one of the more concrete pan-coronavirus vaccine results anyone has shown. The design principle — take a conserved but hidden patch, build an immunogen that presents it out of context, get a focused response — is the same logic Bill Schief's group at Scripps has been using with the germline-targeting H-I-V vaccine, and this is the coronavirus version of the same playbook. Second, the practical stake: right now, the entire coronavirus vaccine strategy is reactive. A new one shows up, you sequence the R-B-D, you update the mRNA, you ship a new booster. Under this paradigm, you would have something already licensed and stockpiled that covers whatever betacoronavirus comes out of the bat reservoir next. That is not a next-flu-season problem — it is a next-pandemic problem, and this is the first primate data that says it might actually be reachable.

On the business side.

On August sixth, the F-D-A granted accelerated approval to Replimune's Tudriqev — chemical name vusolimogene oderparepvec, previously known in trials as R-P one — in combination with the P-D-1 inhibitor nivolumab, for adults with unresectable advanced cutaneous melanoma whose disease has progressed on a prior anti-P-D-1 regimen. It is the first oncolytic viral therapy approved in the United States in ten years — the previous one was Amgen's Imlygic, approved in twenty-fifteen — and the approval story itself is unusual: the F-D-A had rejected this same product twice already, in July twenty-twenty-five and again in April twenty-twenty-six.

Let me define what an *oncolytic virus* is, because it is not the same category of drug as anything else in oncology. Tudriqev is a genetically engineered herpes simplex virus type one — the same virus that causes cold sores — that has been modified in two important ways. First, its ability to replicate in normal cells has been disabled so it only replicates efficiently inside tumor cells. Second, it has been engineered to carry two extra genes: one encoding *G-M-C-S-F*, an immune-signaling molecule that recruits and matures antigen-presenting cells; and one encoding a protein called *GALV fusogenic glycoprotein*, from gibbon ape leukemia virus, that makes infected tumor cells fuse with their neighbors and effectively syncytize before dying. You inject the modified virus directly into a visible tumor. The virus preferentially replicates inside the tumor cells, lyses them from the inside, and as they die they release both tumor antigens and G-M-C-S-F into the local tissue. That combination is designed to convert a "cold" tumor — one the immune system is ignoring — into a "hot" one that the immune system attacks systemically, even at distant, un-injected sites. The addition of nivolumab, an anti-P-D-1 checkpoint inhibitor, releases the brakes on that immune response.

The efficacy data come from a single-arm Phase two trial called IGNYTE. One hundred forty patients with stage three-B, three-C, or four advanced cutaneous melanoma, all of whom had progressed on prior P-D-1 blockade for at least eight weeks. In the ninety-one-patient efficacy-evaluable subgroup — patients with at least one non-injected lesion so that the readout captures the systemic effect, not just what happens to the injected tumor — the combination produced an objective response rate of twenty-four-point-two percent by independent central review, with a median duration of response of fourteen-point-one months, a complete-response rate of fifteen percent in the broader cohort, and a three-year overall survival of forty-seven-point-eight percent overall — eighty-three-and-a-half percent among responders. For a population that had already failed the standard of care, those are meaningful numbers.

The regulatory back-and-forth is the part worth understanding. The F-D-A's original objections in July twenty-twenty-five and again in April twenty-twenty-six were not primarily about the efficacy numbers — they were about the trial's *design*. IGNYTE is a single-arm study: everyone got Tudriqev plus nivolumab. There was no arm that got nivolumab alone. The agency's concern was that nivolumab has known, if modest, activity in post-P-D-1 melanoma on its own — some patients respond to a rechallenge — so a single-arm study cannot cleanly attribute the twenty-four percent response rate to R-P one versus to nivolumab alone. In April, that concern was enough to reject the drug again despite a positive advisory committee vote. On July thirtieth of this year, a new adcom voted ten to three in favor of the data as clinically meaningful for an unmet need, and F-D-A accepted the argument on the third submission. The catch — this is an *accelerated* approval, meaning Replimune must run a confirmatory randomized Phase three trial, called IGNYTE dash three, of about four hundred patients, with data due around twenty-thirty. If IGNYTE dash three misses, the accelerated approval can be withdrawn.

Two implications. First, this is a partial validation of oncolytic viral therapy as a platform. Imlygic — the twenty-fifteen approval — never took off commercially despite being technically approved, largely because its response numbers as monotherapy were modest and it required repeated intratumoral injection. Tudriqev is different because it is explicitly combined with a checkpoint inhibitor and shows durable responses in a population that has run out of options. If it holds up in Phase three, several other companies with pipeline oncolytic viruses — including Turnstone Biologics, Candel, and Oncorus — get more oxygen. Second, this is a case of the F-D-A adjusting to what "adequate evidence" means for a niche indication where a randomized trial arm is hard to construct and the alternative is nothing at all. Rare-disease and post-standard-of-care oncology developers will read this approval carefully.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A preprint posted this week on bioRxiv from a group at Northwestern Polytechnical University in China describes a tool called Vir3D that takes a swing at one of the most consequential prediction problems in public health: which strains of influenza to put into the next season's vaccine.

Some context on why this matters. Influenza A viruses — the H-one-N-one, H-three-N-two, and various avian subtypes like H five N one — undergo continuous small mutations in their surface proteins, a process called *antigenic drift*. The main immune target is a surface protein called *hemagglutinin*, or H-A, which is what the virus uses to bind sialic acid receptors on your airway cells. Antibodies from prior infection or vaccination recognize specific three-dimensional patches on the H-A surface. When the H-A drifts enough — mutations accumulate in those patches — antibodies from last year's exposure no longer bind well, and you can be reinfected. This is why the flu vaccine is reformulated every year.

The specific decision every February is: given the H-A sequences of the strains circulating right now, which one should we put into the vaccine for next winter? The World Health Organization convenes a consultation and picks strains based on hemagglutination inhibition assays — a lab test where you take patient sera and see whether antibodies against candidate strain A also block strain B. It is empirical, laborious, and always months out of date because you have to wait for the wet-lab data to come in. Sequence-only prediction methods exist — they look at the amino acid changes in H-A and predict antigenic distance from primary sequence — but they miss something important: antigenicity is not really a function of which residues are different. It is a function of which residues are different *and exposed on the three-dimensional surface where antibodies can reach*. A mutation deep inside the folded protein might change nothing antigenically; a mutation on the surface at an antibody-binding site might change everything.

Vir3D's contribution is to plug in a modern structure predictor — ESMFold, which is Meta's protein-language-model-based structure prediction system — as an intermediate step. From the raw H-A sequence of a candidate strain, ESMFold builds the predicted three-dimensional structure; Vir3D then reads structural features off that predicted fold — where residues sit in three-space, which residues are near each other on the surface, which changes are in exposed antibody-recognition regions — and uses those features to predict pairwise antigenic distances between any two H-A variants. That is the core methodological move: don't predict antigenicity from sequence, predict it from *structure inferred from sequence*.

The retrospective test is the part that makes this concrete. The authors run Vir3D over a decade of historical H-A sequences and, at each vaccine-selection moment, ask: which strain would Vir3D have chosen for next season's vaccine, and how well does its choice cover what actually circulated? They report that Vir3D's picks consistently give broader antigenic coverage than the strains W-H-O actually selected — in other words, in a replay of the last ten years, this tool would have made better calls than the human consensus process did.

The forward test is the H five N one on U-S dairy cattle. That outbreak, which started in early twenty-twenty-four, is caused by a clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b H five N one. The public-health question is whether the H-A of the cattle virus has drifted far enough from the vaccine strains the U-S has stockpiled that those stockpiled vaccines wouldn't work if the virus goes pandemic. Vir3D predicted that the U-S cattle H five N one, designated TX-slash-twenty-four, has remained antigenically close to the clade two-point-three-point-four-four-b vaccine strains — meaning the stockpiled vaccine should still cover it. And then the group ran the actual wet-lab hemagglutination inhibition assay, and confirmed the prediction.

Two takeaways. First, going off the abstract — the full preprint text is not yet rendered on bioRxiv, so I have not gone through the ablations. The claim that structure-based prediction beats sequence-based prediction is plausible on general grounds, but the size of the win and the robustness across subtypes is exactly the kind of detail you want to see in the wet-lab section, and I would want to read the full paper before making strong claims. Second, and more broadly: this is a good example of what foundation models are actually starting to change in biomedicine. Not the direct "AI designs a new drug" story that gets the headlines, but the quieter shift in which a general-purpose protein structure model becomes a reusable feature extractor that a domain-specific prediction task can plug into. Vaccine strain selection has been an artisanal human process for seventy years. It might not stay that way.

And finally, animals.

At the Natural History Museum of Denmark this week, staff have quietly announced the rediscovery of several enormous Megalodon vertebrae that had been thought destroyed thirty-seven years ago.

Some background. *Otodus megalodon* — Megalodon for short — is an extinct shark that lived from roughly twenty-three million to three-and-a-half million years ago, and is the largest predatory fish that has ever lived. Almost everything we know about it comes from teeth, because sharks have cartilage skeletons that decay rather than bone that fossilizes, and teeth are essentially the only body part that survives in the fossil record. That is a real problem for size estimation. Extrapolating from teeth to total body length is genuinely difficult — different shark species have very different tooth-to-body-length ratios, and there is no living Megalodon to calibrate against. Body-length estimates from teeth for Megalodon range from about fifteen meters to more than twenty-four, with a lot of uncertainty in either direction.

Vertebrae are much better. In sharks, the vertebrae *are* mineralized enough to fossilize, and their diameter scales predictably with body length across species. If you have a big vertebra, you have a much sharper estimate of the animal's size than any tooth can give you.

The specimens in question originally came from the Upper Miocene Gram Formation in Denmark, and were kept at the Geological Museum in Copenhagen. In nineteen eighty-nine, that museum's collections were moved to the Museum of Southern Jutland, and paperwork at the time recorded that these vertebrae had been damaged or lost in the move. For thirty-seven years, they were treated in the paleontology literature as destroyed. In fact, it turns out, they had simply been placed on a shelf at what is now the Natural History Museum of Denmark and had sat there unnoticed.

The re-analysis, from a group led by Kenshu Shimada at DePaul University, along with researchers at Aarhus University and Australia's Western Australian Museum, published this week in the journal Palaeontologia Electronica, does what nobody has been able to do for these specimens before — measure them. The largest vertebra is twenty-three centimeters across, or about nine inches. That is the largest Megalodon vertebra ever measured. Scaling from the vertebral diameter to body length, that individual would have been at least twenty-four meters long — that is seventy-nine feet, longer than a semi-truck — and consistent with a lifespan approaching a century. And, because the vertebrae came from a single individual with growth-ring data, they can back-calculate the animal's birth size: newborn Megalodons were about three-point-six meters — twelve feet — which is larger than most adult great white sharks.

Two small things. First, this is a nice reminder that a lot of the highest-value paleontology work isn't done in the field. It is done in the collections. Museum specimens get catalogued and forgotten routinely, and the science of going back through drawers with modern imaging and re-measurement techniques is producing a steady stream of these rediscoveries. Second, the specific ecological picture that comes out — an apex predator that could exceed twenty-four meters, live nearly a hundred years, and be born larger than most adult sharks — really underscores how different the marine mammal food web of the Miocene was from anything living today. There is nothing in modern oceans that touches these numbers.
