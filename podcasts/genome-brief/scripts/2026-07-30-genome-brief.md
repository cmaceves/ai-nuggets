## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Thursday, July thirtieth, twenty twenty-six. Four items today. First, a mathematical-modeling paper out of Singapore, the UK, Toronto and Berkeley looks at the long-term consequences of using a single daily antibiotic pill to prevent sexually transmitted infections — and finds that on its own the strategy backfires, by selecting for the very resistance it was meant to sidestep. Second, on the business side, the FDA's expert committee meets today to vote on Replimune's oncolytic virus therapy for melanoma. It's an unusually contentious meeting where the agency has already told the panel it cannot interpret the trial. Third, in AI in biomedicine, a group at the Berlin Institute of Health at Charité re-implements a big medical-history foundation model, and then reads what the model has quietly learned about biology — by running a genome-wide association study on the model's own internal representations. And finally, animals — a five-year study of eleven elephants released back into the wild after lifetimes in human captivity finds they adapt more or less like wild elephants, each with their own individual coping style.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

The topic here is something called doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis, or doxy P E P. The idea is simple. Doxycycline is an old, cheap, tetracycline-family antibiotic. Randomized trials in men who have sex with men have shown that taking a single two-hundred-milligram dose of doxycycline within seventy-two hours of unprotected sex cuts the risk of a bacterial sexually transmitted infection — syphilis, chlamydia, and to a lesser extent gonorrhea — by roughly sixty to eighty percent for the first two of those. Because those trials were so successful, the CDC issued guidelines in twenty twenty-four supporting doxy P E P for people at high risk of STIs, and it is now being rolled out across the US and the UK.

But there is a well-known reason to worry about long-term use of any antibiotic for prevention. It selects for resistance. Every time you take doxycycline, you put pressure on the entire community of bacteria in your body — not just the sexually transmitted ones — and you give an advantage to any strain that can shrug off tetracyclines. Over years and across populations, that adds up.

The specific worry with gonorrhea is that gonorrhea already has a scary resistance story. The bacterium behind it, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, is on the World Health Organization's list of high-priority drug-resistant pathogens. Frontline treatment right now is a shot of a cephalosporin called ceftriaxone — because gonorrhea has already broken through nearly every other antibiotic humanity has thrown at it, including tetracyclines. Doxy P E P will not cure a gonorrhea infection, but it does put tetracycline pressure on gonorrhea. And if a tetracycline-resistant strain then picks up ceftriaxone resistance, you get what public-health people call a dual-resistant strain. That is the nightmare scenario, because we do not have a good third-line drug ready to go.

So here is what the paper actually did. The authors — a collaboration led out of the National University of Singapore, with Imperial College London, the University of Toronto, and Berkeley — built what's called a compartmental transmission model of gonorrhea, calibrated to the population most affected: men who have sex with men in England. Compartmental means they split the modeled population into buckets — susceptible, infected, treated, recovered — and let simulated people move between buckets at rates matched to real surveillance data. But instead of just modeling one flavor of gonorrhea, they modeled four in parallel — a susceptible strain, a tetracycline-resistant strain, a ceftriaxone-resistant strain, and the dual-resistant strain — so they could watch which strains grow and which shrink over a fifteen-year horizon, depending on what interventions you deploy.

They then simulated three intervention strategies. Doxy P E P alone. A moderately effective gonorrhea vaccine alone — one is genuinely on the horizon, a repurposing of the meningococcal B vaccine, which has shown roughly thirty to fifty percent cross-protection against gonorrhea in observational studies. And doxy P E P plus vaccine together.

Here are the results. Doxy P E P alone gives you a small overall benefit. On average, one enrollment averts about seven-hundredths of a gonorrhea infection over fifteen years. That's small. Vaccination alone — even at moderate efficacy — gives you ten times that: about seventy-three hundredths of an averted infection per person vaccinated. And the two together add up almost linearly to about eighty-three hundredths.

But the aggregate number hides the important story. Doxy P E P alone actively expands the tetracycline-resistant strain. It does what tetracycline pressure has always done — it selects for tetracycline resistance. The vaccine, because it doesn't select on any antibiotic, doesn't do that. And in the combined strategy, the vaccine's overall reduction in transmission is enough to keep the tetracycline-resistant strain in check while still capturing the doxy P E P prevention benefit.

There is a second, harder result buried in the paper that is worth pulling out. What actually determines whether the dual-resistant nightmare strain emerges over the fifteen-year horizon is not doxy P E P itself — it is the background rate at which frontline ceftriaxone treatment is failing. Right now, ceftriaxone treatment failure for gonorrhea is under one percent. But the model shows that if that failure rate rises to twenty percent — which is not implausible over a decade, given how gonorrhea has broken through every previous antibiotic — then adding doxy P E P on top can drive on the order of five hundred additional dual-resistant infections over fifteen years, and under some assumptions many more, compared to no intervention at all. Under those conditions, doxy P E P tips gonorrhea from a manageable problem to an actively deteriorating one. In that same scenario, vaccination — with or without doxy P E P — still keeps things under control.

Why does this matter? Doxy P E P is being rolled out right now, in the real world, largely on the strength of those short-term randomized trials showing individual protection. This paper is a formal, calibrated argument that the fifteen-year population-level story is different — that the intervention's public-health value depends heavily on whether we also have a working vaccine and a working frontline drug alongside it. If ceftriaxone starts to falter and we are relying on doxy P E P alone, the intervention becomes a resistance amplifier. If we have a vaccine coming up behind it, doxy P E P is safe to keep using.

The practical implication is that doxy P E P should not be treated as a solo tool. It should be paired with active surveillance of both tetracycline and ceftriaxone resistance, and its deployment should be timed to the availability of the vaccine that is genuinely on the horizon. Given that this is a single modeling study — going off the abstract here, since the medRxiv preprint body was still rendering at run time — you wouldn't want to make policy on it alone. But the direction of the result is consistent with everything we know about how antibiotic-prevention programs have gone in the past, and it argues loudly for not putting all the eggs in the doxy P E P basket.

On the business side.

The FDA's Cellular, Tissue, and Gene Therapies Advisory Committee is meeting today at White Oak in Maryland to vote on Replimune's melanoma therapy R P one. The vote is non-binding but it heavily determines what the FDA does at its formal decision deadline this Sunday, August second. Ahead of the meeting the agency posted its briefing documents on Monday — and the documents were unusually harsh. Replimune's stock dropped about a third on the day.

Some context first. R P one — the generic name is vusolimogene oderparepvec — is an oncolytic virus therapy. Oncolytic virus is exactly what it sounds like — a live virus engineered to selectively infect and kill tumor cells. R P one is built on herpes simplex virus one — the same family of virus that causes cold sores — but the version Replimune uses has had several viral genes deleted and several human immune-signaling genes inserted, so it preferentially replicates in tumor cells rather than normal ones, and when it kills those tumor cells it releases immune-activating molecules that recruit the rest of the immune system to attack the surrounding tumor. Oncolytic H S V in melanoma is not new — Amgen's talimogene laherparepvec, marketed as Imlygic, was approved a decade ago — but Imlygic is used earlier in the disease, before frontline immunotherapy. R P one is being pitched for a much harder patient population: people whose melanoma has already progressed on frontline P D one blockade with drugs like Keytruda or Opdivo. That is a population with essentially no remaining options.

The trial in question is called I G N Y T E. It is a Phase one, Phase two study in one hundred and forty patients whose melanoma had progressed on prior anti-P D one therapy. All of them received R P one plus nivolumab — Bristol Myers Squibb's P D one drug, Opdivo. There was no control arm — no group that received only Opdivo, no group that received standard of care. The trial was single-arm. And the raw numbers looked good — objective response rate about thirty-four percent, and among responders, the median duration of response was about twenty-five months, which is a genuinely long and durable benefit in a population this sick.

But here is the FDA's problem, laid out in the briefing document. Without a control arm, you cannot cleanly attribute those responses to R P one. Patients on the trial had already progressed on P D one blockade — but they then received R P one plus more P D one blockade, meaning some of them may have simply been slow responders to the P D one drug, not to the oncolytic virus. In addition, the FDA notes that the way response was assessed uses a bespoke methodology that mixes lesion-level and patient-level measurements — different from the standard R E C I S T criteria most oncology trials use — and that this makes the reported response rate hard to verify. And the overall-survival analysis, in the FDA's exact words, is "not interpretable" because you cannot know what would have happened to the same patients on standard of care.

Replimune's counterargument is essentially — the population is too sick and too under-served to run a randomized trial ethically. Because there is no established second-line therapy after P D one failure, randomizing patients to standard care would mean randomizing them to nothing that works. And they have external real-world data on outcomes for anti-P D one-refractory melanoma patients that they argue can serve as a historical control. The FDA in the briefing document does not accept that argument.

The stakes are unusually high. This is Replimune's third try — the FDA has rejected R P one twice already on similar single-arm-data grounds. The AdComm meeting starts this morning and runs into the afternoon; the formal decision deadline is Sunday. Zooming out, if R P one gets rejected today, the oncolytic-virus field is going to have to reckon with the fact that single-arm data is going to keep hitting this wall, and future oncolytic-virus programs — of which there are many in development — will need to design randomized comparator arms from the start, even into pretreated populations.

Turning to AI in biomedicine.

There is a preprint out of the Berlin Institute of Health at Charité and Precision Healthcare University Research Institute in London — senior author Claudia Langenberg — that does something clever with a class of models people have started calling medical-history foundation models. These are large transformers, trained the way GPT-style language models are trained, but instead of tokens of English text they see tokens of electronic health record events — I C D ten diagnosis codes, procedure codes, lab tests, medications — arranged along a patient's timeline. The one used in this paper is called Delphi 2 M. It is a generative transformer trained on medical histories from the UK Biobank. Once trained, it can predict a patient's future diagnoses given their past medical record, and predictive performance is very good — competitive with, or better than, hand-engineered clinical risk scores across hundreds of diseases.

The problem — and this is what motivates the paper — is that nobody quite knows what these models have actually learned. Because they were trained on diagnosis codes, when you apply standard interpretability techniques to them, you get back — essentially — descriptions of the input diagnosis codes. Not the underlying biology those codes are proxies for. You could imagine a model that has secretly discovered that patients with a certain code plus a certain lab value are actually two distinct disease subgroups because of an underlying pathway — and you'd never see it. The model's internal representations, called embeddings, are just numbers to us.

Here is the clever trick. Every UK Biobank participant used to train Delphi 2 M also has their entire genome sequenced. So the authors did the following. They took Delphi 2 M's one hundred and twenty learned embedding dimensions — the low-dimensional summary vector the model produces for each patient — and they treated each of those dimensions as a phenotype. Then they ran a genome-wide association study — a G W A S — on each of those hundred and twenty embedding dimensions, across more than five hundred thousand people. G W A S asks the question: which single-letter variants along the genome correlate with a given trait. Normally the trait is height, or blood pressure, or a specific diagnosis. Here the trait is what a foundation model has learned to represent.

What they found is striking. The embeddings are heritable — that is, they have a genetic signal — meaning the model has picked up on something real about biology, not just accounting artifacts in the electronic health record. They identified four hundred and thirty-four genome-wide-significant genetic associations across ninety-eight of the one hundred and twenty embedding dimensions, at one hundred and fifty-one distinct locations in the genome.

Now — the biology the model has quietly learned. Two pathways come out very strongly. First, cholesterol metabolism — meaning the model has secretly learned some organizing principle about blood lipids, even though blood lipid levels are not diagnosis codes and are not part of what it was trained on. Second, a cytokine pathway called the I L one epithelial alarmin pathway, which is central to asthma and other allergic diseases. When they compared these signals to variants already known to associate with body mass index, blood lipids, and asthma, they got more than fifty-fold enrichment for those trait categories in the embedding G W A S. That is a large signal.

And then the most concrete result. They mapped these genetic loci to specific target genes, and asked whether those target genes are already known drug targets. The answer is that essentially all approved lipid-lowering drugs — statins, P C S K nine inhibitors — and essentially all approved severe-asthma biologics fall out of these embedding G W A S peaks. So Delphi 2 M has independently rediscovered known drug targets, just from watching diagnosis codes go by. And twelve additional genes came out that are not obvious targets from single-disease G W A S alone but were supported by the embedding analysis — meaning this could be a way to nominate new drug targets that a code-by-code approach would miss.

There is one caveat the paper is honest about. The embeddings — while they encode biology beautifully — are not always the best predictor of individual disease outcomes on their own. For most common diseases, adding specific biomarkers like a lipid panel or a spirometry reading to Delphi 2 M improves prediction substantially. So the practical read is not "throw out biomarkers and use only foundation-model embeddings." It is "the next generation of these models should be trained with genetic and biomarker information as an explicit regularizer, not as a replacement for it."

Zooming out — this is a genuinely novel interpretability technique for medical AI. If you can use human genetics to audit what a foundation model has learned, you have a partial answer to the reproducibility and safety concerns that have dogged clinical AI. You are no longer stuck asking whether the model has learned an artifact or has learned biology. You can measure how much of what it learned is heritable — and heritable is a decent proxy for biological — and you can find out which pathways it discovered.

And finally, animals.

The Elephant Reintegration Trust of South Africa published a five-year study in PLOS ONE yesterday, led by Tenisha Roos and Tamara Eggeling, that followed eleven African elephants which had spent most of their lives in human care — circuses, safari operations, private ownership — and were then released onto four large game reserves in South Africa. The team observed each elephant for three weeks at a stretch, at least once a year, for five years. They tracked wariness, self-directed touching — which is a stereotyped behavior common in captive elephants — aggression toward other elephants, and general movement patterns. And they collected fecal samples to measure metabolic stress hormones — mostly cortisol metabolites — as an objective physiological readout of chronic stress that isn't visible in behavior alone.

The finding is that the rewilded elephants behaved, more or less, like wild elephants. Their movement patterns, their responses to other elephants, their day-to-day activity — all fell within the range that wild-born elephants exhibit. Females tended to show slightly elevated stress hormones compared to males, and older animals took longer to settle than younger ones. But crucially, none of the stress-hormone values were outside the normal range for wild elephants. Individual personality mattered a lot. Some elephants seemed to bounce back into full wild behavior in months; others took years. But all of them, eventually, got there.

Why is this interesting? The debate over what to do with captive elephants — of which there are still thousands worldwide — has long assumed that release is not a real option, because captive elephants have been so behaviorally and physiologically altered that they cannot survive in the wild. This is the first longitudinal, quantitative refutation of that assumption. Rewilded elephants can regain full autonomy, adapt to unfamiliar environments, and — in the authors' words — leave a lifetime of confinement behind. It reframes elephants that have been in captivity for decades as a real conservation resource, not just a welfare problem.
