## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Tuesday, July twenty-eighth, 2026. Five items today. First, a paper from the University of Kansas argues that Pseudomonas aeruginosa — one of the most consequential hospital pathogens — evades antibiotics not by mutating a drug target, but by inducing our own liver enzymes to clear the drug faster. Second, on the business side, ProMIS Neurosciences reported six-month interim safety data from its Phase 1b Alzheimer's trial: zero cases of brain swelling across a hundred and thirty-six patients, including a large APOE4-heavy population — a striking result if it holds. Third, in AI, Brian Hie's group at MIT used a protein language model to redesign the active-site region of Rubisco — the enzyme that fixes atmospheric carbon dioxide into biomass — and got a single amino-acid substitution that improves the carboxylation rate by twenty-nine percent. Fourth, on AI tools, the Model Context Protocol finalized its biggest specification change since launch today — dropping stateful sessions entirely, which matters if you're building agent-based tools over bioinformatics infrastructure. And finally, animals — a paper in Science showing that mosquito-relative larvae in Lake Malawi dive over two hundred meters down every day and back up, using tiny air sacs whose volume they control by chemistry.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

A group at the University of Kansas Medical Center — the Lampe lab — posted a preprint this weekend on a mechanism of Pseudomonas aeruginosa antibiotic evasion that I don't think most clinical microbiologists are thinking about. To set this up I need to walk through three pieces of biology.

Piece one — the pathogen. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a Gram-negative bacterium that lives freely in water, soil, and biofilms almost everywhere. In healthy people it's mostly harmless. In hospitals, in people on ventilators, in burn patients, in people with cystic fibrosis whose lungs are chronically colonized, it's one of the leading causes of ventilator-associated pneumonia and one of the most stubbornly drug-resistant infections we treat. The World Health Organization lists carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas as a critical-priority pathogen.

Piece two — the drug. Ciprofloxacin is a fluoroquinolone antibiotic — the class most doctors reach for when they want oral coverage of Gram-negatives including Pseudomonas. Once ciprofloxacin gets into the body, some of it is excreted by the kidneys unchanged and some of it is metabolized in the liver by an enzyme called cytochrome P four fifty one-A-two — usually written as CYP1A2. Cytochromes P four fifty are a huge family of enzymes that chemically modify drugs and other foreign molecules to make them easier to get rid of. When CYP1A2 activity goes up, ciprofloxacin gets cleared faster, and the drug concentration in your blood and tissues goes down.

Piece three — the pigment. Pseudomonas produces a blue-green pigment called pyocyanin. If you've ever seen a Pseudomonas-infected wound turn a striking teal color, that's pyocyanin. Pyocyanin has been studied for decades as a virulence factor — it damages host tissue by generating reactive oxygen species. But more recently, pyocyanin has been found to bind a host protein called the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, usually abbreviated as AhR. The aryl hydrocarbon receptor is the transcription factor your body uses to sense foreign chemicals. When it's activated, it turns on genes for drug-metabolizing enzymes — including the CYP1A family — as a kind of chemical defense system. It's how your liver ramps up its detox machinery when something new shows up.

Now put those three pieces together. Pseudomonas floods its local environment with pyocyanin. Pyocyanin activates the human aryl hydrocarbon receptor. That drives up the expression of CYP1A2. And CYP1A2 is exactly the enzyme that clears the antibiotic being used to treat the infection.

That's the hypothesis. What the Kansas group did is test it in primary human hepatocytes — real human liver cells taken from donor tissue rather than a cancer cell line. They dosed the cells with pyocyanin at concentrations that overlap what's been measured in the lungs and sputum of infected patients, and they measured two things: the messenger RNA level of CYP1A2, and how fast the cells actually metabolized ciprofloxacin.

The result. At five micromolar pyocyanin — a sub-toxic concentration, well within the physiological range — CYP1A2 messenger RNA went up six point two-fold, and the rate at which the hepatocytes converted ciprofloxacin into its main metabolite, oxo-ciprofloxacin, went up three point six-fold. So Pseudomonas, without mutating anything, without acquiring a resistance gene, without changing its own enzymes — just by making its normal pigment — can substantially increase the rate at which the host's liver destroys the antibiotic being used to kill it.

The reason this matters. Antibiotic resistance is almost always taught as something that happens inside the bacterium: efflux pumps, target mutations, beta-lactamase enzymes, that whole catalog. This paper says: even if the bacterium is fully susceptible on a plate in the microbiology lab, the concentration of drug actually reaching it in an infected patient can be lower than we think, because the pathogen is telling the host to burn through the drug faster. If that's happening clinically, sub-therapeutic exposure is exactly the condition that selects for resistance. You'd generate resistant mutants inside a patient whose infection was supposed to be susceptible.

Two things to flag as caveats. This is a preprint, so it hasn't been through peer review yet. And the work is in liver cells in culture — the next thing you'd want to see is whether ciprofloxacin plasma or tissue levels are actually depressed in Pseudomonas-infected patients versus matched controls, which is a much harder study. But as a mechanistic proposal it's clean, and as an example of pathogen-host pharmacological warfare it's the kind of thing that would change how you think about dosing.

On the business side.

ProMIS Neurosciences, a small clinical-stage biopharma with sites in Cambridge and Toronto, put out a six-month blinded interim readout this morning from its Phase 1b Alzheimer's trial, called PRECISE-AD. The drug is called PMN310. I want to explain why this readout got noticed, because the number itself is small but the context is not.

Anti-amyloid therapy for Alzheimer's, as of today, is dominated by two approved antibodies: lecanemab, marketed as Leqembi, from Eisai and Biogen; and donanemab, marketed as Kisunla, from Eli Lilly. Both drugs bind aggregated forms of amyloid-beta — the protein whose plaques accumulate in the brains of Alzheimer's patients. Both drugs slow cognitive decline modestly. And both drugs carry a specific and dangerous side effect called ARIA — amyloid-related imaging abnormalities. ARIA comes in two flavors: ARIA-E, which stands for edema, meaning brain swelling; and ARIA-H, which stands for hemorrhage, meaning small bleeds that show up on M R I. ARIA-E is the more feared one — it can be symptomatic and, in rare cases, fatal. On lecanemab, roughly thirteen percent of patients develop ARIA-E. On donanemab, roughly twenty-four percent. And APOE4 carriers — people carrying one or two copies of a particular variant of the apolipoprotein E gene, which is the biggest genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer's — are especially prone to ARIA. Genome-editing of the anti-amyloid class hasn't been able to escape this trade-off.

The mechanistic bet behind PMN310 is that ARIA is driven by antibodies binding amyloid deposits in the walls of blood vessels — that is, on the vascular plaques, not on the toxic, soluble forms of amyloid-beta that are thought to actually damage neurons. Amyloid-beta doesn't just exist as plaques. Before it aggregates into insoluble fibrils, it exists as smaller soluble clumps called oligomers, and there's a long-standing hypothesis in the field — the oligomer hypothesis — that these oligomers are what actually kill neurons. PMN310 is engineered to bind those oligomers, and specifically to avoid binding to plaques and to vascular amyloid. If the hypothesis is right, you get the therapeutic benefit without the vascular disruption that produces ARIA.

The interim readout. A hundred and thirty-six patients evaluated, dosed intravenously across three levels — five, ten, and twenty milligrams per kilogram. Sixty-one percent were APOE4 carriers, and eleven percent were homozygotes, meaning they carry two copies of the risk allele — exactly the group most likely to develop ARIA on the approved drugs. Six months in, on a blinded pooled analysis: ARIA-E, zero cases; ARIA-H, four point four percent of patients, all mild and asymptomatic. No treatment-related serious adverse events. No drug-related discontinuations.

On biomarkers: on the same blinded basis, sixty-eight point five percent of patients showed a decline from baseline in plasma phospho-tau two-seventeen, and sixty-two point five percent showed a decline in cerebrospinal fluid microtubule-binding-region tau two forty-three. Both of these are proxies for the tau pathology that follows amyloid pathology in Alzheimer's brains. ProMIS explicitly said this is not a determination of efficacy — the trial is still blinded, and biomarker trends don't always translate into clinical benefit — but the direction is at least consistent with target engagement.

What to take away. First, on safety, if this holds up when the trial unblinds, you're looking at a drug in the anti-amyloid class with dramatically lower ARIA risk than the approved competitors, in a population enriched for the highest-risk genetics. Second, the mechanistic frame is testable — the topline unblinded twelve-month data are expected in the first quarter of 2027, and that's when we'll see whether oligomer-selective binding actually produces a clinical effect. Third — and this is where I'd be cautious — a lot of Alzheimer's drugs have looked encouraging at biomarkers and Phase 1 safety and then failed on cognition. This is a signal worth watching, not a result yet.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A paper posted to bioRxiv this weekend from Matthew Shoulders's lab at M I T, with Brian Hie as a corresponding author, applied a protein language model to one of the most famous — and most stubborn — engineering targets in biology. The target is an enzyme called Rubisco.

Rubisco is short for ribulose-one-five-bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase. It's the enzyme in plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria that grabs a molecule of carbon dioxide out of the air and attaches it to a sugar backbone. This is the reaction that pulls essentially all fixed carbon in the biosphere into living cells. If Rubisco stopped working, life on Earth would collapse in weeks. It is generally considered the most abundant protein on the planet.

The weird thing about Rubisco is that it is also famously bad at its job. Its turnover rate — the number of carbon dioxide molecules a single enzyme fixes per second — is between two and ten, depending on the organism. For comparison, most enzymes turn over in the hundreds to thousands of molecules per second. Rubisco is also promiscuous — it will bind oxygen instead of carbon dioxide about one time in four in ambient air, which triggers a wasteful side reaction called photorespiration that plants have to pay energy to clean up. Plants compensate for a slow, error-prone enzyme by making enormous quantities of it, but the underlying rate limits crop productivity. If you could speed Rubisco up, you'd expect higher photosynthetic efficiency and higher yield.

People have been trying to engineer Rubisco for decades. It has resisted. The active site is highly constrained, the enzyme has to assemble correctly in chloroplasts, and directed evolution campaigns have mostly returned variants that either work about the same or work worse.

Here's what the M I T group did. They used a machine learning model called E S M - I F 1, developed by Meta A I. E S M - I F 1 is an inverse-folding model. Given a three-dimensional protein structure, it predicts what amino acid sequences are compatible with that structure — essentially, it reads the geometry and reasons about what letters would fit at each position. Brian Hie's group has been central to developing and using this class of model for protein engineering. In this paper, they used E S M - I F 1 not to design the whole enzyme, but as a filter — to nominate which specific positions in the large subunit of tobacco Rubisco to mutate, and which substitutions at those positions were most likely to produce a folded, functional variant. They then built a small library of those variants and selected in E. coli engineered to require Rubisco to grow. Cells that grow faster carry variants that fix carbon dioxide faster.

The result. The best variant carried a single substitution, threonine to isoleucine at position three ninety-one, which improved carboxylation rate by twenty-nine percent and aerobic carboxylation efficiency — meaning performance in the presence of oxygen, which is what a real leaf sees — by forty-three percent. Multiple other enriched variants also improved on the wild type.

Here is what I think is the most interesting finding. Several of the winning substitutions, including the threonine-to-isoleucine variant, are not present in any known plant lineage. Rubisco has been under natural selection for over three billion years, and the sequence space you observe in extant plants is a slice through that history. This paper says that a structure-aware model, trained on the space of stable protein folds in general, can propose functional substitutions that plant evolution has never sampled. In other words, the machine learning model isn't just recovering what evolution has already tried — it's finding solutions that were, apparently, accessible but not visited.

What this changes. For working scientists using protein language models on directed evolution problems, this is a template. E S M - I F 1 as a way to prune library space, followed by selection in the appropriate host, on a protein that has resisted every other engineering approach. Whether the T391I Rubisco actually improves plant biomass in a chloroplast, when moved out of E. coli and into a real crop, is the next question — the authors show it's assembly-competent in chloroplasts, but yield gains in the field are a very different thing. Still, this is the most encouraging Rubisco engineering result in years, and the method is generalizable.

On AI tools.

The Model Context Protocol, which the industry usually abbreviates M C P, finalized its 2026-07-28 specification today. This is the biggest spec change since M C P launched, and I want to explain what M C P actually is, because it matters for anyone building AI-driven scientific tools.

M C P is a standardized wire protocol between a language-model application and the tools, data sources, and instruments it needs to talk to. If you want your AI agent to be able to search PubMed, run BLAST, query a lab information management system, or pull data from an electronic health record, M C P defines how those tools describe themselves and how the model calls them. It was originally released by Anthropic in late 2024, and last year the project moved to the Linux Foundation, which now governs it. Every major model provider — Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and most of the open ecosystem — implements M C P clients today.

The biggest change in this spec is the removal of what were called stateful sessions. In the old protocol, a client had to open a connection to a server by sending an initialize handshake and receiving an M C P session I D header, which then had to be reused on every subsequent request. That worked, but it meant that if you were running a large fleet of M C P servers behind a load balancer, you needed sticky sessions — every request from a given client had to route back to the same server instance, because the state was pinned there. That's operationally painful at scale. The new spec throws that out. Client information travels as metadata on each individual request, servers are stateless, and any request can be handled by any server instance behind a plain round-robin load balancer. This is the sort of unglamorous plumbing change that makes remote M C P deployment substantially cheaper and simpler.

Two other additions worth calling out. The first is called M C P Apps. It lets an M C P server declare an H T M L interface that the client can render inside a sandboxed iframe. The idea is that some tools benefit from a real user interface — imagine a genome browser or a molecule viewer that you want the model to hand off to you visually — and M C P Apps standardizes how those UIs are declared, prefetched, and audited. The second is M C P Tasks, which formalizes long-running work. Previously, an M C P tool call was expected to return promptly; if you wanted the model to run something that takes minutes or hours — say, a large sequence alignment — you had to hack around the protocol. Tasks makes that a first-class concept, with defined lifecycle methods for querying, updating, and cancelling.

Why this matters for biomedicine. Almost every serious AI application in this field involves calling external tools — databases, algorithms, instruments. If M C P consolidates as the standard for that, then bioinformatics tooling exposed as an M C P server becomes portable across every model that speaks the protocol. That's good news for anyone who has spent time writing bespoke tool integrations against three different vendor APIs.

And finally, animals.

A team led by researchers at the University of British Columbia published a paper in Science this month describing the diving behavior — and its physiology — of a tiny freshwater insect in Lake Malawi. The species is called Chaoborus edulis. It's the larval stage of what's known as a phantom midge, a mosquito-relative. Lake Malawi is one of the deepest lakes in Africa, and Chaoborus larvae are extremely abundant there — swarms of them are thick enough to be visible from satellites when the adults hatch.

The observation. Every day, the larvae migrate vertically. During the day, they descend more than two hundred meters into the deep, cold, oxygen-starved lower waters of the lake, where fish can't follow them. At night, they come back up to the surface layers to feed. Two hundred meters underwater is a lot of pressure — roughly twenty atmospheres. For an animal only a few millimeters long, that's not an obvious thing to survive, let alone to do every day.

The mechanism. Chaoborus larvae have two pairs of internal air sacs, roughly along the length of their body. In other insects, these are respiratory reservoirs. In Chaoborus, they've been repurposed as ballast tanks. The wall of each sac is built out of a rubbery insect protein called resilin — the same material used in the springs that let a flea jump or a wasp's wings beat. What the U B C team showed is that the larva controls the volume of each air sac not by pumping air in and out, but by changing the p H of the fluid around the sac walls. Resilin's mechanical properties depend on the p H it's in, so a small shift in acidity makes the sac contract or expand, which changes the animal's buoyancy. It is, essentially, a chemically driven submarine ballast system built out of a single elastic protein.

They also tested the sacs in a miniature pressure chamber. The sacs withstood pressures corresponding to depths beyond four hundred meters — twice what the larvae actually go to. So the animal isn't operating anywhere near the limit of its own hardware.

The broader implication, which the authors flag and which I find really interesting, is about insect biogeography. Insects are the most diverse group of animals on the planet — over a million described species — and yet they are almost entirely absent from the open ocean. There are only a handful of marine insect species. The standard explanation for that has been physiological: insects can't handle salt, they can't handle the pressure, they can't handle the depth. This paper is a data point against the pressure part of that story. A freshwater insect larva, without any obvious specialized adaptation, tolerates pressures that would put you two-thirds of the way to the deep sea. So maybe the reason the ocean is closed to insects isn't that they can't survive it. It's something else — competition with crustaceans, egg-laying constraints, salt tolerance — that we haven't identified yet.
