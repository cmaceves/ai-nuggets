## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Wednesday, August fifth, twenty-twenty-six. Four items today. First, on pathogen watch — the C D C's tally of the summer's massive Cyclospora outbreak crossed ten thousand laboratory-confirmed domestic cases yesterday, with a further twelve thousand still awaiting confirmation and the first two deaths — both in Michigan — reported over the weekend. The outbreak has been traced back to iceberg lettuce grown by Taylor Farms de México and served at Taco Bell across a nine-state cluster, and it is a case study in the surveillance blind spot that opens when the pathogen you are chasing cannot be grown in a lab. Second, on the business side — Otsuka yesterday released the full twenty-four-month eGFR data from the VISIONARY Phase three trial of sibeprenlimab in patients with I-g-A nephropathy. Sibeprenlimab is an antibody that blocks a cytokine called APRIL, and the drug does something no previous I-g-A nephropathy therapy has cleanly demonstrated. It flattens the trajectory of kidney function decline back to essentially the physiologic baseline. That is a paradigm change for a disease whose defining feature has been that eGFR keeps dropping despite treatment. Third, on AI in biomedicine — Pathos, an AI-native biotech, announced yesterday a package of deals worth more than two billion dollars: a licensing agreement with China's Alphamab for a TROP-two-slash-HER-three bispecific antibody-drug conjugate, a collaboration with AstraZeneca on a protein-degrader breast cancer drug, and a new Series E raise of up to three hundred million dollars. The story here is the shape of the business model. Pathos is not making drugs — it is buying human-designed molecules and applying an AI platform called Foundry to design the trials. That is the version of AI-in-drug-discovery that is actually finding money right now. And finally, animals — a paper in Nature last month, and picked up widely this week, describes a two-centimeter, five-hundred-eighteen-million-year-old creature from southern China called Urokodia aequalis. It has pincer-like appendages behind the eyes that are the earliest known ancestor of the venom-delivering fangs on every spider walking around your basement.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Cyclospora cayetanensis is a single-celled parasite, a coccidian in the phylum Apicomplexa — the same broad branch that gives us the malaria parasite and Toxoplasma. It causes cyclosporiasis, an intestinal illness in humans defined by watery, prolonged, and often relapsing diarrhea. The parasite has one very specific ecological quirk that shapes everything about how outbreaks unfold and how surveillance chases them. When an infected person sheds Cyclospora oocysts — the environmental resting stage of the parasite — those oocysts are not immediately infectious to the next person. They need days to weeks in the outside environment, at the right temperature and moisture, to sporulate — to mature into the infectious form. This means Cyclospora essentially cannot spread person-to-person. It spreads from a shed source — usually contaminated water or produce — after enough time in the environment.

That biology means Cyclospora outbreaks look like classic foodborne outbreaks — clustered around a single contaminated product — but with an unusually long incubation window, typically one to two weeks between exposure and symptoms. And what has been unfolding in the U-S since May is the largest such outbreak on record.

Here is the state of the tally as of yesterday's C D C update. Just over ten thousand laboratory-confirmed domestic cases nationally since May first, with another roughly twelve thousand cases still awaiting laboratory confirmation. Michigan is by far the epicenter — the state health department's own count, which lags the federal confirmation process, is now above ten thousand, with more than one hundred and ninety hospitalizations. The main nine-state cluster spans Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Michigan, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, and West Virginia. On Sunday the C D C reported the outbreak's first two deaths, both in Michigan. That is by an order of magnitude the largest U-S Cyclospora outbreak on record.

The traceback. On July seventeenth, Taylor Farms de México — a subsidiary of one of the largest fresh-produce shippers in North America — initiated a voluntary recall of iceberg lettuce sourced from its central Mexico growing operations, after F-D-A trace investigation pointed to iceberg lettuce distributed to more than two dozen states and served at Taco Bell restaurants and sold at Walmart under the Marketside label. The Mexican farm implicated has since resumed operations.

Here is what makes this outbreak diagnostically hard, and the surveillance angle worth spending a minute on. When you want to answer whether two people who both got sick in different states were infected by the same source, the standard workflow for bacterial foodborne pathogens is simple. You grow the bacterium from each patient's stool, whole-genome sequence the isolates, and see how closely related the sequences are. For E. coli, Salmonella, Listeria, that pipeline is what the C-D-C's PulseNet network runs continuously. Cases that share a genetic fingerprint cluster into an outbreak; unrelated cases fall out. This works because you can *grow* those pathogens in a lab.

Cyclospora, you cannot. The parasite has never been reliably cultured outside a human host in a way that supports clinical genotyping. So the C-D-C relies on what they call *partial genotyping* — sequencing a handful of variable markers directly from clinical stool, rather than the whole genome — and the resolution of that method is genuinely limited. Craig Hedberg, the University of Minnesota foodborne-disease researcher, put it plainly in an interview last week: the subtyping needed to identify which cases share a source is not readily available for Cyclospora the way it is for bacterial pathogens. That means when a cluster like this one appears, the identification of the specific food source rests heavily on old-school epidemiology — food history interviews with sick patients — supplemented with whatever partial genotyping can distinguish.

That epidemiological process is why, in this outbreak, we know Taylor Farms lettuce is the source without any product sample having tested positive for Cyclospora. That fact — no positive product test — has been used publicly to argue against the identification. But that framing gets the biology backwards. Once contaminated oocysts are shipped, washed, chopped, and sit on a produce line for days, the chance that any given sample retains a detectable pathogen load is low. The epidemiological match is stronger than the microbiological one, and for Cyclospora it usually will be.

Two things for a working biomedical listener to take from this. First, the outbreak is real, ongoing, and it is genuinely on the downslope of its natural curve — Cyclospora outbreaks self-terminate as the contaminated product clears the supply. Second, the surveillance lesson is that our detection infrastructure for foodborne pathogens is essentially bacterial-first. For parasites you cannot culture, we are running with a much blunter tool set, and the political discourse about food-safety adequacy tends to conflate absence of a positive product test with absence of a causal link. That is the wrong inference for this parasite.

On the business side.

Otsuka Pharmaceutical yesterday released the full two-year kidney-function data from VISIONARY, the pivotal Phase three trial of a monoclonal antibody called sibeprenlimab — brand name Voyxact — in patients with I-g-A nephropathy. And the result is unusual enough to matter beyond the specialist audience.

Some context. I-g-A nephropathy — sometimes called Berger's disease — is the most common form of primary glomerulonephritis worldwide. It is caused by deposits of an immune complex containing I-g-A, one of the antibody classes, in the glomeruli of the kidney — the filters. Those deposits trigger inflammation, scarring, and progressive loss of filtration capacity. About thirty to forty percent of patients progress to end-stage kidney disease within twenty years of diagnosis. Treatment historically has been blood-pressure control with A-C-E inhibitors and, in higher-risk patients, immunosuppression with steroids. Neither of these actually halts the disease. They slow it. And "slowing kidney function decline" is the language of I-g-A nephropathy management specifically because arresting the decline was not thought to be achievable.

Sibeprenlimab targets a cytokine called APRIL, which stands for "a proliferation-inducing ligand." APRIL is a member of the T-N-F cytokine superfamily. It binds two receptors — B-C-M-A and T-A-C-I — on B cells, the antibody-producing arm of the immune system. When APRIL binds T-A-C-I on a B cell, it signals class-switching toward I-g-A production. In I-g-A nephropathy, a subset of B cells is producing a specific pathogenic version of I-g-A — I-g-A-one with abnormally low galactosylation — and APRIL signaling is what keeps those B cells alive and productive. Block APRIL, you shut off the pipeline of pathogenic I-g-A being made. That is the mechanistic bet.

Here is what VISIONARY reported. Adults with primary I-g-A nephropathy at risk for progression, randomized to sibeprenlimab or placebo on top of standard supportive care, and followed for two years. The primary endpoint at nine months — proteinuria reduction — was already known; that is what supported the F-D-A accelerated approval in November of last year. What was new yesterday is the twenty-four-month kidney-function slope. The annualized rate of eGFR decline — meaning, how much filtration capacity a patient loses each year — was minus four-point-two milliliters per minute in the placebo group. In the sibeprenlimab group, it was *plus* zero-point-three. Statistically indistinguishable from zero, and clinically indistinguishable from the physiologic decline you see in a healthy person aging normally. The difference was four-point-five milliliters per minute per year — highly statistically significant.

The safety picture matched the enthusiasm. Overall adverse events were essentially equal in the two groups — ninety percent versus ninety point seven. And unusually for a drug that suppresses part of B-cell activity, serious infections were *lower* in the sibeprenlimab arm — one-point-nine percent versus four percent in placebo. The likely explanation is that APRIL blockade affects a narrower B-cell repertoire than broad B-cell depletion agents like rituximab do, and the residual immune coverage against bacterial pathogens is intact.

The reason this matters, beyond I-g-A nephropathy itself. There is a K-D-I-G-O guideline threshold that says a treatment which reduces eGFR decline to less than one milliliter per minute per year is essentially matching normal physiologic aging. That was the aspiration for I-g-A nephropathy therapy, not the assumption. VISIONARY says a targeted anti-APRIL antibody can do it. That validates the APRIL-B-cell-I-g-A axis as a genuine causal driver of the disease, and it hands the field a rational drug target rather than a management strategy. Otsuka is now rolling a supplemental application to the F-D-A for full traditional approval on the basis of this data. Novartis has a competing anti-APRIL antibody, zigakibart, running its own Phase three. Vera Therapeutics has a related dual-blocking antibody, atacicept, that hits both APRIL and its sibling cytokine BAFF. This is going to be a real competitive space, and it opens the same question that got asked in ophthalmology after the first anti-V-E-G-F drugs — namely, if you can arrest the disease, when and in whom do you start, and for how long do you continue.

On AI in biomedicine.

Yesterday, a San Francisco biotech called Pathos A-I did three things at once. It signed a licensing deal with a Chinese biotech called Alphamab Oncology for a drug called J-S-K-N-oh-one-six. It signed a collaboration with AstraZeneca on a molecule called A-Z-D four-two-four-one. And it opened a Series E funding round of up to three hundred million dollars. And the reason to talk about all three together is that they say something specific about where AI-in-drug-discovery has actually settled as a business model.

Some background on Pathos. It was founded a few years ago on the premise that AI could accelerate cancer-drug development. Its central product is a platform called Foundry, which the company describes as running thousands of AI agents in parallel to analyze biological, clinical, and real-world data. What Foundry does, in the company's own language, is: identify drug candidates worth developing, match them to the patient populations whose biology suggests they will respond, design the clinical trial, and select the sites and the patients to run it. The C-E-O, Iker Huerga, gave the summary line: Foundry's job is to design the trial that proves the drug — matching the drug to the patients whose biology demands it.

Now what did Pathos actually announce yesterday.

The Alphamab deal. Pathos licensed J-S-K-N-oh-one-six, a bispecific antibody-drug conjugate targeting TROP-two and HER-three, from Alphamab for one hundred and twenty-five million dollars up front and up to two-point-one billion dollars in milestones, plus tiered royalties. Alphamab keeps rights in mainland China, Hong Kong, Macau, and Taiwan. Pathos gets the rest of the world.

Let me unpack the drug itself, because the mechanism is a good example of a specific direction cancer therapy has been moving in. TROP-two is a cell-surface protein overexpressed on many solid tumors — breast, lung, pancreas. It is one of a small handful of antigens that make good handles for antibody-drug conjugates. HER-three, meanwhile, is a receptor in the same family as HER-two — but unlike HER-two, HER-three is often *upregulated* by tumors as an escape mechanism after they have been treated with a HER-two-targeting drug. Tumors compensate by turning up HER-three signaling, and the drug that worked initially stops working. So a bispecific A-D-C — an antibody-drug conjugate that binds *both* TROP-two and HER-three on the same tumor cell simultaneously — has two potential advantages. One, it is more selective for tumors than for normal cells, because you need both antigens present on the same cell to get high-affinity binding and internalization of the payload. Two, it addresses HER-three-driven resistance up front rather than waiting for the tumor to develop it.

J-S-K-N-oh-one-six is already in Phase three trials in China. Pathos is licensing a drug in mid-stage clinical development from a company that developed it. That is the key point.

The AstraZeneca deal — terms undisclosed, so numbers unknown — hands Pathos early clinical development of A-Z-D four-two-four-one, a protein degrader targeting estrogen-receptor-driven breast cancer. Protein degraders are a newer therapeutic modality: instead of blocking a target protein's active site, they physically tag it for destruction by the cell's own protein-recycling machinery, the ubiquitin-proteasome system. That mechanism can hit targets which traditional inhibitors cannot — for example, scaffolding proteins with no obvious binding pocket — and can be more potent than reversible inhibitors because the target is gone, not just blocked. AstraZeneca made the protein degrader; Pathos will run the clinical program.

So both deals fit the same pattern. Pathos did not discover J-S-K-N-oh-one-six. Pathos did not discover A-Z-D four-two-four-one. Pathos is *licensing* human-designed drugs — one from a Chinese biotech, one from big pharma — and applying its A-I platform to the clinical development side. This is worth naming clearly, because a couple of years ago the pitch on A-I biotech was that A-I would design the molecules. What has actually happened — and this is the market signal — is that the A-I companies that are getting funded and building serious pipelines are the ones running the trial-design and patient-selection layer, not the molecule-invention layer. Recursion, Insitro, Isomorphic have all had partial versions of this pivot. Pathos is doing it explicitly. Trial design and patient-population matching is a real problem in oncology, and there is a plausible economic case that A-I can compress the timelines and improve success rates. Whether that specifically is what Pathos's platform is doing at a level that beats a good human trial designer is not yet public — the data will come out with the trials.

The other tell is the Series E. Pathos is raising up to three hundred million dollars on the back of these deals, at a valuation reportedly north of a billion and a half. That is real money in the current biotech financing environment, and it reflects investor conviction not that A-I designs better drugs, but that A-I can run better trials of the drugs that already exist. That is a narrower claim, but it is the operational claim the market appears to be pricing.

And finally, animals.

A paper published in Nature earlier this summer, from Yu Liu's lab at Yunnan University working with the University of Leicester, hit wider science-press circulation this week. The paper describes a two-to-three centimeter-long marine creature from the Chengjiang biota of southern China, five hundred and eighteen million years old, that they named Urokodia aequalis. The paper appeared in Nature on the forty-second anniversary of the Chengjiang site's discovery — the site being one of the world's most important Cambrian fossil localities.

Some context on what a chelicerate is, because it is the whole point of the paper. Chelicerates are one of the four main groups of living arthropods. Spiders, scorpions, ticks, mites, and horseshoe crabs are all chelicerates. Their defining anatomical feature is a pair of appendages at the front of the head called chelicerae — the pincer-like or fang-like structures used to seize and, in venomous species, to inject venom into prey. The origin of the chelicera has been one of paleontology's open questions. When did those appendages first appear? What did they evolve from?

Urokodia is the answer, or as close to one as we currently have. The fossil is exquisitely preserved — X-ray micro-tomography of the specimens reveals three-dimensional anatomy including a seven-segmented head, pincer-like appendages positioned immediately behind the eyes, jointed trunk limbs with double branches, and — this is the second important finding — trunk appendages with overlapping flap-like structures that the authors argue are the ancestors of book gills. Book gills are the stacked, page-like respiratory structures in horseshoe crabs, and their derivative in land arachnids is the book lung. So Urokodia hands paleontology two evolutionary origin stories at once. The pincer-like appendages are the direct ancestors of the fangs of every spider. And the book gills — the breathing apparatus horseshoe crabs still use — trace back to this same tiny Cambrian animal.

The image to hold onto is this. You are looking at a two-to-three-centimeter animal that lived in a warm shallow sea five hundred and eighteen million years ago on what is now southern China. It has large stalked eyes, seven head segments, and just behind its eyes, a pair of tiny grasping pincers. Those pincers — through a chain of descendants across five hundred and eighteen million years of evolution — became the fangs of the wolf spider on your porch, the venomous chelicerae of a black widow, the pincers of a scorpion, and the mouthparts of every tick. The fangs that inject spider venom today started as a grasping tool on a Cambrian marine predator smaller than a paperclip.
