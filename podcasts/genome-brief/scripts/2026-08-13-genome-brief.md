## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Thursday, August thirteenth, twenty twenty six. Today: a really interesting new mechanistic paper on SARS cov two shows that a small accessory protein called ORF eight — one you don't often hear about — actively promotes the virus's spread by *inducing* inflammation in the upper airway, which is the opposite of what you'd expect a virus to want. On the business side, Jazz Pharmaceuticals is paying up to one-point-three billion dollars for a small San Diego biotech called Actio Biosciences, entirely to get one drug — a first-in-class inhibitor of a potassium channel called KCNT one, and the biology of why this channel exists at all is worth unpacking. In A I for medicine, a group in Beijing posted a preprint on de novo siRNA design using discrete diffusion models — which is a nice bookend to yesterday's Silence Therapeutics story, because it's asking, essentially, whether A I can now *design* the kind of siRNA drug that took Silence years to engineer by hand. And to close: a beautifully clean fMRI study out of Vienna, published in iScience, provides the first neurobiological evidence that dogs can tell the difference between a scared human face and a sad one — and it turns out happiness lights up their reward circuit the same way food does.

Let's get into it.

Pathogen watch, first. A collaboration between labs at N Y U Grossman School of Medicine and the University of Maryland posted a preprint on bioRxiv this week that changes how we should think about a particular corner of SARS cov two biology. The senior author is Meike Dittmann's group at N Y U, working with Matthew Frieman at Maryland, and the paper is about a viral protein called ORF eight — one of the accessory proteins in the SARS cov two genome that doesn't get much press. Their finding, in one sentence: ORF eight actively *helps* the virus transmit from one host to another, and it does it by turning on the host's own inflammatory response — not by suppressing it.

Let me set that up carefully, because the biology is genuinely surprising.

SARS cov two has fifteen or so genes. Four of them — spike, envelope, membrane, and nucleocapsid — make the structural pieces of the virus particle. Sixteen more encode the machinery for making new copies of itself. And then there's a handful of what are called *accessory proteins* — small extra proteins that aren't strictly required for viral replication in a dish but seem to be there for interacting with the host. ORF eight is one of those. It's a small secreted protein — the virus makes it and releases it into the space around infected cells — and it has been known for a while to interfere with certain host immune signals. What was *not* known is whether it actually matters for transmission — for the virus getting from one host to the next, which is arguably the only currency evolution actually cares about.

Here's what the group did. They engineered a version of the ancestral SARS cov two — the Wuhan reference strain — with the ORF eight gene deleted. They call it "delta ORF eight." Then they infected infant mice with either the wild-type virus or the delta ORF eight virus. Infant mice are used here because, unlike adult lab mice, infant mice actually transmit SARS cov two between littermates well, which lets you measure not just how much virus you get in a nose swab but whether the mouse gives it to the mouse next to it.

Three main findings. First: mice infected with the delta ORF eight virus had substantially less infectious virus in their upper respiratory tract — that's the nasal cavity and throat — and shed less virus into their nasal secretions. Second: they transmitted the virus much less efficiently to their uninfected cage-mates. Third — and this is the interesting mechanistic part — mice infected with the ORF eight-sufficient virus had a *stronger* upper-airway inflammatory response than mice infected with delta ORF eight. More macrophage recruitment. More inflammatory cytokines. And in particular, more type-one interferon signaling.

Now, type-one interferons — that's I F N alpha and I F N beta — are usually described as the front line of the innate antiviral response. When a cell senses that it's been infected, it dumps interferon into its surroundings, and that hormone tells neighboring cells to switch on hundreds of antiviral genes. It's supposed to *shut down* viral replication. So why would the virus be inducing more of it? The counter-intuitive result is: type-one interferon signaling in the upper airway, in this system, correlates with *more* virus shedding, not less. The authors show two elegant complementary experiments to nail that down. In one direction, they gave the delta ORF eight mice extra interferon beta squirted up the nose — and it partially rescued upper-airway shedding, and it rescued transmission to contacts. In the other direction, they blocked the type-one interferon receptor in the wild-type infected mice — and that reduced shedding and reduced onward transmission. Both directions point the same way: the inflammatory secretory response is not just a host defense, it's also a *transmission enhancer*.

Let me sit with that finding for a second, because it flips a very common frame. We tend to think of respiratory-virus symptoms — runny nose, cough, throat irritation, general inflammatory mucus production — as the *body's response* to the virus. What this paper is arguing is that at least some of what SARS cov two does in the upper airway is engineered — through ORF eight — to *provoke* that inflammatory secretory response, because a nose that's producing more mucus, more macrophages, more inflammatory soup, is a nose that's better at aerosolizing virus into the world. In other words, the sneeze and the drip are not accidents. They're partly the virus's design.

Why does this matter? Two things. One: ORF eight is variable across SARS cov two lineages — some circulating variants have naturally lost ORF eight function, some have variant forms — and if this transmission mechanism is real, then the ORF eight status of a given variant might be as important to how contagious it is as changes in Spike. That's a fresh angle for surveillance. Two: it raises a slightly uncomfortable public-health question about anti-inflammatory treatments in the upper airway during acute infection — could damping the interferon response near the nose actually reduce onward transmission? That's not what the paper argues, and this is one mouse study — but it's the kind of question the mechanism now makes it reasonable to ask.

On the business side. Late Monday, Jazz Pharmaceuticals — which most of you probably know as the company behind Epidiolex, the cannabidiol drug for rare pediatric epilepsies — announced it is acquiring a small privately-held San Diego biotech called Actio Biosciences. The deal is eight hundred and twenty million dollars in cash up front, plus up to another five hundred million in milestone payments, for a potential total of one-point-three-two billion. Expected to close in the fourth quarter of this year. And basically all of that money is for one drug — a Phase two compound called A B S-twelve-thirty — and the biology of what it does is worth walking through.

Let me start with the disease. K C N T one-related epilepsy is a rare — vanishingly rare — genetic form of epilepsy. About two thousand five hundred people in the United States are estimated to have it. It's caused by a gain-of-function mutation in a gene called K C N T one, which encodes a potassium channel found in neurons in the developing brain. And by "gain of function," I mean the channel is stuck too open — potassium flows through it much more freely than it should — and the consequence, paradoxically, is that neurons become *more* excitable, not less.

Here's why. In the standard mental model, potassium channels stabilize neurons: they let positive charge leak out of the cell, which keeps the cell close to a resting negative voltage. So more potassium efflux ought to mean a quieter neuron. But in a subset of inhibitory interneurons — the neurons whose job is to *silence* other neurons — a runaway potassium current makes them fire too fast, exhaust themselves, and drop out of the network. When those inhibitory brakes come off, the excitatory neurons they were holding down go into runaway firing. That's the mechanism people think produces K C N T one seizures. Kids with these mutations tend to have their first seizures within the first few months of life, and they can go on to have dozens or even hundreds of seizure episodes per day, which — as you would expect — is catastrophic for brain development. There are no approved treatments. Existing antiseizure medicines barely work.

A B S-twelve-thirty is a small-molecule inhibitor of the K C N T one channel. First in class. In a small proof-of-concept Phase two study — around fifty-five participants — Actio reported meaningful seizure reductions earlier this year, which is essentially why Jazz just paid a billion dollars for the company. The registrational Phase three is still ahead. But the mechanistic logic is clean and matched to a genetically defined patient population, which is exactly the setup where a rare-disease drug tends to work if it's going to work.

Why does this matter? Three things. One, at the science level: this is a nice example of what people have been calling "precision epilepsy" — pick a specific ion channel mutation, drug that specific channel. There are hundreds of monogenic developmental and epileptic encephalopathies, and the drug industry is starting to pick them off one at a time. Two, at the business level: this is Jazz's second big rare-epilepsy move — they own Epidiolex, they just bought K C N T one — and it tells you where they think their moat is. Epidiolex, which was originally G W Pharma's drug, generates around a billion dollars a year, and Jazz is clearly trying to build the "rare severe epilepsy" franchise. Three: for the families of the two-and-a-half thousand kids in the U S with this condition, this is genuinely important news. The financial terms are eye-catching, but the underlying story is that a first-in-class targeted therapy for an untreatable ultra-rare condition just got picked up by a company with the resources to run it through Phase three and file it.

Now, A I in biomedicine. And this one connects back to yesterday's episode nicely. Yesterday I walked through Silence Therapeutics' Phase two data on divesiran, which is a small interfering R N A — an siRNA — designed to knock down the T M P R S S six messenger R N A in the liver and unleash the master iron regulator hepcidin. Beautiful mechanism, big Phase two effect. And the point of that mechanism is that siRNA drugs are, when they work, extraordinary tools — one shot every three months, knocks down one specific gene product. The catch is that designing them is *hard*. For any given target, you have to find a short — nineteen-to-twenty-three nucleotide — R N A duplex that binds efficiently, silences the target well, doesn't hit anything else in the genome, and survives chemical modification. That process today is mostly still hand-tuned rules plus screening.

A group led by Luhua Lai at Peking University posted a preprint on bioRxiv this week describing an A I system called siDiff that tries to *design* siRNAs de novo — from scratch, target sequence in, siRNA sequence out — using a technique called discrete masked diffusion. Let me define that so it's not just jargon.

Diffusion models are a class of generative A I that most people have encountered through image generation — models like Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, and so on. The basic idea is: start with pure noise, and iteratively refine it toward something structured, using a learned model that at each step is asking "given what this partly-noisy image looks like, what would the *less* noisy version look like?" Discrete diffusion is the same trick but applied to sequences of discrete tokens — for example, the four R N A bases — rather than continuous pixel values. "Masked" means that at each step, you're revealing a bit more of a masked sequence rather than denoising a continuous value. So siDiff starts with a mask over what an siRNA might look like, and then, conditioned on the target messenger R N A, iteratively fills in the bases to produce a candidate siRNA duplex.

The novel piece here is what the authors call "efficacy guidance." Diffusion models by themselves just sample from a distribution — they'll happily generate siRNAs that look plausible but don't actually silence the target well. So they pair the diffusion model with a second model that's been trained to *predict* how well a given siRNA would silence a given target. During generation, the efficacy predictor is used to steer the diffusion sampling toward high-efficacy candidates — the model won't just generate anything, it'll generate things that are plausible *and* the efficacy predictor thinks will work. They add three biology-aware constraints on top: a structural filter to make sure the candidate can actually fold into a functional duplex, dynamic guidance during the unmasking that keeps the sampling on track, and a clustering step at the end to make sure the returned hits aren't ten near-copies of the same sequence.

Tested against four datasets — including patent-derived siRNA design data — siDiff outperformed the existing state-of-the-art models by roughly thirty percent on hit rate. And crucially, it generalized to gene targets that weren't in the training set, which is the failure mode that older siRNA design tools were famously bad at.

Why does this matter? Modestly, at this stage. It's one preprint, one benchmark set, and hit rate on a benchmark is not the same as a drug that works in a patient. But this is real progress on a hard problem — siRNA design has been a bottleneck for the whole space of R N A therapeutics for two decades, and if models like this genuinely raise the hit rate for de novo design against new targets, that shortens the timeline for the next divesiran-like story quite a bit. Something to watch.

And finally — animals. A really lovely fMRI study out of the University of Vienna, published last week in the Cell Press journal iScience, by Raúl Hernández-Pérez and colleagues, working with Laura Cuaya. They wanted to answer a question that dog owners have been arguing about for a very long time — does your dog actually recognize the difference between when you're scared and when you're sad, or is it all just "human mad" versus "human happy" from the dog's point of view?

Here's how you answer that. You train pet dogs to lie still, awake and unrestrained, inside an fMRI scanner while you show them pictures. This is not easy — dog fMRI is a whole field of people who have spent years figuring out how to get border collies to hold their heads still in a magnet — but it's tractable. They ran two experiments. In the first, eight dogs saw images of unfamiliar humans making either happy or neutral faces. In the second, twelve dogs saw images of humans making four different expressions — happy, angry, fearful, and sad — and the team looked at whole-brain activity patterns to see whether the four expressions produced distinguishable brain responses.

First finding: happy faces lit up two regions in the dog brain that other studies have linked to reward — a right-side temporal area and the caudate nucleus. The caudate is worth flagging: it's the part of the striatum that responds to expected reward, and in earlier work it's the region that lights up in dogs when they smell food they like or hear their owner's voice. So the dogs are, to a first approximation, processing a smiling human face the way they process a treat or their owner walking through the door.

Second finding — and this is the world-first part — the whole-brain activity patterns for anger, fear, and sadness were also distinguishable from each other. It's not just "happy versus not happy." The pattern for a fearful human face was different from the pattern for a sad human face, which was different from an angry face. That is the first neurobiological evidence that dogs can tell the negative emotions apart, rather than lumping them into one bucket of "the human is upset."

The nice implication — which the authors put out there gently and which I think is genuinely fair — is that when your dog acts like it knows what mood you're in, it might really know. Not just that you're okay or not okay. But specifically whether you're anxious or downcast. That's the animal news for today.
