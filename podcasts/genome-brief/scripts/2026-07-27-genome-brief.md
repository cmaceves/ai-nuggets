## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Monday, July twenty-seventh, 2026. Five items today. First, a preprint from the Boyd lab at Stanford arguing that the H five N one clade currently circulating in cattle, birds, and mammals has picked up a second way into human cells — not through sialic acid, the way flu is supposed to work, but through the human immune system's own antigen-presentation machinery. Second, on the business side, argenx is paying two point two billion dollars in cash for Forte Biosciences and its anti-C D one twenty-two antibody — a bet that quieting one specific arm of adaptive immunity, while sparing the regulatory arm, can address several autoimmune diseases at once. Third, in AI, a paper introducing a generative model that designs molecular glue degraders — the class of drug behind Revlimid — by learning to hallucinate a plausible three-way protein-drug interface. Fourth, on AI tools, Moonshot released the weights for Kimi K three, a two point eight trillion-parameter open model, over the weekend — the largest open weight release in history and a real option for on-prem biomedical work. And finally, animals — a Science paper on a seventeen-million-year-old jawbone from northern Egypt that pulls the deep roots of the ape family tree out of East Africa and possibly into the north.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

The Boyd lab at Stanford posted a preprint on bioRxiv this week that I think will change how people think about the H five N one clade two point three point four point four b outbreak. To explain why, I need to set up how influenza normally infects a cell.

Every flu virion has a coat protein called hemagglutinin — the H in H five N one. Hemagglutinin's job is to grab onto a target cell and pull the virus in. The target it grabs is a sugar called sialic acid, which is decorated all over the surface of most animal cells. Different flu strains prefer slightly different sialic acid linkages. Bird flus tend to like a linkage called alpha two comma three, which is abundant in bird gut and in the deep human lung. Human flus like an alpha two comma six linkage, which is common in the upper human airway. One of the classical worries about H five N one going pandemic has been that it picks up mutations shifting its preference from alpha two comma three to alpha two comma six, which would make it much more transmissible from person to person.

That's the standard story. Sialic acid is the door. The receptor-binding domain of hemagglutinin is the key. Adaptation to humans means tuning that key.

What the Boyd lab shows is that the current H five N one has a second door. And the second door is not a sugar at all. It's an M H C class two molecule, specifically the human variant called H L A - D R.

Some background on M H C class two. M H C class two molecules are proteins on the surface of a specific subset of your immune cells — dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells. Their normal job is antigen presentation. They pick up fragments of proteins the cell has chewed up, hold those fragments up on the cell surface, and show them to T cells so the adaptive immune system can decide whether they're worth responding to. M H C class two is central to how the immune system sees the world.

A handful of flu viruses have been known to exploit M H C class two as an entry receptor — a few bat flus, and a few unusual human strains. But mainstream avian and human H5 lineages have not done this. Historical H five hemagglutinins in the paper's assays do not bind human M H C class two.

The current clade — clade two point three point four point four b, the one in dairy cattle in North America, in wild birds and poultry globally, in the growing tally of mammalian spillovers — does bind H L A - D R. The paper shows that hemagglutinin from these viruses attaches to primary human immune cells in a way that scales with how much M H C class two those cells display. It shows that the binding is independent of sialic acid — you can strip sialic acids off the cell and hemagglutinin still binds. And it shows that binding is modulated by which H L A - D R allele a person carries. Because H L A alleles vary widely across the human population — that's why organ transplants have to be matched — this means different humans literally present a different receptor surface to this virus. That has implications for who is more or less susceptible to infection, and it introduces a genetic axis to spillover risk that we haven't been tracking.

There are two more findings that make this paper interesting. The first is that the substitutions in hemagglutinin that would classically make H five N one better at binding human-type sialic acid — the mammalian-adaptation mutations everyone watches for — actually reduce M H C class two binding. In other words, the two receptor-usage modes are in tension. As the virus adapts to a human-type sialic acid preference, it partially closes the M H C class two door. The authors read this as evidence that this second entry mode is being remodeled as the virus adapts to humans — not just a coincidence of the current viral geometry.

The second finding is that broadly cross-reactive monoclonal antibodies isolated from people who have never seen H five — H five-naive humans, meaning they have never been exposed to this influenza subtype — can block the interaction between H five hemagglutinin and M H C class two. In other words, pre-existing antibodies from prior human flu exposure carry some capacity to interfere with this new pathway.

What this changes. Pandemic-preparedness risk assessment for H five N one has essentially been organized around one question: is the receptor-binding domain shifting from bird sialic acids to human sialic acids. If a second entry pathway is real, and if it depends on human H L A allele identity, we may need to broaden that surveillance. It also reframes the question of who's at risk. Cellular tropism — meaning which cells the virus can actually infect — is not just about which tissues display alpha two comma six sialic acids. It's also about which cells display M H C class two, which for the current clade means immune cells specifically. That's a very different setpoint for the disease. This is a preprint, and the full body of the paper isn't rendered yet, so we're going off the abstract — but the finding is clean enough, and consequential enough, to flag now.

On the business side.

argenx — the Belgian biopharma best known for Vyvgart in myasthenia gravis — announced this morning it's acquiring Forte Biosciences in an all-cash deal at seventy-seven dollars a share. Total value: about two point two billion dollars. The premium is roughly eighty-six percent above Forte's volume-weighted average price since Forte reported positive Phase 1b vitiligo data on July ninth. The whole deal is being funded from argenx's cash on hand and is expected to close in the third quarter.

What argenx is buying is a single antibody, called F B one oh two. F B one oh two targets C D one twenty-two. To explain why that matters, let me back up.

C D one twenty-two is a receptor subunit on the surface of certain immune cells. It's shared between two cytokine signals: interleukin-two and interleukin-fifteen. Interleukin-two is the signal that drives T cell proliferation — it's a canonical growth signal for the adaptive immune system. Interleukin-fifteen does something related, but with more emphasis on natural killer cells and memory T cells. Both interleukins signal through a receptor that includes C D one twenty-two as its beta chain. So an antibody that blocks C D one twenty-two blocks both interleukin-two and interleukin-fifteen signals — the kinds of signals that keep certain aggressive T and N K cell populations alive and hostile.

Now here's the clever part of the biology, and the reason argenx is willing to pay this much. Not all T cells depend on C D one twenty-two in the same way. Regulatory T cells — Tregs, the calming, tolerogenic arm of adaptive immunity — signal through a different high-affinity interleukin-two receptor configuration that includes a subunit called C D twenty-five. That configuration is much more sensitive to interleukin-two, and it is relatively spared by a C D one twenty-two antibody. Pathogenic, tissue-attacking effector T cells and N K cells, on the other hand, rely much more on the C D one twenty-two-driven signals. So the pharmacological hope is that F B one oh two turns down the population you don't want — the effector cells destroying pigment-producing melanocytes in vitiligo, or the ones damaging the intestinal lining in celiac disease — while leaving the regulatory arm largely alone. That would be a real advance over the sledgehammer of broadly immunosuppressing patients.

The clinical evidence, so far, is early. On July ninth, Forte reported that the Phase 1b vitiligo trial hit statistical significance on its primary endpoint. Effect sizes and detailed re-pigmentation numbers were not disclosed in this morning's argenx announcement — we're working off the top-line. A separate Phase 1b in celiac disease had already produced positive data last year, and Phase 2 celiac data is expected in the second half of this year. Forte and argenx are also talking about alopecia areata and other autoimmune indications where C D one twenty-two-driven effector cells matter. argenx's framing is what they call "pipeline in a product" — one antibody, several diseases, if the mechanism holds.

What to take away from this deal. First, the size of the premium — eighty-six percent above a share price that had itself just re-rated on the vitiligo readout — tells you argenx is willing to pay a lot for early clinical validation of a novel mechanism. Second, argenx has essentially been converted from a strategic investor in Forte, which they were, into an owner in about four months. And third, the mechanism itself — selectively muting effector T and N K cells while sparing Tregs — is one of the more attractive frames in autoimmunity right now. Whether F B one oh two delivers on it clinically is what the celiac Phase 2 readout will tell us.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A paper posted to arXiv this week from a group working on generative models introduces something called TriGlue. It's a generative model for designing molecular glues. Molecular glues are one of the most interesting drug modalities to come out of the last decade, and I want to spend a minute explaining what they are before I explain why the design problem is hard.

Your cells maintain protein levels through a garbage-disposal system called the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. When a protein needs to go, a class of enzymes called E three ubiquitin ligases tags it with a chain of a small protein called ubiquitin, and the proteasome — a large barrel-shaped protease inside the cell — chews the tagged protein up. E three ligases normally recognize specific target proteins through direct interactions.

A molecular glue is a small drug molecule that doesn't behave like a classical inhibitor. It doesn't block an active site. Instead, it sits between an E three ligase and a target protein that the ligase would not normally recognize, and forces them into contact — a new protein-protein interface, held together by the drug. Once the two proteins are stuck together, the ligase tags the target with ubiquitin, and the target gets degraded. The classical example is thalidomide, and its descendants lenalidomide — Revlimid — and pomalidomide, which glue the E three ligase cereblon onto specific transcription factors and drive their degradation, killing multiple myeloma cells. The whole class of drugs is called targeted protein degraders, and molecular glues are one of the two main flavors.

The design problem is genuinely hard, and this is what TriGlue is going after. In classical drug design, you have a target protein with a defined binding pocket, and you design a small molecule to fit that pocket. That's what basically every structure-based drug design workflow, from docking to protein-ligand generative models, is optimized for. Molecular glue design isn't like that. You need to simultaneously design a small molecule that, in the presence of two specific proteins, induces an entirely new protein-protein interface that doesn't otherwise exist. So you're solving three problems at once: what does the molecule look like, what does the induced protein-protein interface look like, and how are the two proteins geometrically arranged when the glue is holding them together.

TriGlue decomposes this into two coupled stages. The first stage is called interface estimation. Given the two unbound protein structures — the E three ligase and the target — a symmetry-aware neural network, one that respects the fact that rotations and translations of the whole system shouldn't change the physics, predicts a geometrically constrained candidate interface: where on each protein's surface the interaction would sit if a glue could be found to enforce it. This is done without ever generating a molecule. It's a pure geometric prediction about where the interface could plausibly be.

The second stage is called interface-conditioned ternary flow matching. Flow matching is a generative modeling technique that learns to transport samples smoothly from a simple noise distribution to a target distribution. In this application, the target distribution is the ternary complex — the arrangement of the two proteins plus a chemically valid glue between them. Conditioning on the estimated interface from stage one, the network jointly generates the molecule and predicts the rigid-body transformation that assembles the two proteins around it.

The authors show the model produces chemically valid molecules and plausible ternary complexes across the evaluation suite. This is a proof-of-concept paper — it does not claim to reproduce known glues out of the box, and I would not expect these designs to be experimental candidates yet — but it's an important architectural step. Almost every previous generative-model attempt at glue design either fixed the interface up front, based on a known example, or generated the molecule without simultaneously reasoning about the protein-protein assembly. TriGlue is one of the first attempts to model all three components jointly, and to do it with the geometric-symmetry constraints that this kind of problem actually requires. For a working scientist using generative models on degrader chemistry, this is a template worth looking at.

On AI tools.

Late Sunday U T C, the Chinese lab Moonshot pushed the weights for Kimi K three to Hugging Face — a day ahead of its own announced schedule. Kimi K three is a two point eight trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts language model with a one million token context window. It's the largest open-weight language model that has ever been released.

A quick explanation of mixture of experts. A dense two point eight trillion-parameter model would need enough hardware to activate every parameter on every token — which, for a model this big, would mean many thousands of the highest-end accelerators just to run inference. A mixture of experts, by contrast, splits the network into many small "expert" sub-networks and only routes a token through a handful of them. In Kimi K three's case, the model has eight hundred and ninety-six experts total, and each token activates sixteen of them. Which means the effective compute per token is much smaller than the raw parameter count suggests, even though the model still has the representational capacity of the full two point eight trillion parameters.

On performance, Moonshot's own numbers put Kimi K three behind Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 and OpenAI's G P T five point six Sol on aggregate — the current U S frontier — but ahead of Claude Opus four point eight and G P T five point five on coding and agentic benchmarks. Independent evaluators have it leading on frontend code generation. It's not a frontier-toppling model, but it's the first time an openly-released model is credibly in the same neighborhood as the closed frontier for those workloads.

Why this matters for biomedicine specifically. Frontier closed models can't be run on-premises. If you're doing anything with patient data — clinical text, electronic health records, protected genomic data — sending it to a hosted A P I is often not compliant with H I P A A or with institutional data-governance rules, and even where it is compliant, it puts you in the position of trusting a third party with sensitive data. Open weights let you host the model inside your own compute perimeter. Kimi K three under a modified M I T license, at frontier-adjacent quality on coding and agentic tasks, is the first model of its class that is a genuine option for that kind of workload. It's also enormous — the compressed weights are around one point four terabytes — so it's not going to run on a laptop. But for large academic and clinical compute environments, it's a real option to look at.

And finally, animals.

A team from Mansoura University's Vertebrate Paleontology Center in Egypt and the University of Southern California published a paper in Science last week describing a new species of ancient ape. They're calling it Masripithecus moghraensis. The specimen is a fragment of lower jaw, with worn teeth, recovered from a site called Wadi Moghra in northern Egypt during fieldwork in 2023 and 2024. It dates to about seventeen to eighteen million years ago — the Early Miocene.

A little context on why this fossil is interesting. For a long time, the mainstream story about where the great apes came from placed the deep root of the family firmly in East Africa. The Miocene of East Africa — Kenya, Uganda, Ethiopia — has produced most of the ape fossils in the twenty-plus-million-year window that matters. And the assumption was that early apes evolved there and radiated out from there.

Masripithecus is a problem for that story on two counts. The first is geographic — it's in northern Egypt, not East Africa. Northern Egypt at seventeen million years ago wasn't the desert it is today; it was a forested coastal margin along the ancient Tethys Sea. But it's still not the region the East African cradle model would predict for the deepest node of the ape family tree. The second is anatomical. The morphology of the jaw and the teeth — the shape of the molars, the geometry of the tooth root arrangement, the details of the enamel — places this animal closer to the common ancestor of the living apes than any East African contemporary the authors compared it to. In other words, this is a jaw from the wrong place, at more or less the right time, that looks more like it belongs to your family than the East African fossils do.

The authors argue that this reopens a question the field had mostly stopped asking. Instead of a single East African cradle, they propose that the deep origins of the ape lineage were split between North Africa and the Middle East — a region that at the time was a forested corridor between Africa and Eurasia — and that East Africa was one of several places apes ended up rather than the place they started from. That reframes the biogeography of an entire family, though from one lower jaw, so the caveat is severe: fossil apes from this window are rare, and one specimen can rearrange the tree only until the next specimen comes in.

What I like about this paper — and this is a general point about paleontology in the M R I and micro-C T era — is how much sample-poor deep-time work is now being redone as scanning technology gets better. Fossils that were cataloged forty years ago as fragmentary and unassignable are turning out to have anatomical detail that lets you place them, when you look inside the bone rather than just at the outside. Whether Masripithecus is the missing common ancestor or a promising cousin will depend on more specimens. But the Miocene of North Africa just got a lot more interesting.
