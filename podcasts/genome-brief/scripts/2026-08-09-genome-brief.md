## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Sunday, August ninth, twenty-twenty-six. Four items today. First, on pathogen watch — a preprint out this week on bioRxiv from a group at the European Bioinformatics Institute takes on a problem that has quietly haunted the pandemic sequence archive since the beginning: contamination. They built a tool called PhyCD that uses the pandemic's own phylogenetic tree to flag SARS cov two consensus genomes whose mutation patterns don't fit where they sit on the tree, ran it over nearly five million public genomes, and flagged just under eleven thousand of them as putatively contaminated — masking roughly sixty-five thousand suspect substitutions in the process. Second, on the business side — a Los-Angeles biotech called Latigo Biotherapeutics went public on Nasdaq this week, raising three hundred forty-six million dollars at eighteen dollars a share to bankroll a class of non-opioid painkillers that block a specific sodium channel called Nav one-point-eight — the same target Vertex hit last year with Journavx, and the same one Eli Lilly paid up to a billion dollars for when it bought SiteOne Therapeutics. Third, on AI in biomedicine — a group affiliated with the Nuclear Threat Initiative posted a bioRxiv preprint this week proposing the first serious biosecurity framework for the new generation of A-I protein-binder design tools. The key move is philosophical: instead of screening the designed molecule after the fact, screen the target the user asked the model to bind. They release a curated database of over fourteen thousand potentially harmful human proteins classified by biosecurity risk, and show that a modern protein language model can flag risky requests with over ninety-seven percent accuracy. And finally, animals — researchers in Costa Rica have described a new species of tree frog that lives its whole life inside coffee plantations, sings from the coffee shrubs at night by inflating a translucent throat pouch, and is being called the "coffee frog." On with the show.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

The publicly deposited SARS cov two sequence archive is, quietly, one of the most valuable scientific resources ever built. As of this year it contains something on the order of five million consensus genomes from all over the world, and essentially every downstream claim about SARS cov two evolution — how variants arose, how lineages spread, when Omicron emerged, whether a new subvariant has a growth advantage — is drawn from that archive. Consensus genomes, to define the term, are what you get when you sequence a clinical sample many times over and, at each position in the genome, you pick the most common nucleotide the sequencer saw. That single consensus sequence is what gets uploaded to GISAID and to public databases and is what every phylogenetic tree of the pandemic is built from.

The problem the paper posted this week on bioRxiv, from a group led by Nicola De Maio and Nick Goldman at the European Bioinformatics Institute in Cambridge, tackles is that some of those consensus genomes are wrong. Specifically, they are contaminated. In a real diagnostic lab, samples are prepared in batches — dozens or hundreds of clinical swabs sequenced side by side in the same run — and a small amount of genetic material from one sample can end up in the sequencing reads assigned to another. If the contamination is high enough, the consensus caller will pick a base at some positions that came from the contaminant rather than from the sample's real virus. The result is a consensus genome that looks like a real virus but carries a small number of spurious mutations. Feed that genome into a phylogenetic tree of the pandemic, and it looks like a lineage with a strange combination of mutations — sometimes flagged as a potential recombinant, sometimes as an unusually saltational branch, sometimes just as noise. Multiply this by a few thousand contaminated genomes across five million, and you start distorting downstream inferences: which mutations are under selection, which lineages are recombinants, how fast the virus is evolving in a given region.

The approach they take, called PhyCD — phylogeny-aided contamination detection — is clever, and worth walking through. Every consensus genome has an associated *depth-of-coverage* profile — that is, at each position in the genome, how many sequencing reads actually covered that position. Real SARS cov two samples have relatively even coverage across the roughly thirty-thousand-base genome; drops in coverage at specific positions can signal problems in the sequencing that make those positions unreliable. So step one of PhyCD is: mask out positions where coverage looks suspicious. Step two is the phylogenetic move. There are now scalable methods — pandemic-scale phylogenetic placement techniques developed for exactly this archive — that can drop a new genome onto the existing global tree in constant time and ask, essentially, does this genome fit somewhere sensibly, or does it sit awkwardly on the tree with a strange combination of mutations that don't co-occur naturally? A genuinely contaminated consensus tends to have a small number of mutations that were "borrowed" from a different clade — different variant, different geographic origin — and those mutations don't belong on the branch where the rest of the genome places the sample. PhyCD looks for exactly that mismatch pattern.

Applied to nearly five million SARS cov two genomes, PhyCD flagged just under eleven thousand of them as putatively contaminated under conservative parameters, and identified roughly sixty-five thousand individual substitutions — that is, spurious mutations — that ought to be masked before those genomes are used downstream. To put the numbers in context: eleven thousand out of five million is a small fraction, about zero-point-two percent. But sixty-five thousand spurious substitutions is a lot of noise for anyone trying to detect small selective signals or reconstruct fine-scale transmission trees.

Two things worth pulling out. First, going off the abstract here — the full preprint body is not yet rendered on bioRxiv so I have not read the wet-lab and false-positive-rate detail in full. That means I cannot yet tell you how the authors validated that a flagged genome actually is contaminated versus, for example, a genuinely fast-evolving lineage that just happens to look phylogenetically weird. That distinction matters — if the false-positive rate is high, PhyCD might inadvertently mask real signal from long-term-infection lineages and cross-species transmission events, which are exactly the interesting biology. Second, and more broadly: this is the kind of infrastructural work that doesn't get headlines but that everyone downstream benefits from. If the pandemic-scale archives get cleaned this way — with a reproducible, publicly available tool that anyone re-analyzing the data can rerun — the entire evolutionary literature that comes out of that archive gets more trustworthy overnight. It is not glamorous, but it is exactly the kind of paper we ought to be paying attention to.

On the business side.

On Thursday of this week, a Los Angeles biotech called Latigo Biotherapeutics went public on Nasdaq under the ticker L-T-G-O. They sold nineteen-point-two million shares at eighteen dollars a share, for gross proceeds of three hundred forty-six million dollars — one of the largest biotech I-P-Os of the year. That number is worth pausing on. Latigo is the fourteenth biotech to raise more than two hundred fifty million dollars in a public offering in twenty-twenty-six alone. Fourteen. That equals the entire count of large biotech I-P-Os over the previous four years combined. The window that essentially closed in twenty-twenty-two has come back this year with force.

The reason Latigo is worth understanding, though, isn't the I-P-O size — it is the target. Their lead compound, called L-T-G dash zero-zero-one, is an oral small-molecule inhibitor of a voltage-gated sodium channel called Nav one-point-eight. Let me define what that is, because the whole non-opioid pain thesis in biotech right now hinges on it.

Pain signals in the human body start at the nerve endings — the peripheral neurons that sit in your skin, your organs, your gut, and detect damage. Those neurons fire electrical signals up to the spinal cord and then the brain, and it is those signals that the brain interprets as pain. The electrical firing is driven by voltage-gated sodium channels — proteins in the neuron's membrane that open when the membrane voltage crosses a threshold, let sodium ions rush in, and drive the electrical spike. There are ten different sodium-channel subtypes in humans, called Nav one-point-one through Nav one-point-nine. Most of them are expressed all over the body — in the heart, in the brain, in your muscles — which is why classic broad sodium-channel blockers like lidocaine work for anesthesia but can't be given systemically at pain-blocking doses without dangerous cardiac and central-nervous-system side effects. Nav one-point-eight is different. It is expressed almost exclusively in the peripheral pain-sensing neurons and essentially nowhere else. So if you can build a small molecule that blocks Nav one-point-eight specifically and doesn't touch the other Nav subtypes, in principle you shut down pain-signal transmission from the periphery without affecting the brain, the heart, or motor neurons — and, critically, without touching the opioid receptors that drive addiction.

That is the exact thesis Vertex proved out with their drug Journavx — chemical name suzetrigine — which the F-D-A approved in January of last year, twenty-twenty-five, for moderate-to-severe acute pain. It was the first genuinely new mechanism of pain drug approved in the United States in more than twenty years, and it did about ninety million dollars in first-year sales — respectable but not blockbuster, mostly because it faces the usual reimbursement inertia against a decades-old class of cheap opioid alternatives. But Vertex was the proof of concept: you can hit Nav one-point-eight selectively, and it does relieve pain, and it doesn't cause euphoria or physical dependence. Eli Lilly then paid up to a billion dollars to acquire a company called SiteOne Therapeutics, which had its own next-generation Nav one-point-eight program aimed at chronic pain. And now Latigo — staffed largely by neuroscience alumni of Amgen's pain program and funded initially by the venture firm Westlake BioPartners — has raised three hundred forty-six million dollars in public markets to enter the same race.

Latigo's lead, L-T-G dash zero-zero-one, has already run a Phase two trial in patients recovering from abdominoplasty — tummy-tuck surgery — and hit its primary endpoint, called S-P-I-D forty-eight, which is essentially the sum of a patient's pain-intensity scores over the first forty-eight hours after surgery. Beating placebo on S-P-I-D forty-eight is the same benchmark suzetrigine used. Their next step is a placebo-controlled Phase three trial in patients undergoing bunionectomy — foot surgery — which is the standard acute-pain model regulators want to see. They have two other candidates behind L-T-G dash zero-zero-one, though the company has not publicly named their targets.

Two things worth taking from this. First, the fact that four companies — Vertex, Lilly, Latigo, and behind them a handful of smaller biotechs — are all now running late-stage Nav one-point-eight programs is significant. This target went from a genetics-inspired hypothesis a decade ago — inherited Nav one-point-eight loss-of-function makes people insensitive to pain, inherited gain-of-function causes chronic pain syndromes — to a validated drug class with an approved medicine in about ten years. That is fast. Second, the market shape is interesting: the non-opioid acute-pain space is not a rare-disease niche, it is enormous. Every year in the U-S alone, tens of millions of prescriptions are written for post-surgical opioids. A safe, non-addictive oral alternative that actually works has a very large addressable market, and it is not obvious yet whether the winner will be first-mover Vertex or a next-generation entrant with better pharmacology. Latigo's I-P-O is the market betting on the latter.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A short but genuinely important preprint went up on bioRxiv this week from a group at the Nuclear Threat Initiative — the arms-control nonprofit — proposing what they think a serious biosecurity framework should look like for the new generation of A-I protein-design tools. It is titled "Safety First: Input Screening for Protein Design Tools," and the authors are Palmer, Teran, Wheeler, and Yassif.

Context first, because this is a fast-moving corner of the field. Over the last two years there has been a step-change in what generative A-I can do with proteins. Tools like RFdiffusion, from David Baker's group at the University of Washington, and its successors — Chroma, Boltz-design, and others — can now take as input a description of a protein you want to bind and design, from scratch, a new small protein that binds it. You give the model a target, say a specific human receptor, and it hands back designs for what are essentially custom antibodies-in-miniature. This is a real capability, and it is being used productively today for therapeutics — designing binders against difficult drug targets — and for research.

The biosecurity concern is the flip side. If a model can design a binder against any human protein you ask it to, then a bad actor could in principle ask it to design a binder against, say, a specific human immune-system component with the goal of disabling it. Traditional biosecurity screening — the kind D-N-A synthesis companies have been running for decades — works by comparing the sequence a customer has ordered against a database of known dangerous sequences like botulinum toxin, and refusing to ship if there's a strong sequence match. That approach fundamentally breaks in a world where generative models design molecules with completely novel sequences that don't match anything in the database. You could ask a generative binder model to bind a dangerous target, and the sequence it hands you back would sail past every homology-based filter that exists.

The authors' proposal in this preprint is a genuinely new idea, and it is worth walking through. Instead of screening the *binder* — the output — screen the *target* — the input. The user has to tell the model what to bind, and that target *is* a natural sequence: a real human protein or pathogen protein pulled from public databases. So you screen that input, before the model does anything. And what you screen for is not sequence identity — you screen for *functional* relatedness to known-dangerous targets.

To do the screening, they build two things. First, they curate a database of fourteen-thousand-five-hundred-forty-one potentially harmful human proteoforms — that is about seven percent of the human proteome — classified by biosecurity risk level. Proteoforms rather than genes, because different splice variants of the same gene can have very different biological roles. Second, they run those targets through a modern protein language model called E-S-M dash C — the C standing for their latest-generation *Cambrian* release from Evolutionary Scale — and compare each user-submitted target's embedding, in the model's internal representation space, against the embeddings of the flagged proteoforms. A protein language model, if you haven't come across the term before, is essentially a large transformer trained on hundreds of millions of protein sequences that learns to represent each protein as a numeric vector — an "embedding" — where proteins with similar function land near each other in vector space, even if their raw sequences look nothing alike.

The result: E-S-M dash C-based screening achieves an F-one score above ninety-seven percent for detecting variants of known dangerous targets, roughly on par with a classical sequence-homology tool called BLASTP. But — and this is the important asymmetry — E-S-M dash C is much better than BLASTP at telling apart *benign* mutations from *damaging* ones of the same protein, because the language model has learned functional signal that raw sequence alignment cannot capture. In other words, if a user submits a slightly mutated version of a dangerous protein designed to slip past a homology filter, the language model still catches it, because the language model has learned what the protein *does*, not just what it looks like.

They then run the screener on a range of real-world protein datasets to see how disruptive it would be to normal research. The flagging rates are calibrated in a way I found interesting. Random bacterial, fungal, plant, and viral proteins get flagged less than about one percent of the time. But when they weight human proteins by how frequently each is cited in the scientific literature — the intuition being that heavily studied proteins are the ones people actually query — the flagging rate is twenty-three percent for human and twenty percent for mouse. And when they look at the targets of currently-issued antibody patents — a good proxy for "commercially valuable therapeutic targets" — sixty-three percent are dual-use, meaning they are simultaneously legitimate drug targets *and* potentially harmful if bound the wrong way. That is a striking number. It says that a large fraction of the most valuable therapeutic targets are exactly the ones a biosecurity framework has to worry about — because the same protein is a legitimate drug target for one indication and a potential misuse target for another.

The authors' conclusion is that a hard block-list won't work here, because you'd shut down a large fraction of legitimate research. They propose something closer to a *tiered trusted-access* framework: high-risk targets get flagged, and the flagged requests get routed to a verification layer — proof of institutional affiliation, review of the intended use — before the generative model produces designs. In effect, KYC for A-I protein designers.

Two things I'd take from this. First, going off the abstract — I have not read the full body yet, in particular the details of how they defined "harmful" proteoforms, which is going to be politically load-bearing. Different bodies will draw that line differently, and the specific proteoforms on the list matter a lot for the false-positive rate on legitimate research. That is the paper to actually read when the full text renders. Second — and this is the broader point — the biosecurity framework for A-I biology has been essentially non-existent up to now, mostly gestured at with hand-waving about voluntary norms. This preprint is one of the first concrete, deployable, empirically-tested proposals I have seen, and it comes from a group with genuine credibility on the arms-control side. Whether or not this specific framework becomes the standard, the concept — screen the target, not the binder — is likely to stick. It is a much more scalable approach than trying to catch novel outputs, and it is easier to implement without slowing down legitimate research.

And finally, animals.

Researchers at the University of Costa Rica, working with Argentina's National Council for Scientific and Technical Research — known as CONICET — have described a new species of tree frog, endemic to the mountains of Costa Rica's central Pacific region, that lives exclusively inside coffee plantations. They are calling it the "coffee frog," or in Spanish, the "spring frog," and it was described in a paper this week.

The frog is small — under four centimeters, so about an inch and a half — and dark green with black spots. It is nocturnal. What is charming about it is the behavior: at night, the males climb up onto the coffee shrubs and call for mates by inflating a translucent throat pouch that lights up with each short, high-pitched note. That behavior, incidentally, is not a curiosity but the whole reason the species was discovered — before it was described, its call was the only sign it existed, and its call turned out not to match any known frog in the region.

The habitat story is the interesting ecological piece. This frog is what biologists call *anthropophilic* — it thrives specifically in a human-modified environment, in this case shaded coffee farms. Coffee shrubs, particularly the shade-grown variety, create a humid understory with moderate temperatures that seems to match what these frogs would have historically found in the region's natural cloud-forest fragments. Coffee plantations, in effect, are functioning as a partial substitute habitat for a species whose original forest has largely been cut down.

The catch is what happens to the tadpoles. Frog tadpoles need standing water — small pools, drainage ditches, irrigation reservoirs — and they are extremely sensitive to chemical runoff. The scientific team is warning that pesticides applied on the coffee farms can travel through the air, seep into the soil, and reach the aquatic microhabitats the tadpoles depend on. So the same coffee plantations that provide the adult frogs a place to live are also the source of the biggest threat to the next generation. The recommendation is that farmers in the Tarrazú coffee region — which produces some of the best-known coffee beans in the world — should consider reducing pesticide use in the specific micro-drainages where these tadpoles develop.

Two small things. First, this is a nice reminder that new species descriptions in relatively well-studied regions like Costa Rica are still happening — usually because someone actually goes and sits in a habitat at night and listens, rather than relying on a genetic sample from a museum drawer. There is still an enormous amount of undescribed vertebrate diversity out there, and the majority of it is in the tropics. Second, the specific dependence of this animal on shade-grown coffee is a real-time example of how agricultural practices at the individual-farm level can determine whether a species persists or disappears. Shade-grown coffee is often marketed as bird-friendly. It looks like it is frog-friendly too — provided the pesticide runoff stays out of the tadpole ponds.
