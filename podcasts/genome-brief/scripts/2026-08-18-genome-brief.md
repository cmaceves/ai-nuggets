## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Tuesday, August eighteenth, twenty twenty six. Today: a preprint out of Texas Biomedical Research Institute compares, side by side in ferrets, the two H five N one genotypes that have caused the first human infections in the United States — and it finds that they are on very different evolutionary paths, with real implications for what "pandemic potential" means for each of them. And to close: researchers in Queensland catch wild bottlenose dolphins hunting fish with giant sea shells, and film a mother teaching her calf how to do it, in only the second population on earth known to have invented the trick.

Let's get into it.

First, pathogen watch.

A group led out of the Texas Biomedical Research Institute and the University of Texas at Austin, with collaborators at Mount Sinai and the Friedrich-Loeffler-Institute in Germany, posted a preprint yesterday comparing head-to-head, in ferrets, the two U.S. human H five N one cases from twenty twenty four — the Texas dairy-worker case and the Louisiana backyard-flock case. I want to flag upfront that I'm working off the abstract here; the full body of the preprint isn't rendered yet on bioRxiv. But this comparison is important enough that even the abstract is worth walking through carefully.

Here's the context. Since twenty twenty, the H five N one clade two point three point four point four b — that's a specific genetic lineage of the highly pathogenic avian influenza virus — has been sweeping through wild birds worldwide, and increasingly into mammals. In the United States, this clade has split into a bunch of internal genotypes named alphabetically by the U S D A. Two of them matter most right now. Genotype B three point one three is the one that got into U S dairy cattle in early twenty twenty four and has been circulating in cows ever since — that's the bovine strain, and it produced the first human case in Texas, a dairy worker with conjunctivitis. Genotype D one point one is the one dominant in wild birds and in commercial poultry outbreaks this season, and it produced the second confirmed human case in Louisiana — a backyard poultry exposure that resulted in a much more severe hospitalization. So the field has been asking, essentially forever: are these two genotypes equally dangerous, or is one of them more mammal-adapted than the other?

The preprint takes both viruses, isolated directly from the Texas and Louisiana patients, and puts them through a ferret transmission study. Ferrets are the standard mammalian model for influenza — their upper-respiratory-tract receptor biology mirrors ours closely enough that ferret-to-ferret contact transmission is treated as the best proxy we have for pandemic potential in humans.

The result is genuinely surprising. In ferrets, the Texas B three point one three virus — the dairy-cattle-derived strain — produced more severe clinical disease, higher viral shedding, more extensive lung pathology, and stronger evidence of contact transmission than the Louisiana D one point one virus. That's the opposite of what you'd predict from the human clinical outcomes alone, where the D one point one Louisiana patient was much sicker.

Now here's the interesting part, and this is where I want you to hold two ideas in your head at once. When the authors sequenced the viruses from the infected ferrets, the Texas B three point one three virus stayed genetically stable. It didn't need to acquire new mutations to transmit well in mammals, because it apparently already had them — the adaptation to a mammalian upper respiratory tract was baked in from its time in cows. In contrast, the Louisiana D one point one virus rapidly picked up a mutation called E six two seven K in its polymerase complex during the ferret infection, and the one contact ferret that died had both E six two seven K and Q one nine four K — two mammalian-adaptive mutations.

Let me explain what those mutations do, because they are two of the most-watched positions in influenza surveillance. Influenza's polymerase — the enzyme that copies the virus's RNA genome inside an infected cell — has three subunits. One of them, called PB two, evolved in birds to function efficiently at bird body temperature and to work with a bird host factor called ANP thirty two A. In mammals, the temperature is different and the ANP thirty two A protein has a slightly different structure. To replicate efficiently in a mammalian cell, the virus has to change its polymerase to adapt. The E six two seven K mutation — swapping glutamic acid for lysine at position six twenty seven of PB two — is the single most famous mammalian-adaptation marker in avian influenza. It makes the polymerase work better with mammalian ANP thirty two A, and it has shown up over and over in every mammalian-transmitted avian flu virus we've ever tracked. Q one nine four K is a related enabling mutation. In the abstract, the authors ran computational analyses that suggest both mutations enhance interactions with mammalian ANP thirty two A and B.

So the story is: the Texas B three point one three virus is already at the top of the mammalian-adaptation staircase — it doesn't need to change. The Louisiana D one point one virus is not there yet, but it can climb the staircase quickly. Which one you'd think is scarier depends on how you frame the question. B three point one three is scarier today because it's the version of H five N one currently circulating in the country's dairy herds — a permanent mammalian reservoir where cross-species jumps to farm workers are a routine occurrence. D one point one is scarier as a trajectory, because it's the version dominant in wild birds and commercial poultry across the continent, and every time it infects a mammal it has the opportunity to pick up the exact mutations we just watched it acquire in ferrets.

For the surveillance world, this is a useful finding. It says: watch both genotypes, but weight them differently. And it argues for continuing to look hard at the mammalian-adaptive markers in every H five N one sequence coming out of clinical, wildlife, and livestock samples — because those markers really do map onto transmissibility, and the ferret work here is direct experimental evidence for that.

While we're on pathogens, one brief note: the WHO International Health Regulations emergency committee is meeting today to review the status of the Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which is the outbreak we covered in depth two days ago. That outbreak is now above four thousand nine hundred cumulative cases with a case fatality ratio still holding around forty-six percent, and today's meeting will decide whether to maintain, upgrade, or downgrade its Public Health Emergency of International Concern designation. If a decision drops later today, we'll cover it in tomorrow's episode.

On the business side.

argenx, the Belgian-Dutch antibody-engineering company, reported topline results yesterday from a Phase three trial called ALKIVIA of its drug efgartigimod — brand name VYVGART Hytrulo — in autoimmune myositis. The trial hit its primary endpoint in the overall population, and it hit cleanly in the immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy subgroup, which is a disease with no approved therapies today. It missed formal statistical significance in dermatomyositis, though the effect size was essentially identical. So it's a nuanced result and it's worth understanding the drug, the disease, and the numbers.

Let me start with the disease. Myositis, in general, is a group of autoimmune diseases in which the body's immune system attacks skeletal muscle. Two important subtypes are dermatomyositis — where you also get a characteristic skin rash — and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, which is the aggressive muscle-only form that causes rapid, sometimes irreversible muscle destruction and is driven by IgG-class autoantibodies against things like the HMGCR enzyme and the SRP signal recognition particle. Standard of care is chronic high-dose steroids plus immunosuppression, which brings all the toxicity you'd expect. And for immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy specifically, no drug has ever been approved.

Now the drug. Efgartigimod is a first-in-class biologic that targets the neonatal Fc receptor — abbreviated FcRn. FcRn is not, despite the name, a receptor only in newborns; it's expressed throughout life on cells that handle antibodies, especially endothelial cells and monocytes. Its job is to keep IgG antibodies from being degraded. When an IgG antibody gets taken up into a cell, the acidic environment of the endosome makes it bind FcRn, and instead of being sent to the lysosome for destruction, it gets recycled back out to the bloodstream. This is why IgG has such an unusually long serum half-life, around three weeks. Efgartigimod is an engineered Fc fragment that binds FcRn much more tightly than natural IgG does, at both acidic and neutral pH. It occupies the receptor. That means normal IgG in the body can no longer be recycled — it gets degraded on schedule instead of rescued — and total IgG levels in the blood drop by roughly sixty to seventy percent. If your disease is driven by pathogenic IgG autoantibodies — as myositis is — that reduction in total IgG proportionately reduces the pathogenic fraction too. That's the mechanism. It's already approved for myasthenia gravis and for a nerve disorder called CIDP, and myositis is the next indication argenx has been chasing.

The ALKIVIA trial randomized one hundred seventy-five adults, one-to-one, to efgartigimod or placebo, on top of a protocol-mandated corticosteroid taper. The primary endpoint was the Total Improvement Score at week fifty-two — a composite of six standard measures of muscle strength, everyday physical function, and disease activity beyond the muscle. In the combined myositis population, the efgartigimod arm improved by an average of forty-seven point nine five points, versus thirty-two point five six points for placebo — a fifteen point four point treatment difference, with a p value of zero point zero zero one one. Separation from placebo started as early as week four and held through the full year, even as patients were tapered off steroids.

Then the subgroup story. In the immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy subgroup — the one with no approved therapies — the treatment difference was fourteen point eight points, p value zero point zero zero four eight. Clean win. In the dermatomyositis subgroup, the treatment difference was fourteen point five points — essentially identical in magnitude — but the p value was zero point one zero nine. It didn't clear significance. The company's read, and I think it's a defensible one, is that the DM subgroup was simply too small to power. Same effect size, wider confidence interval.

Safety was consistent with the drug's existing label. Efgartigimod has been on the market for myasthenia gravis for over three years now, so the safety profile is well-characterized: mild infusion-site reactions, some increase in mild infections consistent with reduced IgG, no unexpected signals.

Why it matters. Two things. First, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy patients now have a mechanism-of-action-appropriate drug on a clear path to approval. Second, this is another indication where the FcRn-antagonism strategy has cleanly hit a Phase three primary endpoint, and it strengthens a broader industry bet — argenx, Johnson and Johnson with nipocalimab, UCB with rozanolixizumab — that a lot of IgG-driven autoimmune disease can be treated by pulling down total IgG. Every clean win in that class validates the whole thesis.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A group at Caltech and UCLA, led by Matt Thomson and Long Cai, posted a preprint yesterday that I think is worth walking through carefully — even though I'm going to flag, again, that I'm working from the abstract because the full body text isn't yet rendered on bioRxiv. The paper takes on a hard clinical prediction problem in brain cancer, does it with a spatial multi-omics dataset and a foundation model, and gets an answer that essentially says everyone has been looking in the wrong place.

Here's the context. Isocitrate dehydrogenase mutant gliomas — usually called IDH-mutant gliomas — are a category of brain tumor that starts out relatively indolent, low grade, sometimes stable for years. But a substantial fraction of them eventually undergo what pathologists call malignant transformation: they turn into aggressive, high-grade, fast-growing tumors. Predicting which patient will transform and when — before it happens — is one of the most important unsolved clinical questions in neuro-oncology, because it determines whether you monitor conservatively or you intervene early with something more aggressive. And for decades the assumption has been that the answer must live in the genetics — you scan the tumor genome for high-risk mutations or copy-number changes, and those tell you the trajectory.

What the Caltech-UCLA group did is take biopsies from eighteen retrospective glioma patients — some who stayed stable, some who eventually underwent malignant transformation, some who had already transformed — and profile them with integrated spatial DNA and spatial RNA imaging. The dataset is dense: more than six hundred thousand individual cells profiled, nine hundred sixty-two DNA loci measured per cell, and one thousand one hundred fifty RNAs, with the exact spatial location of every cell preserved so you know which cells are neighbors.

Then they ran the analysis you'd expect first. Does the risk of malignant transformation live in copy number changes in the tumor DNA? No. Does it live in the composition of cell types — the mix of tumor cells versus immune cells versus supporting cells? No. Does it live in cellular neighborhoods — which cells cluster near which? Also no. None of those first-order features predicted whether a tumor would go on to transform.

What did predict it were second-order features. Two of them, specifically. First, they saw abnormal chromosomal contact patterns — which regions of DNA physically touch which other regions inside the nucleus — that were different in patients who would go on to transform versus those who would stay stable. Second, and I think this is the more actionable finding, they identified twenty-four specific ligand-receptor pairs that were cross-expressed in neighboring cells — meaning, cell A next to cell B, cell A makes the signaling molecule and cell B makes the receptor for it, but neither cell alone is doing both. In other words, the risk lives in cell-to-cell signaling that requires spatial adjacency to work. They then took one of these pairs, ENPP two and its receptor LPAR one — a well-known signaling axis involved in cell motility and tumor invasion — and validated the finding in an independent cohort. The signal held.

And here's where the foundation model piece comes in. They then used something they call a cell-interaction-based foundation model — CIFM — trained on spatial RNA data in a broader context, and ran forward simulations to nominate which upstream signaling factors were driving the ligand-receptor cross-expression patterns they observed. The foundation model pointed at myeloid signaling — the tumor-infiltrating immune cells of the myeloid lineage, macrophages and their kin. That's a candidate you could test with an existing drug.

Why this matters, beyond glioma specifically. This is a nice, concrete example of the second wave of what "foundation model for biology" actually means. The first wave was AlphaFold-style — predict a static thing from a sequence. The second wave, of which CIFM is one instance, is: pre-train a large model on all the general spatial cell-cell signaling data we have across many contexts, and then use that model as an inference engine to say "given the interactions I see in this specific tumor, what would perturbing X do?" It's the same intuition that motivates virtual-cell modeling programs at the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, Arc, and various academic groups. Glioma malignant transformation is a really rigorous test case, because the ground truth — did this patient actually transform, and when — is knowable from the medical record. If you can predict it before it happens using nothing but spatial signaling data plus a foundation model, that's a template that could apply to a lot of other cancers.

The generalizable methodological takeaway is worth emphasizing separately. The paper argues that increased depth of measurement — more transcripts per cell, more spatial resolution — on a smaller number of samples may be more informative than the shallow-and-wide approach the field has often defaulted to. That's because these key ligand-receptor transcripts are present at fewer than ten copies per cell. If your assay isn't sensitive enough, you miss them entirely, and the entire second-order signal collapses. Depth beats sample count when the biology lives in a sparse signal.

And finally, animals.

The University of the Sunshine Coast, on Australia's east coast, has just released a set of drone and underwater videos of wild Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins doing something that until now had only been documented on the other side of the continent. In the sheltered waters of Hervey Bay in Queensland — that's the bay just north of Fraser Island — a research team led by behavioral ecologist Alexis Levengood filmed a group of dolphins hunting fish using enormous empty sea snail shells. The technique, which the field calls "shelling," is this: the dolphin chases a small fish, the fish darts inside one of these football-sized empty shells for shelter, and the dolphin flips the whole shell out of the water, tips it, and drinks the fish out.

Until now, shelling had only ever been documented in Shark Bay, on Australia's west coast — more than six thousand kilometers away on the other side of the continent. So there are two possibilities for what's going on here. Either the Queensland and Western Australian populations invented the same tool-use technique completely independently — a case of convergent cultural evolution — or the technique is more widespread across bottlenose dolphins than anyone had realized, and we've just been under-observing it everywhere else. Both possibilities are individually interesting.

But the best part of the paper — and the reason it's the closer today — is one of the video clips. In the footage, an adult female dolphin the team has given the name Maserati performs a textbook shell hunt: chase, grab the shell, lift, tip, eat. And in the moments right after, her calf, named Bentley, picks up the same shell and attempts to do exactly what mom just did. It's the first direct visual evidence anyone has of a mother dolphin demonstrating shelling to her offspring, and it strongly supports the hypothesis that this tool use behavior is culturally transmitted mother to calf, not somehow encoded in the genome. Cetacean culture — the idea that whales and dolphins pass learned skills across generations the way we do — has been an area with a lot of theoretical interest and not enough clean video, and Maserati and Bentley have just added a striking new piece of it.
