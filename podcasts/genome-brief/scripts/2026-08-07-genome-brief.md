## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Friday, August seventh, twenty-twenty-six. Three items today. First, on pathogen watch — a preprint from Sam Diggle's group at Georgia Tech and Kendra Rumbaugh's lab at Texas Tech, out this week on bioRxiv, describes a strategy that turns antibiotic resistance itself into a trap. They use a class of narrow-spectrum bactericidal particles called R-pyocins to select against pan-drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and the escape mutations the bacteria are forced to make delete a huge stretch of chromosome that also happens to carry a major efflux pump and most of the organism's virulence machinery. What comes out the other side is an attenuated, aminoglycoside-sensitive bug that is easily cleared in wound models. Second, on the business side — a big first-in-class week from the F-D-A. Moderna's mRNA-ten-ten, brand name mFlusiva, became the first mRNA-based seasonal influenza vaccine ever licensed, approved this week for adults fifty and up; and Takeda's oveporexton, brand name Orzeyful, became the first ever orexin receptor agonist, approved this week for narcolepsy type one — meaning the first drug that treats the underlying cause of that disease rather than just papering over the symptoms. And finally, animals — a big evolutionary comparative study out this week in Nature Communications from Scott Pitnick's lab at Syracuse looks across six hundred million years of arthropod evolution and finds that in a huge number of lineages, sperm from the same individual physically bind together into cooperating teams before they go looking for the egg, and that this cooperation has evolved and been lost over and over across the tree. On with the show.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is on the World Health Organization's critical-priority pathogen list for a reason. It is a Gram-negative opportunistic pathogen that colonizes chronic wounds, burns, cystic fibrosis airways, and immunocompromised patients. It carries an unusually rich set of intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms — beta-lactamases, porin mutations, and, importantly for this story, a family of multidrug efflux pumps that actively pump antibiotics back out of the cell across a wide range of drug classes. Clinical isolates that resist every antibiotic in the standard therapeutic arsenal — pan-drug-resistant, or P-D-R — are increasingly common, and once you are looking at a P-D-R Pseudomonas wound infection you are essentially out of drugs.

The Diggle and Rumbaugh labs, in a preprint posted this week on bioRxiv, ask a different question. Instead of trying to add another antibiotic that the bug will eventually resist too, can we exploit the way the bug becomes resistant?

Let me define the tools first. *R-pyocins* are a family of bactericidal particles produced by Pseudomonas aeruginosa itself, one strain against another. Structurally they look like the contractile tails of bacteriophages — imagine a phage that lost its head and kept only the syringe. They bind to a receptor on the surface of a target Pseudomonas cell, contract, and puncture the membrane. They are highly specific — one R-pyocin type targets one narrow set of surface receptors, so they are useless as broad-spectrum antibacterials, but very good at picking out a particular strain. In this study, the R-pyocins target a component of the bacterial cell surface called the lipopolysaccharide, or L-P-S, core. L-P-S is the outer layer of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria; it is essential for membrane integrity in most conditions and it is what the R-pyocin recognizes as its docking site.

Now the experiment. The authors take two laboratory reference strains of Pseudomonas — the workhorses called P-A-O-one and P-A-K — plus, critically, a real clinical isolate designated M-R-S-N sixty-two-twenty. That clinical isolate came from a patient wound, and it is genuinely pan-drug-resistant — it resists every antibiotic in the standard panel. They put these strains under R-pyocin selective pressure — expose them to R-pyocins, and let the survivors grow. The bacteria that survive have to have found a way to avoid the R-pyocin, and the only real way to do that is to change or eliminate the L-P-S receptor the R-pyocin is docking on.

Here is where the story gets interesting. The escape route Pseudomonas takes is not a delicate point mutation. Across every strain tested, resistance emerges through massive chromosomal deletions — huge chunks of the chromosome, between two hundred fifty and three hundred eighty-eight kilobases, are simply excised. And crucially, those deletions always span the same conserved genomic region — the region that contains, side by side, three things: the gene called *galU*, which encodes an enzyme essential for building the L-P-S receptor the R-pyocin is targeting; a gene called *hmgA*, whose loss makes the resulting colonies visibly brown because a pigment precursor accumulates, so you can literally see which cells have made the deletion; and — this is the key — the *mexXY* multidrug efflux pump operon, one of the major exporters Pseudomonas uses to pump antibiotics back out.

Why does the deletion span all three? Because they are physically close together on the chromosome in a conserved cluster, and the recombination pathway that produces these escape mutations doesn't have surgical precision — it excises the whole neighborhood in one event.

So here is what the bacteria have done to save themselves from the R-pyocin. To avoid being killed by a narrow-spectrum surface-recognition weapon, they have deleted several hundred kilobases of their own genome, including — you can predict where this is going — the very efflux pump that gave them multidrug resistance in the first place. Turn off the L-P-S surface receptor, and mexXY, sitting on the same excised chunk, goes with it.

The consequences the authors document are almost too neat. Loss of mexXY produces what is called *collateral sensitivity* — resensitization — to aminoglycosides, an antibiotic class Pseudomonas normally pumps out. In the escaped clone, gentamicin — an aminoglycoside that would have failed against the parent P-D-R isolate — is now effective. On top of that, those hundreds of kilobases of deleted DNA also contain a large fraction of Pseudomonas's virulence genes: the *hcn* cluster for hydrogen cyanide production, *exoY*, the phenazine biosynthesis genes that produce the classic blue-green pigment and are toxic to host cells, and the *cup* biofilm attachment genes. When the authors take these R-pyocin-selected brown mutants and inject them into Galleria mellonella — a wax-worm model widely used for bacterial virulence screens — the mutants are essentially non-lethal. And in a mouse chronic wound model, treating with R-pyocins first and then gentamicin gives a three-log reduction in bacterial load — a thousand-fold drop — compared to what gentamicin alone can do against the parent.

Two things worth taking from this. First, the mechanism is what the authors describe as an evolutionary *checkmate*: the pathogen has essentially one route out from under this particular selective pressure, and that route runs through a piece of chromosome the pathogen can't afford to lose. You are not trying to kill the bacterium with the R-pyocin — you are using the R-pyocin to force the bacterium to disarm itself in exchange for survival. That is a fundamentally different logic than adding one more antibiotic and hoping resistance is slow. It doesn't fight resistance; it *uses* resistance. Second, the applied significance for pan-drug-resistant infections is real. The clinical isolate here — M-R-S-N sixty-two-twenty — came from a burn wound and had already exhausted the standard antibiotic panel. If a topical R-pyocin cocktail can be applied to a wound bed to precondition it before aminoglycoside therapy, the resistance profile of what remains after selection is predictably better than what you started with, and the virulence profile is dramatically lower. This is a bioRxiv preprint, so it is not yet peer-reviewed, and it is a proof-of-concept in mice and wax worms rather than a clinical trial. But the mechanism has the kind of geometry — a conserved chromosomal linkage forcing an unavoidable trade-off — that tends to reproduce.

On the business side.

Two first-in-class F-D-A approvals landed this week, both worth noting individually.

First. On August fifth, the F-D-A approved Moderna's mFlusiva — the trade name for mRNA-ten-ten — as the first mRNA-based seasonal influenza vaccine ever licensed in the United States. It is approved for adults fifty and older, with an accelerated approval component for the sixty-five-and-over subgroup requiring a confirmatory post-marketing readout.

Some context on what mFlusiva actually is, because "mRNA flu vaccine" tells you the platform but not the product. mFlusiva is a *trivalent* vaccine — three strains, not four. It encodes the hemagglutinin proteins of A-slash-H one N one, A-slash-H three N two, and B-slash-Victoria. Hemagglutinin, or H-A, is the viral surface protein influenza uses to bind sialic acid receptors on host cells and enter them, and it is the dominant target of the neutralizing antibody response. Trivalent means three components; and if you were listening to yesterday's episode, you already know why. The Yamagata lineage of influenza B appears to have gone extinct during the COVID pandemic, the World Health Organization in twenty-twenty-four formally recommended removing it from seasonal vaccines because continuing to include a dead antigen is dead manufacturing capacity, and this year's vaccines — including mFlusiva — reflect that. So this is the first product to hit the market that both is mRNA-based and drops the extinct fourth strain.

Two things about the platform worth calling out. First, on efficacy. In the Phase three trial published this year in the New England Journal of Medicine — over forty thousand adults aged fifty and up randomized in an active-controlled comparison against a licensed standard-dose flu vaccine — mFlusiva delivered twenty-six-point-six percent higher relative vaccine efficacy against P-C-R-confirmed influenza-like illness, and twenty-seven-point-four percent higher in the sixty-five-and-up subgroup. That is not a huge absolute number of prevented cases at the population level, but it is a real improvement over the standard-dose comparator, and it is genuinely superior efficacy against each of the three strains individually — twenty-nine-point-six percent better on H one N one, twenty-two-point-two on H three N two, twenty-nine-point-one on B-slash-Victoria. Second, on why the platform matters more than the year-one efficacy number. The traditional flu vaccine manufacturing process is egg-based: grow the virus in chicken eggs for months, harvest, inactivate, formulate. It takes so long that the World Health Organization has to commit to a strain formulation each February for the following autumn's Northern Hemisphere season — nine months of forward guess before the flu virus actually shows up. If the wrong strain is picked, or if a match strain drifts between the guess and the season, the whole vaccine's efficacy suffers. mRNA vaccines don't need eggs. Sequencing a circulating H-A protein and synthesizing the corresponding mRNA is a matter of days to weeks. The Moderna approval is not really about this year's flu — it's about proving the platform can be *pointed* at a flu strain and licensed. If the mRNA platform holds up over multiple seasons at superior efficacy versus egg-based standard dose, the strain-selection lead time collapses, and pandemic influenza response gets a fundamentally different tool.

Second approval. Also on August fifth, the F-D-A approved Takeda's oveporexton, brand name Orzeyful, for narcolepsy type one in adults. The framing here — and it is a genuinely correct framing — is that this is the first medicine ever approved that treats the underlying cause of narcolepsy type one, not the symptoms.

Some background. Narcolepsy type one, or N-T-one, affects about a hundred and twenty thousand Americans and is defined by two clinical features: excessive daytime sleepiness — sudden, uncontrollable sleep attacks during the day — and cataplexy, which is a sudden and often complete loss of voluntary muscle tone triggered by strong emotion. A patient laughs and their knees buckle; they are startled and slump. The underlying biology is that N-T-one patients have lost the neurons in a small region of the hypothalamus that produce a peptide called *orexin*, also known as hypocretin. There are two orexin receptors in the brain — O-X-one-R and O-X-two-R — and both, when activated, drive wakefulness and stabilize the transitions between sleep and wake states. When the orexin neurons die — the current best evidence is that this is an autoimmune process that selectively destroys them — signaling through those receptors collapses, sleep-wake regulation falls apart, and R-E-M-associated phenomena like cataplexy intrude on the waking state.

Existing narcolepsy treatments are all symptomatic. Modafinil and armodafinil promote wakefulness through other pathways. Sodium oxybate — the drug marketed as Xyrem — is a G-A-B-A-B agonist you take at night that improves nighttime sleep consolidation and reduces daytime symptoms indirectly. Pitolisant is a histamine-three receptor antagonist. Solriamfetol is a dopamine-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor. All of them work by *substituting for* the missing orexin signal by acting on some other arousal pathway. None of them touches the orexin receptor.

Oveporexton is the first drug that binds to and activates the orexin receptor directly. Specifically, it is a selective agonist of O-X-two-R, the receptor subtype thought to carry most of the wake-promoting signal. The pivotal Phase three data came from two trials — FirstLight and RadiantLight — that together enrolled about two hundred and seventy N-T-one patients on twelve weeks of treatment against placebo. Both trials hit every primary and secondary endpoint. On the objective test of daytime alertness — the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test, in which a patient sits in a quiet room and is timed on how long they can stay awake before falling asleep — patients on the two-milligram twice-daily dose largely reached the normal wakefulness range of twenty minutes or more. On the Epworth Sleepiness Scale, a validated patient-reported measure of daytime sleepiness, roughly eighty-five percent of treated patients scored at or below ten — which is the healthy range. And weekly cataplexy rates dropped significantly compared to placebo.

The adverse-event profile is exactly what you would predict from a drug that revs up the wake system that was missing. Insomnia in about sixty percent of the two-milligram arm versus one percent on placebo. Urinary frequency in fifty-eight percent versus five percent — orexin also modulates autonomic outflow, and increased sympathetic tone plausibly explains that. And an eleven percent versus five percent rate of asymptomatic C-P-K elevations — creatine phosphokinase over five times the upper limit of normal — which is worth monitoring.

Two things to take from this. First, narcolepsy type one has a defined, measurable neurotransmitter deficiency, and for the first time there is a drug that replaces the missing signal at the receptor. This is essentially the pharmacological playbook that worked for insulin in type-one diabetes and for L-dopa in Parkinson's — replace the missing molecule — but at a G-protein-coupled receptor in the brain. Second, the same target is now open across other sleep disorders. Idiopathic hypersomnia, sleep-wake disturbance in Parkinson's disease, some fatigue syndromes — anywhere the underlying pathology is a wake-signal deficit — orexin agonism is now a validated approach, and Takeda plus the several other companies with orexin agonists in earlier stages will move fast into those indications.

And finally, animals.

A study out this week in Nature Communications, from Antonio Gomez in Scott Pitnick's lab at Syracuse University, with collaborators from the University of Siena and the University of Szeged in Hungary, takes on a piece of biology that turns out to have been much stranger than the textbook version.

The textbook story — the one embedded in everyone's mental picture of fertilization — is a race. Millions of sperm swim toward the egg, all competing individually, and the fastest wins. That mental picture came originally from mammal reproduction, where it happens to be mostly true. But the Pitnick lab, going back several decades in various forms, has been building a case that in a striking number of animal groups sperm do not compete as individuals at all — they cooperate. Specifically, sperm from the same individual physically bind together into structured teams before or during their journey to the egg, and those teams often swim faster, straighter, or last longer than any individual sperm cell could on its own.

This new paper is the biggest evolutionary look at that phenomenon so far. The group assembled sperm morphology data across arthropods — the enormous animal phylum that includes all insects, all crustaceans, all arachnids, and all myriapods — and mapped the presence or absence of two specific forms of sperm cooperation onto the arthropod phylogenetic tree. The two forms are: *sperm conjugation*, in which multiple sperm from the same individual physically stick together into a coordinated bundle; and what the authors call *sperm-associated material*, or S-A-M, a secreted membrane-bound substance that packages sperm together or forms a structure that hitches them to each other in transit.

The finding is that both forms of sperm cooperation are widespread across arthropods and have evolved independently many, many times over roughly six hundred million years. In the paper's phylogenetic reconstruction, they estimate that the common ancestor of *all* insects already had conjugated sperm. Some lineages then lost the trait, some regained it, some elaborated it into striking multi-sperm structures. This is not a rare exception — it is, in one of the biggest phyla of animal life, closer to the rule.

Why would cooperation evolve? A few plausible reasons. Cooperating sperm move faster through the geometry of the female reproductive tract. Larger sperm bundles are more resistant to displacement by later matings — many arthropods mate promiscuously, and a coordinated sperm mass is harder to physically dislodge than isolated cells. And in some species the cooperating structure carries proteins or other cargo that the individual sperm cell couldn't. The evolutionary puzzle is that within a single ejaculate, cooperating and non-cooperating strategies have to be genetically equivalent — every sperm was produced by the same male — so the cell-level cooperation is genuinely at the level of sister cells acting for the group, not individuals competing.

The takeaway for a working listener is small but real: the "sperm race" is a marketing simplification, not a general biological rule. Across most of the animals on Earth by species count, sperm reproduce by cooperating with their siblings, and that cooperation has evolved from scratch enough times that the underlying selective pressures must be very general. The paper is a nice reminder that the phrase "how it works in mammals" is not usually the phrase "how it works."
