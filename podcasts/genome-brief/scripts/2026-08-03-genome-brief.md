## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Monday, August third, 2026. Four items today. First, on pathogen watch — South Australia has confirmed the first mass mortality event of native wildlife in Australia's ongoing H five N one bird flu outbreak. Forty-nine dead greater crested terns, thirty-five more sick, on a rocky islet off the coast about two hundred kilometers southeast of Adelaide. Australia was the last major continent H five N one clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b had not yet reached; that changed in June, and this is the first spillover event of the size the rest of the world has been watching in Europe, South America, and North America. Second, on the business side — a Financial Times report over the weekend that AstraZeneca has had exploratory talks with Bristol Myers Squibb about what would be the largest pharma merger ever, roughly four hundred billion dollars combined market cap, sent both stocks in opposite directions this morning. Analysts are calling it, essentially, structurally impossible on antitrust grounds — for one specific reason we'll get into. Third, on AI in biomedicine — a benchmark study out of Harvard Medical School, from the labs of Debora Marks and George Church, asks a question that a lot of drug-discovery groups have been quietly experimenting with. Namely, if you take a modern general-purpose language model — Claude, GPT, Gemini — and give it a protein sequence and a bunch of proposed mutations, can it tell you which mutations will improve function? The answer, on two hundred and seventeen benchmark tasks, is: better than most of the specialist tools that used to be state of the art. And finally, animals — a University of Michigan lab used an AI system called Skelevision to measure a hundred and seventy thousand skeletal features across two thousand songbird species, and it confirmed something Stephen Jay Gould argued for fifty years without the data to prove it.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Highly pathogenic avian influenza clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b — the specific lineage of H five N one that has been circulating globally since roughly twenty-twenty — has now been driving the largest bird-flu panzootic in recorded history. It has killed an estimated hundreds of millions of poultry worldwide, tens of thousands of wild mammals — including thirty thousand South American sea lions in a single Southern Hemisphere outbreak in twenty-twenty-two — and has been spreading in US dairy cattle since March of last year, with a growing tally of human cases from occupational exposure. Until this summer, one continent had stayed clean. Australia. It is the only continent in the world that has not previously hosted H five N one in wild birds, and the accepted explanation is that the migratory bird flyways that connect Eurasia and the Americas to their wintering grounds mostly stop short of the Australian mainland.

That protection ended in June. On the fourteenth of June, a brown skua — a large predatory seabird — was found sick at Cape Le Grand National Park near Esperance on the coast of Western Australia. The Australian Centre for Disease Preparedness confirmed H five N one clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b, genotype B three-point-two, in that bird within a week. Since then, positive detections have accumulated in wild seabirds across five Australian states — brown skuas, silver gulls, cormorants, and now, in this event, greater crested terns.

Here is what happened this weekend. Primary Industries and Regions South Australia — the state agriculture department — sent aerial surveillance teams to check on breeding colonies at Baudin Rocks, which is a small protected islet in the Southern Ocean about two hundred kilometers southeast of Adelaide. The islet is home to a substantial greater crested tern breeding colony. The surveillance flight on Friday counted forty-nine dead terns and thirty-five visibly sick terns, and additional samples came back positive on August second. As of the state government's Monday update, South Australia is now at forty confirmed cases, and this Baudin Rocks event is the single largest wildlife die-off in the entire Australian outbreak so far.

Two reasons this matters.

First, the species. Greater crested terns nest in dense colonies on offshore rocks — hundreds to thousands of adults, packed shoulder to shoulder for weeks during breeding. That is exactly the ecology in which H five N one has caused catastrophic die-offs elsewhere. On the north coast of Chile in twenty-twenty-two, a colonial seabird called the Peruvian pelican lost roughly forty percent of its global population in a matter of weeks. In South Georgia, colonies of gentoo and king penguins have been decimated. Colonial seabirds essentially provide the virus a substrate on which every individual is an infected contact within meters of every other, and the mortality curves that come out of those events look nothing like a normal disease outbreak. The Baudin Rocks event may be the leading edge of that pattern in Australia. Environment officials are already saying publicly they cannot stop wild birds from moving between colonies.

Second, what is *not* yet happening — which is what surveillance is watching for. There is still no confirmed spread of clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b into Australian commercial poultry. That is the economically consequential shoe that has not yet dropped, and it is the reason the state government's testing update reads more like a biosecurity war-room briefing than a wildlife bulletin. The other worry is silver gulls — the ordinary urban gulls of Australian coastal cities. They move between beaches, rubbish tips, wetlands, and inland waterways in a way that colonial seabirds do not, and detections of H five N one in silver gulls are already appearing in this outbreak. If the virus establishes in silver gulls in the way it already has in the analogous European herring gulls, geographic containment inside the coastline is over. And that is the transmission pathway most likely to eventually reach a dairy or a poultry shed.

Human risk from this outbreak remains low. Australian health authorities have not issued a public-health alert; the standard occupational advice applies to wildlife responders. But this is now the last major continent added to the map of clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b's spread, and that is worth noting.

On the business side.

Over the weekend, the Financial Times reported that AstraZeneca had held early-stage exploratory talks with Bristol Myers Squibb about a potential all-stock combination. If it happened, the combined enterprise value would be roughly four hundred billion dollars, which would make it the largest pharmaceutical merger ever consummated by a factor of roughly two. Both companies declined to comment. AstraZeneca's stock in pre-market trading this morning was down about four percent; Bristol Myers Squibb was up about six percent. Which is the market's way of saying: we think this could happen, and if it does, BMS shareholders are getting the premium and AstraZeneca shareholders are paying it.

Analyst reaction was almost uniformly skeptical. BMO Capital Markets said flatly: based on significant business overlap, a deal is less likely to materialize. Jefferies said if it did happen it would trigger a new wave of large-cap M and A. But everyone quoted stops short of calling it likely.

Some context. AstraZeneca has a current market capitalization of roughly one hundred and ninety-six billion dollars; BMS is about one hundred and thirty-three billion. The strategic case, on paper, is that both have massive oncology franchises that would be complementary — AstraZeneca is strongest in solid tumors, with Enhertu, the antibody-drug conjugate for HER-two-positive breast cancer, and Imfinzi, its P D-L one immune checkpoint blocker. BMS's cancer portfolio is anchored in blood cancers — the Celgene assets from twenty-nineteen, including the CAR-T therapies Abecma and Breyanzi — plus Opdivo, its P D-one checkpoint inhibitor in solid tumors. Combined oncology sales north of a hundred billion dollars a year.

But that same portfolio is exactly the problem. Opdivo and Imfinzi are direct competitors in advanced non-small-cell lung cancer — Opdivo generated about ten billion dollars in lung cancer sales last year, Imfinzi about six billion. In the United States, Europe, and most other major markets, competition between checkpoint inhibitors from different companies is a foundational assumption of how oncology pricing works. Merging the number-two and number-three players in that field into one company is the kind of thing antitrust divisions exist to prevent. The BMO analyst was essentially making the observation that this is not a fixable problem with a divestiture — you cannot spin out an approved drug to a competitor overnight without collapsing its revenue stream. Which is why the "unlikely" framing dominates the sell-side notes.

There is one other reading of this that is worth noting, because it changes the interpretation. It is possible the talks were exploratory precisely *because* AstraZeneca and BMS both foresee a coming revenue cliff — AstraZeneca has significant patent expiries on Farxiga and Tagrisso in the next few years; BMS is losing Revlimid revenue as generics accelerate. Both companies have said publicly they are actively looking at acquisitions. A large defensive merger is a rational strategy for two companies facing simultaneous late-decade patent losses. It is also worth remembering that AstraZeneca has been here before — in twenty-fourteen it rejected a hostile bid from Pfizer that would have created what was then the largest pharma company in the world; CEO Pascal Soriot argued the standalone pipeline would deliver more value. That pipeline did in fact deliver, so his credibility on this call is real. Whether the current environment is different enough to change his answer is what the market is trying to price this morning.

On AI in biomedicine.

There has been a steady thread of results over the past year suggesting that general-purpose large language models — the kind you would use to write email or debug code — are becoming genuinely useful at scientific tasks nobody expected them to do. A benchmark posted to bioRxiv last week from a Harvard Medical School team led by Rohit Arora, from Debora Marks's lab, with George Church as a coauthor, sets a very clean number on that in one domain: protein variant ranking.

Some background. When a biologist wants to redesign a protein — say, to improve stability, or make it recognize a new binding partner — the practical workflow is often: mutate a bunch of residues, test which mutants are better, iterate. But you cannot test every mutation experimentally, so predicting *in silico* which mutations are likely to improve function is enormously valuable. That prediction problem is called variant-effect prediction, and it is one of the most well-established benchmarks in computational biology. The gold standard is a dataset called ProteinGym, which contains deep mutational scanning data — meaning experimental fitness measurements for thousands of individual mutations across hundreds of proteins.

The specialist tools that have been built for this look nothing like a chatbot. ESM-two, from Meta, is a protein-specific language model trained on billions of protein sequences that has learned the statistical regularities of what a functional protein looks like. VenusREM, currently the leading specialist, uses evolutionary information — it aligns a target protein to millions of related sequences and asks which mutations are conserved across evolution, on the assumption that conserved residues are functionally important. Both of these tools have been carefully optimized for exactly the task of scoring how good a proposed mutation is.

Here is what the Harvard team did. They took thirteen general-purpose language models — Claude Opus five, GPT-five-point-six Sol, Gemini, and ten others — and gave each of them the same prompt on two hundred and seventeen ProteinGym tasks: here is a wild-type protein sequence, here is a description of the assay, here are fifty mutated sequences; rank them by predicted fitness. No sequence alignments. No structural information. Just the sequence and the words describing the experiment.

The results are, honestly, striking. Claude Opus five landed at the top of the leaderboard with a Spearman rank correlation of zero-point-four-oh-six. GPT-five-point-six Sol at zero-point-four-oh-two. To put those numbers in context — the leading specialist tool, VenusREM, scored zero-point-five-two-three. So the specialists still win. But Claude Opus five beat forty-nine out of the ninety-five specialist protein predictors evaluated in the same benchmark, including forty-one out of the forty-six sequence-only specialist methods. It essentially matches ESM-two-six-fifty M, which is the standard mid-sized protein-specific language model.

Three things worth flagging.

First, this was done in what the authors call a *tool-free* setting. No retrieval. No calling external protein-structure predictors. Just the model reading text and outputting a ranking. That is important because the failure mode people worried about was contamination — that the LLMs had memorized ProteinGym data during training. To rule this out, the authors also ran a held-out set of fifty-nine deep-mutational-scanning assays from studies whose fitness scores first became public *after* January of this year, which is after these models' training cutoffs. The same performance patterns held. So this is not memorization.

Second, performance improves with test-time compute — meaning, when you give the model more tokens to reason through the problem, it gets better. This is consistent with what we're seeing on math and coding benchmarks: LLMs are increasingly good at protein reasoning when allowed to think it through. The catch is the gains taper — they do not keep climbing all the way up to specialist performance no matter how much compute you throw at them.

Third — and this is the finding I think is most consequential for a working scientist thinking about their own workflow — the LLMs do *not* rely on evolutionary alignment depth. Traditional specialist methods work well when the protein of interest has thousands of homologs sequenced, and work poorly for orphan proteins with no close relatives. Claude Opus five's accuracy is roughly the same for orphans as for well-aligned proteins. Which means the domain where LLMs are actually competitive with specialists is exactly the domain where specialists fail — proteins nobody has studied deeply before. If you were designing an antibody against a hard, novel target, or working on a de novo enzyme design, that is precisely where you would reach for one of these tools instead of the specialist.

The bottom line is not that general-purpose LLMs beat protein-specific models. They do not. The bottom line is that a general model with no biological training now beats forty of forty-six sequence-only specialist tools that were carefully engineered for exactly this task. That is a meaningful capability signal, and it is the kind of signal that changes how a small biotech might staff its computational team.

And finally, animals.

A paper published last Tuesday in Nature Ecology and Evolution, from Jake Berv, a postdoc at the University of Michigan, in collaboration with Brian Weeks' lab at Michigan and David Fouhey's computer vision lab at N-Y-U, addresses a very old question in evolutionary biology using a technique that is very new.

The old question. In nineteen-seventy-two Stephen Jay Gould and Niles Eldredge proposed that species do not evolve in smooth, continuous, gradual change over millions of years. Instead, they argued, evolution is characterized by long periods of near-stasis punctuated by short bursts of rapid change. That idea is called punctuated equilibrium, and it has been a foundational theoretical claim in evolutionary biology for fifty years — but pinning it down with actual measurements at the scale it needed to be tested has been enormously hard. To do it, you would need to precisely measure the anatomy of tens of thousands of animals across tens of millions of years of evolutionary history. That is a lot of calipers.

Which is where the AI part comes in. The Berv team built a system called Skelevision — a computer vision model trained to look at a photograph of a bird skeleton, laid out on a background grid, and automatically extract precise measurements of every bone. Length, curvature, articulation angle, the whole set. What used to take a human being with calipers a full day to do on one specimen, Skelevision does in forty-five seconds. And it does it consistently, without the drift that happens between human observers.

They pointed it at natural history museum collections. Approximately fifteen thousand museum specimens were photographed. Over one hundred and seventy thousand individual bone measurements were extracted. Two thousand and change species, all within Passeriformes — the songbirds, which are the largest and most diverse order of living birds and about half of all bird species alive today.

Then they used those measurements to reconstruct what songbird body plans looked like at every point in the last forty-five million years of evolutionary history. And here is the punchline. Songbird evolution was not gradual. It had a huge burst of anatomical innovation about thirty-five million years ago, right at the Eocene-Oligocene transition — a period of dramatic global cooling when Earth's climate reorganized itself and formed the Antarctic ice sheet for the first time. And it had a distinct slowdown starting roughly fifteen million years ago in the Miocene, when climate stabilized. In between and outside those windows, evolution proceeded slowly. Punctuated equilibrium, quantitatively, on the scale Gould was arguing for.

There are two things I like about this paper.

One, it is a genuinely elegant use of AI. This is not a foundation model producing a plausible-sounding answer. This is a targeted computer vision model doing an enormous, tedious measurement task that would otherwise be impossible at scale, and turning it into a dataset that can be analyzed with well-established statistical methods. That's a very old-school kind of "AI for science" — automate the measurement, use the measurements to answer the question. And it worked.

Two, the biological story is satisfying. When the planet's climate underwent one of its biggest reorganizations of the last hundred million years, one group of small vertebrates — the songbirds — responded with a burst of anatomical experimentation that founded the family tree of what you can see in your backyard today. That is a lovely inversion of the usual narrative that climate change is what pushes species to extinction. It is also, sometimes, what pushes species to diversify.
