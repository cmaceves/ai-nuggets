# Episode 40: A one-shot pan-H5N1 vaccine, and citations that fool doctors

## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Tuesday, August fourth, twenty-twenty-six. Four items today. First, on pathogen watch — a preprint out yesterday from Mount Sinai, St. Jude, and Texas Biomed describes a single-shot live-attenuated influenza vaccine that mixes hemagglutinins from three genetically distinct H five N one clades and, in mice, elicits antibodies that cross-react against all three. It is a mouse study, not a human trial, but it is exactly the kind of pan-H five N one approach that the ongoing outbreak — including yesterday's news out of South Australia — makes urgent. Second, on the business side — the F D A on Thursday approved Pluvicto, the P S M A-targeted radioligand from Novartis, for hormone-sensitive metastatic prostate cancer. That approval nearly doubles the eligible patient population and moves radioligand therapy from a last-line intervention to an early-line intensification. We will walk through what P S M A actually is, why targeted radiation using lutetium one seventy-seven works, and what the numbers behind the approval actually say. Third, on A I in biomedicine — a paper out of the German Cancer Research Center in Heidelberg, working with clinicians from Vienna, Dresden, and Chile, tested how citation-supported large language models affect physician decision-making. And they found something specific and unsettling. When the model is right, citations make physicians more likely to trust the correct answer, which is what you would hope. But when the model is wrong, citations dissolve physicians' resistance to the wrong answer — from ninety-two percent to about thirty-five percent. Fourth, animals — Danish entomologists have described a new genus of rove beetle from the highlands of southern China and northern Laos, with mandibles and antennae so unusually long and thin they named the whole genus after Monkey D. Luffy, the stretchy protagonist of One Piece.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Yesterday's episode was about the first mass mortality event of native wildlife in Australia's ongoing H five N one outbreak — greater crested terns on a rocky islet in South Australia, dead in the dozens. Today's paper, out of Adolfo García-Sastre's lab at Mount Sinai and Luis Martínez-Sobrido's group at Texas Biomed, working with Richard Webby's team at St. Jude, is what a research response to that global situation looks like. It was posted to bioRxiv on August second.

Some context. H five N one is a subtype of avian influenza A. The clade currently driving the panzootic is called two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b — we covered this yesterday. But it is not the only clade of concern. Two other lineages — clade two-point-three-point-two-point-one-a and clade two-point-three-point-two-point-one-e — have been circulating in poultry and wild birds across South and Southeast Asia, and both have caused human cases in the last few years. If a pandemic H five N one emerges, we do not know today which clade will be the one that succeeds. A stockpile vaccine designed for two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b might match poorly if the pandemic starter is one of the Asian clades. That is what this paper is trying to fix.

Here is what they did. Live attenuated influenza vaccines, or L A I Vs, are the same technology as FluMist — the nasal-spray flu shot approved in the U S. They use a weakened influenza virus that can replicate briefly in the cool tissue of the upper airway, generating an immune response, but cannot replicate in the warmer lower airway, so it cannot cause disease. The specific master donor virus used here is the same Ann Arbor strain from nineteen-sixty that FluMist is built on: it is cold-adapted, meaning it prefers thirty-three-degree temperatures over thirty-seven; it is temperature-sensitive, meaning it stops replicating in the deeper airway; and it is attenuated, meaning even in tissues where it replicates, it does not cause meaningful disease.

Onto that safe backbone, the team dropped in the surface glycoproteins — hemagglutinin, which is what the antibody response actually targets, and neuraminidase, which the virus uses to release itself from infected cells — from three different H five N one strains representing the three clades of concern. One from a two-thousand-twenty-four human case in Louisiana, in clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b. One from a two-thousand-twenty-four human case in Australia, in clade two-point-three-point-two-point-one-a. And one from a two-thousand-twenty-three human case in Cambodia, in clade two-point-three-point-two-point-one-e.

A single intranasal dose of the multivalent vaccine, in mice, produced antibodies that recognized all three clades. And when the vaccinated mice were challenged with matched H five N one virus, they were completely protected — no disease. The abstract does not report challenge with heterologous clades — meaning, does the vaccine also protect against a fourth clade nobody trained it on — and it does not report data in ferrets, which are the standard animal model for predicting human influenza response. So this is a foundational preclinical result, not a translational one. But it does establish something important. You can put three antigenically distinct H five N one hemagglutinins on the same live-attenuated backbone and get broad, cross-reactive immunity from a single intranasal dose. That is meaningful because a stockpile of pan-H five N one vaccine is much more useful than a stockpile of one-clade vaccine when nobody knows which clade will be the surprise.

The realistic timeline for anything derived from a mouse study to reach approval is measured in years. But given the trajectory of H five N one in wild birds and mammals worldwide, and the specific worry — which we talked about yesterday — that silver gulls or dairy cattle in unexpected geographies could be the bridge to a poultry- or human-adapted variant, the pan-clade design is the strategic move.

On the business side.

The F D A on Thursday approved Pluvicto — the brand name for lutetium one seventy-seven vipivotide tetraxetan — for use in combination with an androgen receptor pathway inhibitor, in adults with P S M A-positive metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer. That is a mouthful of jargon. Let me unpack it, because the mechanism here is genuinely elegant.

First, P S M A. It stands for prostate-specific membrane antigen. It is a protein sitting on the outer surface of prostate cells, and — crucially — it is dramatically overexpressed on prostate cancer cells relative to normal tissue. Not just prostate cancer confined to the gland — it is also expressed on prostate cancer cells that have metastasized to bone, lymph nodes, or elsewhere. That difference in expression is what makes P S M A a targeting handle. If you can build a molecule that latches onto P S M A, you can, in effect, tag prostate cancer cells wherever they are in the body.

Second, the drug. Pluvicto is a small molecule with two ends. One end is a P S M A-binding ligand that latches onto the receptor with high affinity. The other end holds a radioactive atom — lutetium one seventy-seven, an isotope that emits beta particles, which are electrons traveling at high speed. Beta radiation does its damage over a short distance — a few millimeters — which is exactly what you want. You infuse the drug into the bloodstream, it circulates, it accumulates specifically on cells that express P S M A, and then it delivers a targeted dose of radiation directly to those cells and their immediate neighbors, and nowhere else. Radioligand therapy, in short — a specific isotope delivered to a specific target by a specific ligand.

Pluvicto has been approved since twenty-twenty-two for prostate cancer that had already stopped responding to hormone therapy — that is, castration-resistant disease — and had failed chemotherapy. In twenty-twenty-five that was expanded to castration-resistant patients before chemotherapy. What happened Thursday is the biggest jump yet. It is now approved earlier still — for hormone-sensitive metastatic disease. Meaning, at the point where a patient is first diagnosed with metastatic prostate cancer that is still responding to the initial hormonal therapy.

The trial is called PSMAddition. Eleven hundred and forty-four patients with newly diagnosed hormone-sensitive metastatic prostate cancer, all P S M A-positive on imaging, all on androgen deprivation therapy plus a next-generation androgen receptor pathway inhibitor — that is the standard-of-care backbone. Randomized one-to-one, they either got Pluvicto layered on top or they got the standard of care alone. Primary endpoint was radiographic progression-free survival — the time until the cancer visibly progresses on imaging, or the patient dies. Adding Pluvicto reduced the risk of progression or death by roughly twenty-eight percent — hazard ratio zero-point-seven-two. The overall survival trend favored the Pluvicto arm, hazard ratio zero-point-eight-oh, though those data are not yet mature, meaning not enough deaths have accrued to declare a statistically clean survival benefit.

There is one nuance in the F D A's approval that is worth naming. Approximately a third of men with metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer never reach an undetectable P S A level on the standard doublet of A D T plus an A R P I — meaning, initial therapy is not enough for them — and about half progress to castration-resistant disease within twenty months. The rationale for pushing radioligand therapy earlier is that intensifying up front, in the patients most likely to fail standard therapy, may buy more time than salvaging with Pluvicto later. Whether the mature overall survival data will confirm that logic is what the field is going to be watching. This approval nearly doubles the eligible patient population and is expected to expand demand for lutetium one seventy-seven manufacturing capacity, which has been a persistent bottleneck for the field.

On A I in biomedicine.

A paper submitted to arXiv over the weekend from Titus Brinker's group at the German Cancer Research Center, with clinicians from the Medical University of Vienna, TU Dresden, and the Pontifical Catholic University of Chile, tests a specific hypothesis about how large language models should be deployed for clinical decision support. Namely — does adding source citations help?

The intuition behind citations is straightforward. If an L L M gives me an answer and also shows me the evidence from the medical literature it is basing the answer on, I can check the sources for myself. That is exactly how a colleague would communicate — with references. So retrieval-augmented generation, where the model is given the ability to search a corpus of medical literature and cite what it retrieves, has become the default for high-stakes clinical use.

The team built a specific system for this — an agentic retrieval-augmented L L M they call CORA — and ran a study with forty-six physicians. Each physician answered clinical questions on their own, then answered the same or similar questions with CORA's assistance. And CORA, when it answered, also displayed which passages from the medical literature it was drawing on.

Two headline results.

The good one first. Physician accuracy went from seventy-one percent unaided to eighty-three percent with CORA. Citation-supported answers were also more likely to be correct than uncited ones — eighty-eight percent of citation-supported answers were right, versus sixty-six percent of uncited answers. So on average, citations are a good signal.

The bad one. The paper measures something called adoption asymmetry — how citations change physician behavior when the L L M's answer is right versus wrong. When the L L M was right, adding a citation moved physician adoption of the correct answer from thirty-four percent to seventy-seven percent — good, that is what you want. But when the L L M was wrong, and it also displayed a citation supporting the wrong answer, physician resistance to that wrong answer collapsed. From ninety-two percent — meaning, unaided physicians reject a wrong L L M answer nine times out of ten — down to thirty-five percent, meaning, once the wrong answer is dressed with a supporting citation, physicians reject it only about a third of the time.

The specific worry here is not that L L Ms are wrong sometimes. That is unavoidable. The worry is that citations do not function as a neutral signal — they function as a trust amplifier that operates the same way on wrong answers as on right ones. The physicians in the study weren't lazy — the paper explicitly says they *checked* the citations. But when the cited passage looked authoritative and topical, they seem to have interpreted its presence as endorsement of the L L M's conclusion, even when the passage itself did not actually support the specific claim.

That is a design problem that goes beyond this particular system. Every retrieval-augmented clinical L L M being deployed today — whether at large academic centers, in ambient scribes, or through electronic-medical-record integrations — shares this failure mode structurally. The intervention this paper suggests is not obvious. Something in the interface has to make it clear when a citation actually grounds the answer versus when a citation is merely topically related. That is a hard user-interface problem, and it is where the field is going to have to move.

And finally, animals.

A new genus of beetle was described in the open-access taxonomy journal ZooKeys, in a paper led by Fang-Shuo Hu and Alexey Solodovnikov at the Natural History Museum of Denmark. Two new species, both from Asia — one from the broadleaf forests of Yunnan Province in southern China, the other from the mountains of Louang Namtha in northern Laos.

Some context. Rove beetles, family Staphylinidae, are the largest family of beetles in the world — north of sixty-three thousand described species, probably many multiples more still undescribed. They are, on average, small, elongate, and predatory. What makes this genus stand out is a set of anatomical features that are unusually stretched. The mandibles — the biting jaws — are longer and thinner than in any closely related group. So are the antennae, and so are the maxillary palps, the accessory mouthparts beetles use for handling and manipulating food. The Danish team named the genus Luffy, after Monkey D. Luffy, the protagonist of the manga and anime One Piece, whose body — after eating one of the fruit-based magical items in that universe — is famously rubbery and stretchy. The two species are Luffy schillhammeri, named for an Austrian entomologist, and Luffy nika, named for Luffy's awakening form in the manga. That is a joke a lot of the target audience will get and no one else will.

The scientific significance, beyond the name, is that this genus is the sister group of a lineage of beetles called Eucibdelus — a group of rove beetles whose evolutionary origins have not been well resolved. Luffy fills a phylogenetic gap. The description also implies these beetles evolved their elongated mouthparts convergently — the same anatomical solution appearing independently in more than one beetle lineage. Something about a hunting strategy in the leaf litter of mountain forests apparently favors long jaws.
