# Episode 42: Why influenza B Yamagata seems to have gone extinct

## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Thursday, August sixth, twenty-twenty-six. Three items today. First, on pathogen watch — a new preprint from Pej Rohani's group at the University of Georgia offers a compact mathematical explanation for one of the more striking events in recent respiratory-virus epidemiology, which is the apparent extinction of the Yamagata lineage of influenza B during the COVID pandemic. Second, on the business side — Moderna dosed the first participants this week in a Phase one trial of an mRNA vaccine against Bundibugyo ebolavirus, the strain driving an ongoing outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo that has now recorded more than three thousand seven hundred confirmed cases and more than sixteen hundred deaths. And finally, animals — a fossil from outback Queensland preserves, inside a single skeleton, a three-tier prehistoric food chain: a pterosaur eaten by an ichthyosaur, which was then eaten by a giant marine reptile called Kronosaurus. There is no AI-in-biomedicine or AI-tools segment today — nothing genuinely fresh in the last day or two rose above the noise. On with the show.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Some background, because this story only lands if you know the setup. Influenza B, unlike influenza A, has no animal reservoir. It circulates almost exclusively in humans. For decades before the COVID pandemic, influenza B was split between two co-circulating lineages named Yamagata and Victoria, after the Japanese cities where their reference strains were first isolated in the eighties. Both lineages differed enough antigenically that quadrivalent seasonal flu vaccines carried components for both — two A strains and two B strains, Yamagata and Victoria — precisely because immunity to one B lineage did not fully cross-protect against the other. That was the standard picture from the mid-two-thousands until about twenty-twenty.

Then something happened. Beginning in the twenty-twenty-twenty-twenty-one flu season, the peak COVID period when non-pharmaceutical interventions collapsed respiratory transmission globally, Yamagata effectively vanished from human surveillance. No confirmed detections. Not for one season, but through twenty-twenty-one, twenty-twenty-two, twenty-twenty-three, and now into twenty-twenty-six. The W-H-O in twenty-twenty-four recommended dropping the Yamagata component from trivalent and quadrivalent influenza vaccines on the grounds that continuing to include it was almost certainly manufacturing dead antigen. That is a formal declaration that a virus lineage that circulated globally for decades appears to be extinct in humans. And in a pathogen that has no animal reservoir to hide in, extinct probably means extinct.

The question the field has been trying to answer since is: how did this happen? A brief drop in transmission during lockdown pushed a lot of viruses down, but only Yamagata failed to come back. That asymmetry is the thing that needs explaining, and the paper posted this week on medRxiv from Manuel Antonio Gutierrez in the Rohani lab, with coauthors from the University of Georgia's flu-immunology group, offers what they describe as a parsimonious explanation using a specific mathematical model.

Let me set up the terminology, because the finding rests entirely on it.

*Cross-immunity* is the protection an infection with one strain confers against a different strain of the same pathogen. If you catch Victoria this year, your immune system generates antibodies and T-cell memory that partially recognize Yamagata as well, so if you're exposed to Yamagata next month, you may not get infected — or you may get a milder infection. That's cross-immunity.

*Asymmetric cross-immunity* is when the protection isn't equal in both directions. Infection with Victoria might provide strong protection against Yamagata, while infection with Yamagata provides only weak protection against Victoria — or the reverse. In the flu B case, there is direct experimental and epidemiological evidence for this kind of asymmetry, driven by the specific antigenic sites the two lineages have diverged at.

*Waning immunity* is the fact that whatever protection you accumulate fades over time — antibody titers drop, T-cell memory attenuates. This is particularly important for pathogens that evolve antigenically, because by the time your immunity has waned enough for reinfection, the pathogen may have drifted enough that reinfection looks like a new exposure anyway.

Now the model. The paper analyzes a two-strain SIRS model — Susceptible, Infected, Recovered, back to Susceptible — with strain-specific rates of transmission, recovery, and waning of immunity, and with asymmetric cross-immunity between the two strains. And what they derive from this model is a mathematical object called an *invasion threshold* — a boundary in parameter space that determines whether both strains can stably coexist, or whether one drives the other extinct. Under certain combinations of the asymmetric cross-immunity and waning-immunity parameters, coexistence is stable and the two strains circulate side by side. Outside that region, only one persists.

Here is the punchline. The authors show that if you have two strains sitting in a *stable* coexistence equilibrium — which is exactly what Yamagata and Victoria were doing globally for two decades — a *transient* reduction in transmission, even a brief one, can push the pair across an invasion boundary. Once you cross that boundary, the weaker strain goes extinct. Not eventually. Structurally. The system is on the wrong side of the line now and it doesn't matter that transmission recovers. The equilibrium you're heading toward no longer has the weaker strain in it.

Applied to influenza B, this says: the COVID-era transmission crash was not a mere numerical reduction that took Yamagata below detectable and it happened to fail to bounce back. It was a *phase transition*. A brief transmission shock moved the two lineages across a coexistence boundary, and Yamagata had been on the losing side of the asymmetric cross-immunity all along.

Two things worth taking from this for a working listener. First, this is a mechanism-level explanation for what would otherwise be a coincidence. It says: any two-strain pathogen system with strong enough asymmetry can be pushed across an invasion boundary by a transient shock, and the weaker strain doesn't come back. If that framework is right, the disappearance of Yamagata is not particularly surprising in hindsight — it's what the system was set up to do given a big enough shove. Second, it changes what to watch for going forward. Any respiratory intervention large enough to durably suppress transmission is not just buying time on incidence — it may be *selecting between coexisting lineages* in ways that permanently reshape which strains circulate afterward. That is a policy consideration you don't normally see raised in the transmission-reduction literature. Going off the abstract here — the full body of the medRxiv preprint hadn't rendered when I pulled it — but the mathematical result is self-contained and consistent with what the pandemic-era surveillance data actually shows.

On the business side.

Moderna announced this week that the first participants have been dosed in the Phase one clinical trial of mRNA-fourteen-sixty-nine, its mRNA vaccine candidate against Bundibugyo ebolavirus. Dosing began August fourth, following Health Canada's authorization of the trial protocol. And the timing here is what makes this worth paying attention to — this is not a trial designed for a future outbreak. It is being dosed into an ongoing one.

Some context on Bundibugyo. There are four species of ebolavirus known to cause disease in humans — Zaire, Sudan, Bundibugyo, and Taï Forest — and they are antigenically distinct enough that a vaccine against one does not necessarily protect against another. The two licensed ebolavirus vaccines currently on the market — Merck's Ervebo, which is a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus expressing the Zaire ebolavirus glycoprotein, and Johnson and Johnson's two-dose Zabdeno-and-Mvabea regimen — are both Zaire-specific. There is no licensed vaccine against Bundibugyo. Which matters, because the outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has by the WHO's most recent situation report — August first — reached three thousand seven hundred and forty-eight confirmed cases and one thousand six hundred and fifty-seven confirmed deaths. That makes it the second-largest Ebola outbreak on record. It has been declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern by the WHO, and the response has been running for months on off-label use of the Zaire vaccine — which no one thinks is fully protective against Bundibugyo — plus supportive care and contact tracing.

The Moderna vaccine, mRNA-fourteen-sixty-nine, is a lipid-nanoparticle-encapsulated messenger RNA that encodes the Bundibugyo glycoprotein — the surface protein the virus uses to enter host cells and the dominant target of neutralizing antibodies. This is the same architectural template as Moderna's COVID vaccine: the mRNA is delivered intramuscularly, taken up by host cells, translated into the viral glycoprotein, and displayed on the surface where the immune system can generate a targeted humoral and cellular response. The advantage of the mRNA platform for Ebola specifically is speed — you can respecify the antigen just by changing the mRNA sequence, without redesigning a manufacturing process. If you get Zaire-specific protection working via mRNA, standing up a Bundibugyo-specific product is fundamentally a sequence swap, not a new pipeline.

The trial itself is small. Approximately eighty healthy adult participants across three Canadian sites, evaluating safety, tolerability, and immune response. It is a Phase one, meaning the endpoint is essentially "does the vaccine trigger an antibody response and does it not cause unacceptable adverse events," not efficacy against actual Bundibugyo challenge — Phase one Ebola vaccine trials cannot be efficacy trials because there is no ethical way to challenge participants with the virus. The Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations — CEPI — is providing up to fifty million dollars for the preclinical and Phase one work, and Moderna has committed under the CEPI access model to provide a minimum of five hundred thousand doses to low- and middle-income countries at access pricing if the vaccine reaches licensure.

Two things to take from this. First, this is CEPI's model of pandemic preparedness working close to as it was designed to. Bundibugyo is an outbreak-relevant pathogen with no licensed vaccine and no natural market; without an ex-market funding mechanism that pays a manufacturer to develop it anyway, you get exactly the situation the response is in right now — an active PHEIC being run on off-label Zaire vaccine and supportive care. CEPI-funded Moderna work goes some distance to fixing that structural gap. Second, this is a real-world test of whether the mRNA-platform-for-outbreak-pathogens promise translates. The COVID mRNA vaccines demonstrated speed of manufacture and clinical development against a novel pathogen; the Bundibugyo trial demonstrates whether the platform can be pointed at a filovirus glycoprotein and generate protective immunity as cleanly as it did for a spike protein. If it works, the same infrastructure is potentially pre-positioned against Sudan ebolavirus, against Marburg, against every filovirus we don't currently have a vaccine for. Phase one first, but the platform question is what to watch.

And finally, animals.

A fossil recovered from a public fossicking site outside the town of Richmond, in outback Queensland, and described in the journal Gondwana Research this summer, contains inside a single skeleton the direct evidence of a three-tier prehistoric food chain. This is genuinely a first — a fossil recording, in one specimen, a predator eating a predator that had itself just eaten prey.

Some context on the setting. About one hundred million years ago, in the Early Cretaceous, much of what is now central and northern Australia was underwater. An inland sea called the Eromanga Sea covered a huge portion of the continent. In that sea lived the animals this fossil belongs to. The main character, so to speak, is an ichthyosaur — a dolphin-shaped marine reptile, in this case a specimen of the species Platypterygius australis, between six and seven meters long. Ichthyosaurs are the classic example of convergent evolution: they were reptiles, closely related to lizards on land, but they had evolved essentially the body plan of a modern dolphin — streamlined, tailed, fully aquatic, live-bearing. This particular ichthyosaur was found by visitors to the fossicking site in August of twenty-nineteen, was informally named B-O-B for "Bag of Bones," and then in twenty-twenty-two the curator of the Kronosaurus Korner Museum, Kevin Petersen, returned to the site and uncovered the rest of the specimen. The team, led by Matt White at the University of New England and Dean Lomax, published the description this summer.

Here is what makes this specimen special. When the researchers used synchrotron C-T scanning at the Australian Nuclear Science and Technology Organisation to look inside rock nodules still cemented to the ichthyosaur's skeleton — nodules positioned in what would have been its stomach cavity — they found undigested prey. Specifically, they found fish remains, belemnite hooks — belemnites being squid-like Cretaceous cephalopods — shell fragments, and, unexpectedly, pterosaur jaw fragments. A pterosaur is a flying reptile, in that era one of the airborne dominants over the same sea. So the ichthyosaur, before it died, had eaten a pterosaur.

That's tier two: pterosaur inside ichthyosaur. Now the third tier. The ichthyosaur's own skeleton bears extensive damage — enormous crushing bite marks, shattered bones, entire sections missing. The pattern is consistent with attack or scavenging by an even larger predator. And the almost-certain culprit is Kronosaurus queenslandicus, a pliosaur — a short-necked, huge-headed marine reptile — that grew to roughly ten to eleven meters and was the apex predator of the Eromanga Sea. The museum where this specimen ended up is named after that animal for a reason.

So what you are looking at, encoded in a single fossilized skeleton, is: a pterosaur that flew over the sea, caught by an ichthyosaur that hunted below the waves, which was then killed and partially eaten by a giant pliosaur cruising the same water. Three trophic levels, three animals, one snapshot. The oldest documented example of that kind of preservation ever found. The specimen is now on display at the Kronosaurus Korner Museum in Queensland.
