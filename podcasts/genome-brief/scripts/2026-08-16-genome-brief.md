## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Sunday, August sixteenth, twenty twenty six. Today: the ongoing Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo just became the largest ever recorded in that country, and it's driven by an unusual strain that has never caused an outbreak this size before. On the business side, a small biotech reports a Phase three win for a single-dose LSD tablet in generalized anxiety disorder, and the effect size is unlike anything the field has seen. In the AI world, a Singapore-based group unveils a deep-learning framework that infers three-dimensional genome structure directly from RNA sequencing across twelve thousand tumors, and it flags a repurposing hit that nobody would have predicted — an old antibiotic. Alibaba drops a twenty-seven-billion parameter open-weight model that runs on a single consumer graphics card and beats frontier models on several coding benchmarks. And to close, a Hamburg group has cracked the genetics behind why male redback spiders literally somersault into their partner's fangs during sex.

Let's get into it.

First, pathogen watch.

The World Health Organization has published a fresh Disease Outbreak News item on the Ebola situation in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, dated August fourteenth. The numbers are stark and the story has quietly become one of the most important public-health emergencies of the year. As of August twelfth, there are four thousand six hundred and eighty-six cumulative confirmed cases, and two thousand one hundred and eighty-six deaths — a crude case fatality ratio of forty-six point eight percent. Four thousand six hundred and sixty-five of those cases are in the DRC itself, with twenty in Uganda, one in France, and one in Germany from cross-border spread. The outbreak was declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern back on May seventeenth, and it has now expanded from its initial location in Mongbwalu, in Ituri province, to fifty-four health zones across six provinces. Peak weekly cases hit five hundred and seventy-nine in early August, with three hundred and four deaths in that single week. This is now the largest Ebola outbreak ever reported in the DRC.

Let me back up and explain what's actually happening here, because there are a few things about this outbreak that make it different from the ones the biomedical community has been most trained on.

The Ebola genus contains several distinct species. When most people hear "Ebola" they think of Zaire ebolavirus, which caused the twenty fourteen West Africa outbreak with roughly twenty-eight thousand cases and eleven thousand deaths. That is the strain the licensed vaccine — Ervebo — is designed against, and it's the strain that most of the therapeutic monoclonal antibody products are targeted at as well. But this current DRC outbreak is not Zaire. It's caused by Bundibugyo virus, which is a genetically distinct ebolavirus first identified in western Uganda in two thousand and seven. Historically Bundibugyo has caused smaller, more contained outbreaks with case fatality ratios in the twenty-five to forty percent range. It has never before caused an outbreak of this scale. Four thousand six hundred cases dwarfs every previous Bundibugyo event by more than an order of magnitude.

Why does the identity of the virus matter? Because Ervebo — the workhorse Ebola vaccine — was designed and licensed against Zaire ebolavirus. Its glycoprotein, the immune target, is Zaire-derived. Cross-protection against Bundibugyo is a real biological question, not a settled one. The animal-model literature suggests some cross-reactivity, but the field has never had to lean on Ervebo in a Bundibugyo emergency at this scale. Regulatory-authorized vaccine trials against Bundibugyo are being prepared in the DRC, and the WHO update notes that treatment expansion is underway and that a clinical trial called PARTNERS — for therapeutic monoclonal antibodies — began enrollment on July second, with more than a hundred patients dosed across three facilities in Ituri.

The mechanistic issue with cross-protection is worth unpacking. Ebolaviruses infect cells by using a surface glycoprotein — call it GP — to bind and fuse with human cells. Neutralizing antibodies raised by Ervebo are tuned to the Zaire GP. Bundibugyo's GP differs by roughly forty percent at the amino-acid level, and the immunodominant epitopes — the specific parts of the protein that antibodies actually recognize — differ enough that off-the-shelf neutralization is a lot weaker. That doesn't mean the vaccine is useless, but it does mean the response is not the same as against a matched strain. This is one of the very live scientific questions this outbreak is going to answer.

The other thing worth understanding is why this outbreak is so much larger than previous Bundibugyo events. Part of it is geography — Ituri is a densely populated, conflict-affected region where movement is difficult to track and where healthcare infrastructure was already strained. Part of it is late detection: retrospective analysis suggests the outbreak likely began at least a month before the index cluster was identified, giving the virus a substantial head start. And part of it may reflect subtle changes in the virus itself — genomic surveillance from the DRC is publishing sequences to shared databases, and the coming weeks will tell whether we're looking at a Bundibugyo lineage that behaves differently in humans, or a normal Bundibugyo virus that got a jump on containment. For a biomedical audience the practical takeaway is: watch this outbreak, watch the sequencing, and watch the Ervebo-versus-Bundibugyo cross-protection data as it comes out of the trials in Ituri. This is likely to reshape how we think about pan-ebolavirus vaccines and stockpiling strategy for years.

On the business side.

Four days ago, a small Nasdaq-listed biotech called Definium Therapeutics reported topline Phase three results for a drug called DT one twenty in adults with generalized anxiety disorder. The result is genuinely eye-catching and the drug is worth explaining carefully because it's not a small molecule of the kind the pharmaceutical industry usually files GAD approvals on — it's a single-dose orally disintegrating tablet containing lysergide, which is the pharmacological name for LSD.

Let me set the context. Generalized anxiety disorder — GAD — is chronic, disabling, and much more common than headline mental-illness diagnoses like schizophrenia. Standard-of-care treatment has been unchanged for roughly two decades: SSRIs, SNRIs, and off-label benzodiazepines. Effect sizes for those drugs, in registration trials, are usually small — placebo-adjusted improvements of two to three points on the standard rating scale, called the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale or HAM-A. Response takes weeks. The last novel mechanism approved for GAD was buspirone in the nineteen eighties, though various SNRI extensions have come along since. So the field has been stuck.

The Voyage trial that Definium reported was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase three study enrolling two hundred and fourteen adults across roughly thirty-five US sites. Half received a single one-hundred microgram dose of DT one twenty; half received placebo. Both groups had baseline HAM-A scores around twenty-seven to twenty-eight, which is the moderate-to-severe range. The primary endpoint was the change in HAM-A at week twelve — twelve weeks after that single dose. The DT one twenty arm dropped by eleven point six points. The placebo arm dropped by six point two. That's a placebo-adjusted difference of five point four points, with a p value below zero point zero zero zero one and an effect size — Cohen's d — of zero point eight one. For context, an effect size around zero point three is what a typical SSRI generates. Zero point eight one is what the psychiatry field calls a "large" effect and something you almost never see in a placebo-controlled anxiety trial. Forty-three percent of treated patients hit the responder threshold — a fifty percent HAM-A reduction — versus sixteen percent on placebo. Fourteen percent hit full remission, versus four percent.

Now — mechanism. LSD is a partial agonist at the serotonin two A receptor, or 5-HT2A. That's the same receptor psilocybin and DMT hit, and it's the receptor most closely associated with the acute psychedelic experience — visual changes, altered sense of self, and so on. The prevailing hypothesis for why a single dose can produce weeks-to-months of benefit is that 5-HT2A activation triggers a broad, sustained increase in neuroplasticity — measurable in animal models as dendritic spine growth and synaptogenesis across cortical circuits. In humans, imaging shows changes in resting-state network connectivity that persist for weeks. The therapeutic idea is that a single dosing session, combined with psychological preparation and integration support, resets patterns of anxious cognition in a way that ordinary daily-dosed SSRIs cannot.

There are two reasons this result is important beyond the drug itself. The first is regulatory. If the second Phase three trial — called Panorama, reading out in September — replicates these numbers, this becomes the first genuinely approvable classical-psychedelic medicine. The FDA rejected Lykos Therapeutics' MDMA-for-PTSD application last year over methodology and safety concerns, and the field has been in a kind of purgatory ever since. A clean second Phase three win for LSD in a different indication would restart the psychedelic-medicine narrative from a much stronger evidence base. The second reason is commercial and clinical. Anxiety is enormous. A one-dose, in-clinic administration model — with roughly a five-and-a-half point effect size that persists for months — would represent a genuinely different economic and clinical structure than SSRI prescribing. Safety in Voyage looked clean: adverse events were mild to moderate, confined to the dosing day, and no suicidality signal. The dose is a hundred micrograms, which is enough to produce a real psychedelic experience but is on the lower end of what recreational users take.

Bear in mind: these are topline results from a company press release. The full data won't be in a journal for months and will be scrutinized by everyone. But the effect size is large enough that even substantial pruning wouldn't move the answer.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

Two days ago, a group led out of the National University of Singapore posted a preprint on bioRxiv describing a deep-learning framework they call AI4Loop, which does something scientifically ambitious and clinically interesting. I'll flag upfront that I'm working from the abstract and the preprint's key figures here — the full body text isn't fully rendered yet on bioRxiv.

Here's what they built. Three-dimensional chromatin architecture — the way DNA is looped and folded inside the nucleus — is one of the most important layers of gene regulation. Enhancers that sit far away on the linear DNA sequence can be brought into physical contact with a gene's promoter through loops mediated by proteins like CTCF and cohesin, and those loops determine which genes get switched on. The standard way to measure these interactions is a family of assays called Hi-C, which chemically cross-link nearby DNA regions before sequencing to figure out which segments were touching in three-dimensional space. Hi-C is powerful but expensive and technically demanding, so we have Hi-C data on maybe a few hundred well-studied cell lines, and essentially nothing at scale on primary human tumors.

What the AI4Loop team did is train a deep-learning model to infer gene-centered chromatin interaction networks directly from RNA sequencing data — which is cheap, ubiquitous, and available for tens of thousands of tumors. The intuition, in plain language, is that co-regulated genes have correlated expression, and specific patterns of co-expression can be used as a fingerprint of the underlying three-dimensional structure. Train the model on cell lines where you have both RNA-seq and Hi-C, learn the mapping, then apply it to samples where you only have RNA-seq.

They ran the model over twelve thousand three hundred forty-seven tumor transcriptomes spanning thirty-two cancer types, and the headline finding is that gene-centered chromatin interactions are pervasively increased in tumors compared to matched normal tissue — particularly at loci surrounding known oncogenes. The interaction networks alone outperformed gene expression alone at classifying cancer type, suggesting the three-dimensional structure carries independent biological information.

The most surprising piece is the drug-repurposing hit. The team cross-referenced their tumor interaction maps against more than fifty thousand transcriptomes from cell lines treated with different drugs, looking for compounds whose treatment signature reverses the tumor-associated interaction gains. Two hits came out: eperezolid and radezolid. These are both oxazolidinone antibiotics — the same class as linezolid, used clinically for gram-positive infections. They confirmed the finding with actual Hi-C experiments showing that treating breast cancer cells with these antibiotics does reduce the aberrant chromatin loops.

Why would antibiotics affect human chromatin structure? Two ideas worth flagging. Oxazolidinones inhibit the bacterial ribosome by targeting the fifty-S subunit, but they also inhibit human mitochondrial protein synthesis, because our mitochondrial ribosome is bacterial in origin. Mitochondrial dysfunction cascades into altered cellular metabolism, and metabolic changes are known to feed back into chromatin state — think of things like alpha-ketoglutarate influencing DNA demethylation. So one plausible mechanism is indirect, through mitochondrial-metabolic-chromatin coupling. A more direct alternative is that the oxazolidinones may have an off-target eukaryotic effect nobody has characterized. Either way, this is a computational-to-experimental repurposing pipeline that generated a testable, non-obvious hypothesis and then confirmed it.

Why it matters: this is a decent example of the maturing playbook for AI in translational biology — a foundation-model-style prediction layer that turns cheap data — RNA-seq — into expensive data — chromatin architecture — then a systematic screen against pharmacologic databases to generate falsifiable drug hypotheses, then wet-lab confirmation. The specific claim about oxazolidinones is small in isolation; the pattern of how it was generated is not.

Now, AI tools.

The other big news two days ago came out of Hangzhou. On August fourteenth, Alibaba's Qwen team released Qwen three point eight twenty-seven B, an open-weight model under the Apache two license. Twenty-seven billion parameters. A native context window of two hundred sixty-two thousand tokens, extendable to a million. And, notably, a hybrid attention architecture that mixes standard full-attention layers with something called gated delta-net linear attention, which makes very long context economically feasible in a way traditional transformers can't achieve.

The reason to flag this, even though the show generally skips model bumps: the benchmarks are unusually strong for something small enough to run on a consumer graphics card. On the SWE-bench Pro coding benchmark, the model reportedly scores sixty-one point seven; Claude Opus four point eight scores fifty-three point four on the same test. On LiveCodeBench version six, ninety point three versus eighty-eight point eight for Opus. On the OSWorld computer-use benchmark, eighty-four point three versus seventy-two point seven. On AndroidWorld, eighty-one point nine versus sixty-two. Opus is still ahead on knowledge-heavy tests like GPQA Diamond and on Terminal-Bench, but for a twenty-seven-billion parameter open-weight model that fits on a single high-end consumer GPU when quantized, this is a genuinely different capability tier than what open-weight releases were producing even six months ago.

Two reasons this matters for a biomedical audience. First, workflows that involve sensitive data — patient records, clinical trial datasets, unpublished sequencing data — often can't be sent to cloud APIs. An open-weight model with strong agentic and coding capability that runs on hardware a lab could actually own changes what's feasible in a locked-down environment. Second, the pricing implications ripple upward. When frontier-adjacent capability is available for the cost of a graphics card, hosted models have to justify their pricing differently. That pressure is a large part of what has been pushing model prices down across the industry — the trend we talked about yesterday with Gemini three point seven Flash and the workhorse tier.

And finally, animals.

A team led by Kardelen Özgün Uludağ, a behavioral biologist at the University of Hamburg, published a paper in Biology Letters on August twelfth that finally makes sense of one of the more disturbing spectacles in the animal world. Male redback spiders — Latrodectus hasselti, the Australian relative of the black widow — perform something called the sexual somersault. During copulation, the male flips his abdomen into the female's fangs, letting her sink them in and begin to consume him while he continues to mate. A majority of the time, he gets eaten completely. When he does survive, he's often too damaged to mate again anyway.

Only two of the thirty-one known widow-spider species do this. Everyone else — the black widow, the false widow, and so on — just mates and leaves. So the obvious evolutionary question is: why? And, mechanistically, what's the genetic difference?

The team crossed redbacks with a closely related New Zealand species called the katipō, which does not somersault and diverged from the redback around a hundred thousand years ago. They then filmed a hundred and four hybrid matings with infrared cameras and scored the offspring for two separate traits: whether they performed the somersault, and whether they used an abdominal-constriction escape maneuver that lets some males survive being eaten. Both traits are controlled by loci on the spider's X chromosome — but the somersault and the constriction don't inherit together. Roughly half of hybrid males did one without the other. Which tells you the two behaviors, though they always occur as a package in the wild, evolved as separate genetic modules.

Why does he do it in the first place? The best explanation, backed by prior behavioral work, is paternity. Female redbacks are large, hungry, and highly likely to mate with multiple males. By offering himself as a meal, the male gets to prolong copulation and transfer more sperm — enough to significantly bias fertilization toward his own genes over any subsequent male's. Being eaten is the cost of siring more offspring. And the earlier behavioral literature bears that out: males who complete the somersault sire a significantly larger share of the female's brood than males who don't.

The nice part of this paper is the resolution. The behavior looks like a single, evolved unit, but genetically it's built from at least two separately inheritable pieces. That's an increasingly common finding across behavioral genetics — complex behaviors are usually additive combinations of simpler behavioral modules, each with its own genetic wiring, and evolution assembles them into what looks like one integrated act.
