## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Sunday, August second, 2026. Three items today. First, on pathogen watch — a group at the Lebanese American University published a small but pointed case study this week showing that within a single patient, over the course of about three weeks of hospitalization, a single clonal lineage of carbapenem-resistant E. coli swapped out one carbapenem-destroying enzyme for a completely different one. Not by mutating the gene — by trading the plasmid the gene was on. And that is a scenario the surveillance playbook was not really written for. Second, on the business side — Novo Nordisk's big cardiovascular bet, an antibody called ziltivekimab that suppresses inflammation, failed its pivotal Phase three trial on Friday. Sixty-three hundred patients. The drug worked biologically — it hit its target, dropped the inflammation markers exactly as expected — and it did not reduce heart attacks, strokes, or cardiovascular deaths. The stock dropped nine percent, and the broader inflammation hypothesis of atherosclerosis just got its first big randomized rebuke. And finally, animals — paleontologists at the Natural History Museum in London named a new dinosaur from the shores of Lake Kariba in Zimbabwe this week. Only the fifth ever named from that country, a lightly-built early ancestor of the giant long-necked sauropods, about the size of a pig, and its skeleton shows it had survived and healed from some kind of serious injury before it died.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales — CRE — are one of the more feared categories of bacterial infection in the hospital. The name refers to gut bacteria like E. coli and Klebsiella that have acquired resistance to carbapenems, which are the beta-lactam antibiotics we reach for when the usual antibiotics have failed. When CRE cause a bloodstream infection, mortality is high — historically twenty to fifty percent — and the World Health Organization ranks them at the top of its critical-priority list for new antibiotics.

The way CRE become resistant is almost always by acquiring an enzyme called a carbapenemase — a beta-lactamase specifically capable of chewing up carbapenems. There are several distinct carbapenemase families with different geographic distributions. OXA-forty-eight is common in the Middle East, North Africa, and Europe. New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase — NDM — is common on the Indian subcontinent and increasingly worldwide. KPC dominates the Americas. Which family a bug is carrying matters enormously for treatment, because the newer beta-lactamase-inhibitor combinations don't cover all of them. Ceftazidime-avibactam, for example, works against OXA-forty-eight and KPC but not against NDM.

The standard clinical assumption when the same bug is recovered from the same patient a few weeks apart — same species, same sequence type, same clonal fingerprint — is that it's the same bug. If it's gotten more resistant, the story is usually chromosomal — a porin channel gets shut, a resistance gene gets upregulated, the same carbapenemase produces more of itself. What the paper this week — from Sima Tokajian's group at the Lebanese American University, first author Léa Kassabian, posted to bioRxiv on Wednesday — documents is a case where that assumption is wrong in a very clean way.

The setup. A single hospitalized patient. Two E. coli isolates recovered twenty-five days apart — the first from a urinary tract infection, the second, weeks later, from a bloodstream infection. Whole-genome sequencing confirms both are the same clonal lineage: sequence type three-sixty-one, phylogroup A, serotype O-nontypeable H-thirty, separated by only twenty-eight single-nucleotide differences across the whole core genome. By any reasonable microbiological standard these are the same bug. But their carbapenemase content is not the same.

The first isolate carried OXA-twelve-oh-seven, a recently described member of the OXA-forty-eight family, on a conjugative plasmid — meaning a plasmid capable of transferring itself between bacteria via direct contact. That plasmid was of a specific plasmid backbone type — I-F-two. The second isolate had *lost* OXA-twelve-oh-seven entirely and *gained* NDM-four, on a completely different plasmid backbone — an I-X-three, which is a globally disseminated backbone that spreads NDM-family enzymes efficiently. The plasmid the group found in the second isolate is nearly identical to one previously described from an unrelated E. coli lineage — meaning what happened inside this patient looks like a horizontal acquisition event: the bug shed one carbapenemase-carrying plasmid and picked up a different carbapenemase-carrying plasmid from some other member of the patient's gut flora.

And the phenotype tracked the genotype exactly. The first isolate was still susceptible to imipenem and meropenem — the two workhorse carbapenems — and resistant only to ertapenem. The second isolate was uniformly high-level resistant to all three, plus resistant to ceftazidime-avibactam, which the first was susceptible to. If the treating team had assumed continuity — same bug, same drugs work — the empiric therapy for the bloodstream infection would have failed.

Why this matters for a working scientist. Two reasons.

First, the mechanism. The dominant models for how within-host resistance escalates in a hospitalized patient are gene-level: a point mutation in a porin, a copy-number increase in an existing resistance gene, a promoter tune-up. Those are all *cis* changes to the pre-existing genotype. What this paper describes is *trans*: the bug's chromosome barely changed, but its accessory genome — the mobile plasmids that carry a huge fraction of resistance determinants — was almost completely swapped. In a gut environment that's rich in other Enterobacterales, plasmid trading among clonal relatives is fast and mostly invisible unless you're sequencing the plasmids specifically. This case is the first documented within-host succession from an OXA-forty-eight-family carbapenemase to an NDM one, in a single sequential infection. It probably isn't the last.

Second, the surveillance implication. Molecular epidemiology often uses core-genome SNP distances to decide whether two isolates from the same patient are "the same" — and by that criterion, twenty-eight SNPs is well within same-strain distance. But same-strain does not mean same-plasmid, and same-plasmid is what determines what antibiotics you should reach for. This is an argument for routine long-read sequencing of CRE isolates to resolve plasmid content — which is doable now with an Oxford Nanopore or PacBio run, but is not yet standard in most clinical microbiology labs. The alternative is that a patient's second infection gets treated as a resistance-escalation of the first, when it's actually functionally a new infection with different susceptibility.

Small paper, one patient, but the mechanism it demonstrates is generalizable.

On the business side.

Novo Nordisk had a very bad Friday. The company put out results from a Phase three trial called ZEUS — Ziltivekimab in Patients with Cardiovascular, Kidney, and Inflammatory Disease — and the trial failed. The stock closed down about nine percent in New York, about seven-and-a-half percent in Copenhagen. And more importantly than the stock: the trial's failure is a real challenge to a hypothesis that has been running the show in cardiovascular pharmacology for almost a decade.

Some background. Ziltivekimab is a monoclonal antibody that binds and neutralizes interleukin six — I L six — a cytokine that is a central driver of chronic systemic inflammation. Novo Nordisk acquired ziltivekimab in the twenty-twenty-one purchase of a biotech called Corvidia Therapeutics, for one-point-nine billion dollars in cash plus milestones. The rationale for the acquisition, and the trial program that followed, comes from a well-known result from a different drug — canakinumab, from Novartis. Canakinumab targets a different cytokine, interleukin one-beta, which sits upstream of I L six in the inflammation cascade. In the CANTOS trial, published in twenty-seventeen, canakinumab reduced major cardiovascular events in patients who had already had a heart attack, at essentially unchanged L D L cholesterol — which was the first randomized-trial evidence that lowering inflammation, independently of lowering cholesterol, cuts heart attacks. Canakinumab never got approved for cardiovascular indications because it also raised fatal infection risk, but it validated a target — the I L one-beta / I L six axis — and it triggered a decade-long race to find a cardiovascular anti-inflammatory that would work as well and be safer.

Ziltivekimab was that bet. Rather than block I L one-beta upstream, block I L six directly. Give it monthly by injection. Use it in the highest-risk cardiovascular patients — people with established atherosclerotic disease *and* chronic kidney disease *and* elevated inflammation, defined by high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, which is the standard clinical readout for systemic I L six activity. That's the population ZEUS enrolled. More than sixty-three hundred patients on top of best-available background therapy, randomized to fifteen milligrams of ziltivekimab monthly or placebo, followed for cardiovascular death, non-fatal heart attack, or non-fatal stroke — the standard three-point MACE endpoint.

Here is what the trial reported. Ziltivekimab did exactly what it was supposed to do biologically. It bound free I L six. It dropped high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. The pharmacodynamic markers were textbook. But the hazard ratio for the primary endpoint was zero-point-nine-nine — with a ninety-five-percent confidence interval that runs from zero-point-eight-eight to one-point-one-one and spans one comfortably in the middle. In plain English: patients on the drug had essentially the same rate of heart attacks, strokes, and cardiovascular deaths as patients on placebo. Analyst consensus going in had been that a fifteen-percent relative risk reduction was the threshold for approval and commercial viability. The trial got zero. Serious infections were more common on ziltivekimab, consistent with what you'd expect from suppressing a cytokine involved in bacterial defense.

Why did this fail? Novo Nordisk's own scientific officer, Martin Holst Lange, gave the honest phrasing on the call: the biology happened; the outcome did not. There are a few candidate explanations. One is that in patients with chronic kidney disease specifically, other drivers of atherosclerosis — uremic toxins, vascular calcification, altered lipid handling — dominate over inflammation. Another is that CANTOS worked in twenty-seventeen because the background therapy was less aggressive than it is now, and modern statins plus P C S K nine inhibitors have already squeezed most of the inflammation-mediated risk out. A third possibility, which some cardiologists are already floating publicly, is that C R P is a reliable *marker* of cardiovascular risk without I L six being a *causal driver* of it — the classic reverse-causality problem that observational epidemiology cannot resolve, and that ZEUS was specifically designed to resolve.

Novo still has two other Phase three cardiovascular-outcome trials of the same drug going: ARTEMIS, in patients who have just had a heart attack, and HERMES, in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Both are expected to read out in the first half of twenty-twenty-seven. Whether either finds a benefit is now genuinely uncertain in a way it wasn't a week ago. But even if ARTEMIS or HERMES lands positive, the ZEUS failure has already changed the landscape — it says the population that seemed most likely to benefit, high-risk atherosclerosis with active inflammation, doesn't. That is a real dent in the inflammation hypothesis of cardiovascular disease.

And finally, animals.

Paul Barrett and colleagues at the Natural History Museum in London, with Zimbabwean and South African collaborators, published a paper this week in the Journal of Systematic Palaeontology describing a new species of Late Triassic dinosaur from the shores of Lake Kariba in northern Zimbabwe. They named it Musango matusadonaensis. It is the fifth dinosaur species ever formally named from Zimbabwe, and the second one described from this specific locality — a spot along Lake Kariba that a few years ago yielded a larger, chunkier sauropodomorph the same team named Musankwa. Two sauropodomorphs, side by side, in the same rocks.

The specimen. About four and a half meters long — think, from head to tail, the length of a small car. About two hundred and twenty-two kilograms, roughly the weight of a large pig. Preserved bones include vertebrae, ribs, limb bones, and pieces of hip and shoulder girdles — a substantial fraction of the skeleton, though not the skull. Growth-ring analysis of the bones puts the animal at about eight years old at death and nearly fully grown. This is one of the more complete Zimbabwean dinosaur skeletons of its age.

A quick word on what a sauropodomorph is. Sauropoda — with an A — is the group of famous long-necked, four-footed plant-eating giants: Diplodocus, Brachiosaurus, Argentinosaurus, animals up to a hundred tons. Sauropodomorpha — with an M — is the wider group that contains sauropods and their earlier, smaller ancestors. Two hundred ten million years ago, in the Late Triassic, sauropodomorphs were not yet giants. They were bipedal or facultatively bipedal, roughly antelope-sized to small-elephant-sized, walked on two legs a lot of the time, and ate plants along the water margins — the paper suggests, for this animal, feeding on vegetation along rivers and streams. Musango sits early on the sauropodomorph family tree; the giants come tens of millions of years later, in the Jurassic and Cretaceous, after the ancestors got much bigger and lost the ability to stand up on two legs.

Two things that are interesting.

First, the two dinosaurs found on the same shoreline are clearly different animals. Musankwa, the earlier find, was much stockier and much heavier — more than twice Musango's weight. Musango is lighter-built with a different hip-and-limb geometry. That means at least two coexisting sauropodomorph lineages were sharing this stretch of Late Triassic southern Africa, which is more dinosaur diversity than the previous fossil record was recording. The paleontological reflex when you find one animal is to assume it dominated its ecosystem; two sympatric animals of the same broad clade in the same rocks argues for niche partitioning — probably diet — even at this early stage.

Second — and this is the small, human detail — the bones of Musango preserve evidence of a serious injury or infection that the animal had recovered from before it died. Bone remodels when it heals, and the remodeling texture and geometry are preserved in the fossil. Something bad happened to this eight-year-old dinosaur, and it survived it long enough for the bone to heal.
