## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Sunday, July twenty-sixth, 2026. Four items today. First, a preprint on influenza that argues one of the most prevalent transcription errors in the flu genome creates a small population of virions that dodge broadly neutralising antibodies — a form of antibody escape that doesn't require any actual genetic mutation. Second, on the business side, an FDA advisory committee narrowly voted this week to move four unproven peptides back onto the compounding pharmacy list, even though the agency's own reviewers said the evidence doesn't support it — plus a Sanofi eczema programme dying because the target itself may carry a cancer signal. Third, in AI, a paper on the reasoning traces that large language models produce when they think through chemistry problems — the traces are heavily hallucinated, but the hallucinations still shape the answer. And finally, animals — a Science Advances paper explaining how the brown tree snake managed to invade Guam despite being incredibly inbred.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

A preprint went up this week on bioRxiv from Jose Sanchez-Ruiz's group at the University of Granada, and it introduces a form of antibody escape that I hadn't really thought about before. The paper is about influenza hemagglutinin — the H in H five N one, or H three N two — which is the surface protein that lets flu bind and enter cells, and the protein most of our neutralising antibodies target. The claim, roughly, is that flu is dodging some of the broadest antibody classes not by mutating its genome, but by mistranslating it.

Let me back up and lay out the concept, because it takes a minute to explain.

Every protein your cells make, or that a virus makes inside your cells, comes from an m R N A that gets read by ribosomes one codon at a time. Translation is not perfect. Ribosomes make mistakes — they occasionally pick up the wrong amino acid, insert one where the codon actually specifies something else. That kind of error is called a phenotypic mutation, because it changes the phenotype of a specific protein molecule without changing the underlying gene. The next round of translation from the same m R N A will produce the correct protein again. Phenotypic mutations are transient, and until now they've largely been considered background noise.

What the Sanchez-Ruiz group did was measure the actual, per-site error rate landscape for influenza hemagglutinin m R N A. Using a consensus approach in high-throughput sequencing — which lets them distinguish real biological errors from PCR and sequencing artefacts — they mapped the frequency at which each individual codon in H A gets mistranslated. And they found that the errors are not uniform. Some sites are essentially error-free. Others produce mistranslations at rates well above baseline.

The main result is what they found at the top of that ranking. The most prevalent transcription error in the H A m R N A encodes a phenotypic mutation that impairs binding of broadly neutralising antibodies. The mistranslation rate at that site is about zero point two to zero point five percent — which sounds small, but that means for every thousand H A proteins the cell produces, two to five of them are the phenotypically-mutated form. Any given virion has hundreds of H A molecules on its surface, and any given infection is producing enormous numbers of virions. So this isn't a rounding error — it's a persistent, low-frequency population of H A variants presented on real virions in real infections that are less accessible to broadly neutralising antibodies. And, crucially, none of it shows up when you sequence the viral genome, because the genome is unchanged.

The authors argue that the R N A sequence and secondary-structure context around the error-prone site is what makes it error-prone, and that context is inheritable. So even though any given error is transient, the tendency of the virus to make that error at that site is fixed in the genome. They call this mechanism analogous to programmed recoding, which is a known phenomenon in some viruses where the genome is deliberately structured to cause the ribosome to slip and produce alternative protein products.

There are two things worth taking away here. First, this changes what "escape" means for flu. The standard mental model is: population-level variation from genetic drift and antigenic shift, plus adaptive selection on stably encoded mutations. This adds a second axis — a small but stable subpopulation of every virion carrying phenotypic escape variants, invisible to the genome, generated fresh in every infection. Second, this may partially explain a longstanding gap in the field. Broadly neutralising antibodies to conserved H A epitopes have been the holy grail of universal flu vaccine work for years, and they work well in the lab, but their real-world protective effect has been more modest than the neutralisation titers would predict. Phenotypic mutation might be one reason why: even when your antibody perfectly targets the conserved consensus H A, a fraction of the H A on the virion isn't the consensus. It's the encoded error.

This is a preprint, and we're going off the abstract — the full paper isn't rendered yet. If the numbers hold up on the read, this is the kind of finding that reshapes how the field thinks about correlates of protection.

On the business side.

The FDA convened its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee on July twenty-third to vote on whether to add four peptides to what's called the five-oh-three-A Bulks List. That's the technical name for a permitted list of bulk drug substances that compounding pharmacies — meaning independent, mostly small-scale pharmacies that mix custom formulations for individual patients — are allowed to work with. The four peptides on the table were B P C one fifty-seven, being pitched for ulcerative colitis; K P V and T B five hundred, both for wound healing; and MOTS-c, for weight loss.

Some context on why this matters. Compounding pharmacies exist as a legal parallel track to the mainstream FDA drug-approval process. If a patient needs a specific drug at a dose the manufacturer doesn't sell, or has a formulation allergy, a compounding pharmacy can make it. But that pathway is limited to substances that appear on the five-oh-three-A Bulks List, which the FDA maintains. The four peptides here had actually been banned about three years ago because of safety concerns. What happened this week is that they were up for reinstatement.

All four sit in what you might call the wellness-adjacent peptide space — heavily promoted online, popular with the biohacking community, prescribed off-label at longevity clinics, and demanded by patients arriving in physicians' offices with printed protocols from social media. B P C one fifty-seven is a synthetic fragment of a stomach protein that shows some tissue-repair effects in animal studies but has essentially no rigorous human clinical trial data. K P V and T B five hundred are in a similar posture. MOTS-c is a small mitochondrial-derived peptide with some interesting metabolism biology attached to it but, again, no human trial data behind the weight-loss claims.

The FDA's own scientific reviewers went into the meeting recommending against reinstating any of them. Their argument was straightforward: no substantive proof of efficacy, unresolved safety questions, and reinstating them onto a compounding list creates a semi-legitimate route to patients for products the agency itself does not consider safe or effective. The committee voted anyway to endorse all four — narrowly, with individual panelists expressing serious concerns on the record. One panelist said she was worried the committee was responding to market demand rather than science. Another voted no because he thought the endorsement could be actively harmful.

Why this matters for people who work in this space. The five-oh-three-A Bulks List is one of the primary channels through which unproven compounds get a route to patients in the United States, because compounding pharmacies fall outside the FDA's normal drug-approval jurisdiction. Adding items to that list effectively imports demand-driven products from the wellness industry into clinical practice, bypassing the standard efficacy and safety thresholds. This is a story about how the pharmacopoeia gets rebuilt not through the drug-approval process, but around it. The committee is scheduled to take up three more peptides — Emideltide, Semax, and Epitalon — later this week. Watch that vote.

Also on the business side, briefly. On Friday, July twenty-fourth, Sanofi announced it is discontinuing development of amlitelimab in atopic dermatitis, which is eczema. Amlitelimab is an antibody that targets O X forty L, a costimulatory signal in T cell activation. This was a drug Sanofi paid one point one billion dollars for back in 2021, positioning it as a potential complement to Dupixent, whose patent expires in 2031. Trial results over the last year have been uneven — the drug met endpoints in some late-stage trials and missed in others — and, worryingly, a handful of participants developed Kaposi's sarcoma, a skin cancer. The safety signal is not unique to amlitelimab. The same problem has appeared in a competing O X forty L-targeting antibody from Amgen and Kyowa Kirin. Two independent programmes with the same tumour signal on the same mechanism is not a coincidence — it suggests the target itself, not just this particular molecule, may carry risk. Sanofi will keep evaluating amlitelimab in celiac disease, but the eczema programme is done, and analysts read this as Sanofi now needing to spend heavily on deals to fill the post-Dupixent hole.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A paper out this week from Jiatong Li and colleagues, posted to arXiv, asks a question that anyone using a large language model for chemistry should care about. When you prompt a language model with a chemistry question and ask it to reason step by step — the technique known as chain-of-thought — the model produces a written trace of its reasoning. It draws structures, invokes rules, considers substituents, cites SMILES fragments. And the field has largely been treating those traces as a kind of explanation — as if we can read them and see how the model actually thinks about the molecule.

Some quick background on what SMILES is, since it's central here. SMILES stands for Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System. It's a way of writing a chemical structure as a string of characters. A benzene ring is lower-case c-one-c-c-c-c-c-one, aspirin has its own short string, and so on. It's the lingua franca that language models use to talk about small molecules, because it turns a picture into text.

The paper's argument, fairly strongly, is that chemical chain-of-thought reasoning is not a faithful window into what the model is doing. It is, in the paper's phrase, a hallucination-prone molecular scratchpad.

Here is what they did. They took a set of chemistry problems and ran them through multiple language models in chain-of-thought mode. Then they audited the intermediate reasoning steps against ground truth — checking every claim the model made about the molecule's structure, atoms, bonds, or substructures against what the molecule actually is. They found that hallucinations — meaning specific structural claims that don't correspond to anything in the real molecule, invented atoms, imagined bond connections, made-up scaffolds — are widespread throughout these reasoning traces. And critically, hallucinations show up even when the final answer the model gives is correct.

So far you could read this as encouraging. The model gets the right answer even when its reasoning is wrong; maybe the trace is just window dressing. But then they did a perturbation experiment: they modified the intermediate reasoning content — swapping in different structural drafts, changing SMILES fragments the model had written — and looked at what happened to the final answer. The final answer changed. Which means the hallucinated content is not decorative. It's actively shaping generation. The model is, in some sense, using this hallucination-laced scratchpad as a substrate for its output. Different models rely on the scratchpad in different ways — some lean on SMILES fragments, some lean on scaffold names — but the pattern is consistent.

The take-home for a working biomedical scientist. If you're using a language model to reason about molecules — to propose retrosyntheses, or annotate structures, or reason about drug-target interactions — do not read the chain-of-thought as if it's a chemically valid derivation. The trace is often false at the level of individual claims, even when the final answer is correct. That means the standard verification workflow of "let me read the reasoning and check the chain" doesn't do what it looks like it's doing. You have to independently verify the final structural claims — the actual molecule, the actual answer — not the trace. And you should assume that any SMILES-like or scaffold-like content sitting in the reasoning could be fabricated, even when the model's overall output happens to be right. The authors argue for training methods that enforce faithfulness in intermediate steps rather than just scoring answer accuracy. For end users, the more immediate takeaway is: don't be reassured by a plausible-looking chain of chemical reasoning. It's a scratchpad, not a proof.

And finally, animals.

A paper published this week in Science Advances, out of Trevor Krabbenhoft's group at the University at Buffalo with the U.S. Geological Survey, tackles a question that has bothered invasion biologists for decades. How did the brown tree snake take over Guam?

Some background. The brown tree snake is a mildly venomous, tree-dwelling snake native to parts of Australia, New Guinea, and the Solomon Islands. Some time shortly after World War Two, a handful of them — the founder population is estimated at a very small number, possibly single digits — arrived on Guam, probably stowed away in military cargo. Guam had no native snakes at all, and no ecological pressure to keep them in check. What happened next is one of the most infamous invasions in modern ecology. Within a few decades the brown tree snake population on Guam exceeded thirty thousand snakes per square mile in some areas. They ate their way through the native bird fauna, driving multiple species to extinction. They still routinely cause blackouts by climbing power lines.

The puzzle has always been: how did a population founded by a handful of animals, with almost no genetic diversity by any conventional measure, adapt so successfully? Small founder populations are supposed to run into the wall of inbreeding depression — reduced fitness from all your matings being between relatives. That obviously didn't happen here.

The paper uses long-read sequencing on brown tree snake genomes to find out why. Long-read sequencing, unlike the short-read Illumina data that most previous work on this population relied on, is good at picking up what are called structural variants — that's insertions, deletions, duplications, and rearrangements of chunks of DNA rather than single-letter changes. Structural variants are notoriously hard to detect with short reads because they get lost in the ambiguity of alignment.

What Krabbenhoft's group found is that the brown tree snake carries more than nineteen thousand structural variants across its genome, and those variants affect close to eight times as much total genomic DNA as the population's single-nucleotide variants do. In other words, the Illumina-era picture of this population as "essentially clonal" was radically undercounting the actual functional genetic diversity — because most of it was living in structural variation.

And the structural variants aren't uniformly distributed. They are enriched in immune genes — which likely explains how a founder population of a few snakes managed to keep up with the pathogens they encountered in a new environment — and in olfactory-receptor genes, the genes that control smell. That second one is interesting for a specific reason. Brown tree snakes on Guam do not cannibalise each other, even though they're at high density, related, and eat other small vertebrates including other snakes elsewhere in their range. The authors suggest the olfactory diversity may let individuals distinguish self and kin from unrelated prey and each other by smell — which would tell you why the population doesn't collapse into cannibalism the way you'd expect.

The bigger picture, and the thing the first author explicitly flags at the end of the paper, is that endangered species may have more genetic flexibility than we think. Conservation genomics has been organised around single-nucleotide diversity for the last twenty years, and if this pattern holds up in other species, then we've been reading a lot of critically-small populations as more genetically dead than they actually are. Which is either encouraging news for conservation, or, in the case of Guam, an explanation for why the birds never stood a chance.
