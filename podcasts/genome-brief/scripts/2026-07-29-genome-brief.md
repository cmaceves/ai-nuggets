## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Wednesday, July twenty-ninth, 2026. Four items today. First, a preprint from Wendy Barclay's lab at Imperial College takes another look at those two severe human H five N one cases in North America from late 2024 — the ones with the worrying mutations in the receptor-binding site — and finds the mutations do something no one expected. Instead of switching the virus to human-type receptors, they weaken binding to bird-type receptors, and that weakening turns out to help the virus escape airway mucus and shrug off Tamiflu. Second, a group at INSERM and Sorbonne University modeled where the ongoing Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak is most likely to spread next across Africa, and which countries are worst prepared for it. Third, on the business side, a small Cambridge, Massachusetts biotech called Apnimed launched its I P O this week to fund the first pill for obstructive sleep apnea. And finally, animals — a decades-long stranding study on pygmy sperm whales just turned up three previously unknown species of a bacterial genus that is very familiar in humans.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Going off the abstract on this first one, since the preprint body is still rendering on bioRxiv — but the abstract itself is unusually detailed, and this is a clearly dominant pick.

To set up why this matters, I need to walk through a piece of virology first.

Influenza viruses get into your cells by having a spike protein on their surface — hemagglutinin, or H A — grab onto a sugar molecule called sialic acid that sits on the outside of your cells. This is what the H in "H five" refers to. But there is more than one kind of sialic acid. Bird flus, including H five N one as it exists in wild birds and poultry, prefer a version of sialic acid where the sugar is attached with a specific chemistry called an alpha two comma three linkage. Human flus prefer a slightly different chemistry — alpha two comma six. That difference in linkage matters because the alpha two three receptors are what you find deep in a bird's gut and in the lower human airway, while alpha two six receptors dominate the upper human airway, in your nose and throat and windpipe. That's the tissue you cough droplets out of. So for a bird flu to become a human pandemic virus, the classic story goes, its H A needs to switch its receptor preference from alpha two three to alpha two six. Only then can it stably infect the upper airway and transmit person to person.

For H five, virologists have known for years that two positions in the H A protein are the key switches for this preference. Position one ninety, and position two twenty-six. Certain amino acid substitutions at those positions in past pandemic H1N1 and H2N2 flus were exactly what flipped those ancestral bird viruses into human-transmissible ones.

Now, late in 2024, two people were admitted to intensive care with severe H five N one infections — one in Louisiana, one in British Columbia. Both had the 2.3.4.4b lineage, both a subvariant called D one point one that came out of the wild-bird reservoir, not the dairy-cow outbreak. And when they sequenced the virus from those patients, the samples showed mixed signals at exactly those two positions — one ninety and two twenty-six. Not clean substitutions, but a mixed swarm suggesting the virus was on its way to something. That was the moment last winter when a lot of pandemic-preparedness people started paying much closer attention.

Here's what Wendy Barclay's group at Imperial College and their collaborators did. They took the specific mutations seen in those two patients, put them into H A one at a time and in combination, and asked two questions. First — do these mutations actually give the virus alpha two six binding? That is, do they switch the receptor preference the way the textbook says? And second — what else changes?

The answer to the first question is no. Alone and in combination, these mutations do not confer detectable binding to alpha two six sialic acid. Whatever the virus is doing, it is not the classic textbook switch.

The answer to the second question is where the paper gets interesting. What the mutations actually do is *weaken* binding to alpha two three sialic acid — the virus's native, preferred receptor. And weaker binding to alpha two three turns out to help the virus in two specific ways.

First, mucus. Your airway is lined with a layer of mucus, produced by goblet cells, and that mucus is loaded with heavily sialylated glycoproteins called mucins. From a flu virus's perspective, mucus is a trap. It's full of decoy sialic acid — bait receptors, floating free, that grab the virus and get carried away on the mucociliary escalator before the virus reaches a real cell. A virus with very high affinity for sialic acid gets stuck in the mucus. A virus with lower affinity slips through. The mutant viruses in this paper, with their weakened binding, evade human airway mucus better than the wild-type parent.

Second, antivirals. The two main antivirals we use for flu — oseltamivir, sold as Tamiflu, and zanamivir, sold as Relenza — don't target H A. They target the other protein on the virus surface, N A, or neuraminidase. Neuraminidase cleaves sialic acid, and its job is to help newly assembled virus particles get off the surface of the infected cell so they can go infect the next one. Oseltamivir and zanamivir are sialic-acid mimics that block neuraminidase in that final step. But how effective they are depends on how tightly H A is holding onto the cell's sialic acid in the first place. If H A grip is strong, neuraminidase has a lot of work to do, and blocking it strands the virus. If H A grip is weak, the virus can pull free without needing as much neuraminidase activity, and the drug matters less. The mutant viruses in this paper are less susceptible to both oseltamivir and zanamivir. Their looser grip lets them slip past the drugs.

The paradigm shift is this. For twenty years the field has framed H five human adaptation as a race for alpha two six binding — the receptor switch. This paper argues that H five can gain fitness in the human airway *without* that switch, by loosening its grip on alpha two three enough to escape mucus and to evade antivirals. That's a different evolutionary path than the one we've been watching for. It also means that when the next severe human H five case gets sequenced, and the H A has changes at one ninety or two twenty-six, the correct question isn't only "did it get alpha two six binding" — it's also "did it get better at surviving in mucus and worse at responding to Tamiflu." Those are separate assays. And the answer to the second question doesn't require the answer to the first.

The caveats. This is a mechanistic paper in engineered viruses and cell systems. It does not show onward transmission in ferrets, which is the closer animal model for human airway biology. And it does not show clinical failure of Tamiflu in the two index patients — they were treated late, but the paper is not framed as a treatment-failure analysis. Still, it complicates the pandemic surveillance picture in a way that seems important.

Staying with pathogens.

A modeling group led by researchers at INSERM and Sorbonne University in Paris — Vittoria Colizza's group, working with clinicians in the D R C and Uganda — put out a preprint mapping the importation risk of the current Bundibugyo Ebola outbreak across the rest of Africa.

Some context on the outbreak, because this is the fastest-growing Ebola epidemic on record and it's had less coverage than it deserves. Bundibugyo is one of six known species of Ebolavirus. The species you usually hear about — the one behind the 2014 to 2016 West Africa epidemic, the one that Ervebo, the licensed Ebola vaccine, protects against — is Zaire ebolavirus. Bundibugyo is a different species. It causes similar disease — high fever, hemorrhagic complications, historically case-fatality around thirty to forty percent — but Ervebo does not protect against it. There is no licensed vaccine for Bundibugyo. There are no licensed therapeutics. The current outbreak was declared in the D R C on May fifteenth of this year, and by July twenty-seventh — that's Monday — the D R C reported three thousand two hundred and sixty-two confirmed cases and one thousand four hundred and thirty-seven deaths. Uganda has had twenty confirmed cases. The W H O declared it a public-health emergency of international concern on May seventeenth.

What Colizza's group did is combine several data layers into an importation-risk model. High-resolution population data. Air-travel networks. Land-mobility data. Terrain-adjusted travel times, corrected for conflict zones where roads aren't usable. And then, national readiness indicators — health-system capacity, laboratory capacity, surveillance infrastructure — to figure out which countries would be least prepared to catch an imported case before it seeded local transmission.

The finding. Eighty-five percent of the estimated continental risk sits in immediate neighbors of the D R C. That's not surprising. What is more useful is the specific ranking, and it splits into two groups. The first-tier high-risk countries are South Sudan, Rwanda, Kenya, Tanzania, Zambia, Ethiopia, Burundi, Angola, South Africa, the Central African Republic, and Nigeria — a mix of neighbors and countries connected by air travel. Within that group, the biggest readiness gaps sit in South Sudan, Burundi, and the Central African Republic — countries at high importation risk with the weakest health systems. Then there is a second tier — Botswana, Burkina Faso, Cameroon, Chad, and Ghana — with intermediate risk but also low readiness. Those five are the ones the authors flag as most under-prioritized in current preparedness planning.

Why this matters. This is exactly the kind of analysis that lets W H O regional offices and country governments allocate limited stockpiles — protective equipment, diagnostics, ring-vaccination capacity where an experimental Bundibugyo-specific vaccine ends up being deployed — before an imported case turns into a cluster. And it makes the point that "neighboring country" is not the same as "highest risk" — some neighbors are low-risk because their border with the D R C is remote and poorly connected, while some non-neighbors are higher-risk because of airport connectivity or overland trade routes.

On the business side.

Apnimed, a Cambridge, Massachusetts biotech, priced its I P O terms this week — ten million shares at fourteen to sixteen dollars, targeting around a hundred and fifty million dollars in proceeds, with pricing expected Thursday night and trading Friday on the Nasdaq under the ticker A P M D. What matters here is not the deal size — it's the drug. Apnimed has a single clinical candidate, called A D one-oh-nine, brand-named Oxnimbi, that would be the first F D A-approved pill for obstructive sleep apnea.

To explain why that's a big deal — obstructive sleep apnea affects somewhere between twenty and thirty million adults in the United States. During sleep, the muscles of the upper airway relax, the tongue and soft palate slump backward, and the airway collapses. Breathing stops. Oxygen drops. The brain jolts the person half-awake to reopen the airway, and the cycle repeats — sometimes hundreds of times a night. Long-term consequences include daytime sleepiness that drives car accidents, and elevated risk of hypertension, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and metabolic disease. The standard treatment for the last forty years has been C P A P — continuous positive airway pressure — a bedside machine that pushes pressurized air through a mask to hold the airway open. C P A P works when patients use it. But roughly half of patients stop using C P A P within a year. The mask is uncomfortable, the machine is noisy, and it interferes with sleep in exactly the way it's trying to fix. There has never been an approved oral drug for the condition.

Apnimed's drug is a fixed-dose combination of two off-patent generic molecules — aroxybutynin, an anticholinergic used for overactive bladder, and atomoxetine, a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor originally approved for A D H D as Strattera. The mechanistic bet is that during sleep, the neurons that maintain tone in the airway dilator muscles — particularly the genioglossus, the muscle that keeps your tongue forward — lose their input. Combining anticholinergic action with elevated norepinephrine restores drive to those muscles enough to keep the airway open. It's not a novel biology story. It's a repurposing story, but backed by real trial data.

The trial data. Oxnimbi has been evaluated in something like thirteen hundred patients in Phase 2 and Phase 3 trials. The pivotal readouts, presented at the American Thoracic Society earlier this year, showed a roughly forty to fifty percent reduction in apnea-hypopnea index — the standard measurement of sleep apnea severity, counting breathing pauses per hour — versus baseline, sustained across multiple trials with dose-dependence. Effect size is smaller than what C P A P delivers when C P A P is worn all night, but Oxnimbi is delivered as one pill before bed, and adherence in the trials was above eighty percent. That's the practical trade-off: less pharmacological potency, dramatically higher real-world use.

What to watch. The company plans to file for F D A approval in the second half of this year, with commercial launch potentially in 2027 if the F D A holds to standard timelines. And in the background — as with every metabolic-adjacent chronic-disease indication right now — the G L P-one drugs like tirzepatide already reduce sleep apnea severity in obese patients through weight loss, and Eli Lilly won an F D A label expansion for that indication earlier this year. So Apnimed enters a market where a much more expensive, injectable drug already has a foothold. Their pitch is that a cheap oral pill, taken nightly, works on both obese and non-obese patients — sleep apnea affects both — and doesn't require the person to be on a chronic weight-loss regimen. Whether the market believes that pitch shows up in the I P O pricing on Thursday night.

And finally, animals.

Researchers at Florida Atlantic University's Harbor Branch Oceanographic Institute published a paper this month in the Journal of Wildlife Diseases documenting three previously unknown genotypes of bacteria — in the genus Helicobacter — inside pygmy sperm whales. The team pulled the samples from two decades of stranding events along the Florida coast.

The pygmy sperm whale is a species most people, including most biologists, have never seen. They are small — under about eleven feet — and they live in deep pelagic water, rarely near shore. Most of what we know about their biology comes from strandings, when a sick or injured animal washes up on a beach. Necropsies over the years have shown that a strikingly high fraction of stranded pygmy sperm whales have gastric ulcers — open sores in the stomach lining — and that the tissue is often full of a distinctive spiral-shaped bacterium. What the F A U group did is take stored tissue from twenty-plus years of pygmy sperm whale strandings, run histopathology plus 16S ribosomal R N A sequencing plus targeted Helicobacter genome sequencing, and characterize what these bacteria actually are.

Which brings me to the genus. Helicobacter is the same genus that includes Helicobacter pylori — the bacterium that colonizes the human stomach, is present in something like half the world's population, causes most peptic ulcers, and is a Class one carcinogen for gastric cancer. Pylori is genuinely one of the most consequential bacterial pathogens of humans, and the discovery in the 1980s that ulcers were an infection rather than a stress condition — the Nobel Prize work by Marshall and Warren — completely reframed how gastroenterology thinks about the stomach. What the F A U team found in pygmy sperm whales are three new members of that same genus — provisionally named Kogia Helicobacter one, two, and three, Kogia being the whale's genus name — associated with ulcers, inflammation, and fibrosis in the animals' stomachs, tracking the human pattern remarkably well.

Two things I think are interesting here. First, the finding argues that whatever biological trick H pylori pulls to survive in the extreme acid of a mammalian stomach — it has a whole battery of adaptations, including an enzyme called urease that generates ammonia to buffer local acid — is a much older and more widely deployed trick across mammals than we might have thought. Helicobacter has quietly infected marine mammals, apparently, since long before we noticed. Second, it's a reminder that stranding-based research is genuinely science — decades of tissue samples archived without knowing exactly what they'd eventually be used for turned into a comparative-genomics discovery about a globally important pathogen family. The pygmy sperm whales themselves get an explanation for a big fraction of their stranding-related ulcer disease. And the F A U team says the same approach — biobanked stranding tissue plus modern sequencing — should be run on other cetaceans next. There are almost certainly more Helicobacters out there, in more whales, doing the same thing.
