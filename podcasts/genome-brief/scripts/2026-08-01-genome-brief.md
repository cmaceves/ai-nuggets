## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Saturday, August first, 2026. Four items today. First, a group at the University of Veterinary Medicine Hannover built organoids from cow udders and threw a panel of flu viruses at them — and the answer to whether the dairy H five N one situation is really an H five N one problem, or a general cow-udder problem, is decidedly the second thing. Second, on the business side, Replimune's engineered herpes virus for anti-P D one-refractory melanoma cleared its F D A advisory committee ten-to-three on Wednesday, and the P D U F A decision is expected tomorrow — after two prior rejections. Third, in A I tools — Google DeepMind has quietly dissolved the AlphaFold team, less than a year after it won them a Nobel Prize, with the Nobel laureate and two key coauthors gone to Anthropic. And finally, animals — biologists slipped into a cave pool underneath a U S Army chemical-weapons arsenal in Alabama and found a completely new lineage of eyeless, colorless, faintly electric-blue cavefish that they named after the Demogorgon.

Let's start with pathogen watch.

Since the spring of 2024, the story that has dominated influenza surveillance in the United States is H five N one in dairy cattle. Highly pathogenic avian influenza — the same panzootic clade of H five that has been ripping through wild birds and poultry worldwide since roughly 2020 — jumped into North American dairy cows, and instead of causing a respiratory infection the way flu usually does in mammals, it went straight to the udder. Infected cows shed enormous quantities of virus in their milk. Something like a hundred and eighty herds across dozens of states have been affected. And that's a genuinely weird tissue tropism, because in every other mammal we know about, flu is a respiratory or intestinal infection, not a mammary one.

Which raised a question virologists have been asking for over a year now: is the cow udder specifically hospitable to *this* virus — highly pathogenic H five N one, with its particular biology — or is the cow udder generally hospitable to influenza A viruses, and we just didn't know because no one had ever put a flu virus into a cow udder before? Those are two very different worlds. In the first, the dairy problem is a peculiar collision of one virus and one tissue. In the second, the cow udder is a potential new niche for the whole influenza-A ecosystem, and the current H five N one outbreak is just the one we happen to be watching.

A group at the Institute of Virology at the University of Veterinary Medicine Hannover, in Germany, published a paper in Emerging Microbes and Infections this week that tries to answer that question head-on. The lead author is Henrik Fritsch. The senior author is Paul Becher. And what they did is build an organoid model of the bovine mammary gland — the cow udder — and then infect it with five different flu viruses spanning avian, swine, and human origins, to see which ones replicate.

A quick word on what an organoid is, because they show up in the podcast a lot. An organoid is a three-dimensional mini-tissue grown in a dish from primary cells — in this case, primary epithelial cells taken from the udder tissue of two dairy cows that had been euthanized for unrelated orthopedic problems. You start with a single cell type and coax it, using a defined cocktail of growth factors and hormones and a Matrigel scaffold, into self-organizing into a small hollow ball a fraction of a millimeter across that reproduces the *architecture* of the real tissue — the polarity, the cell layers, the differentiated cell types, the ability to respond to hormonal signals. In the case of the udder, the organoids formed bilayered structures with the correct luminal and basal cell markers and, when given lactation hormones — prolactin and the rest — turned on the milk-protein genes just like real mammary tissue does during lactation. It's a scalable, controlled system for infection experiments that you cannot do at scale in a live cow, and that requires B S L three containment when you use highly pathogenic avian flu.

Then they infected the organoids with five viruses. The current North American dairy H five N one — the actual 2024 Texas cattle isolate. Two low-pathogenic avian flus — H seven N seven from a duck, and H nine N two from a chicken. A swine H one N one from 2006. And a human pandemic H one N one from 2009, which is one of the direct descendants of the 2009 swine-origin pandemic that circulated globally.

Here are the numbers. H five N one replicated efficiently — hitting on the order of ten to the seven infectious virus particles per milliliter of supernatant within forty-eight hours, and with the flu nucleoprotein visible in essentially every cell of an infected organoid by immunofluorescence. That was the expected result. The human H one N one replicated to *the same level* as H five N one. The swine H one N one also replicated to the same level. The avian H seven N seven replicated productively, though somewhat less efficiently. Only the avian H nine N two was restricted — its replication stayed near the detection threshold, and it did not spread widely through the organoids.

So four out of five diverse flu subtypes replicate productively in cow udder tissue, and the mammalian-adapted human and swine H one N ones are just as efficient in the udder as the current dairy H five N one is. The cow udder is not selectively hospitable to H five N one. It is a broadly permissive niche for influenza A.

Why is it permissive? Here's where the paper connects back to receptor biology we've talked about on the show before. Recall that flu attaches to cells via sialic acid, and there are two versions of sialic acid — an alpha two-three linkage that's the classic bird-flu receptor, and an alpha two-six linkage that's the classic human-flu receptor. Different viruses have different preferences. And in most mammalian tissues, one linkage or the other dominates. What the Hannover group found by staining their udder organoids with lectins that specifically recognize each linkage is that the cow udder abundantly displays *both*. It is a two-receptor tissue. That, mechanistically, is a plausible reason why viruses from every host-species background can dock and get in.

And here is why that matters — this is the pandemic-preparedness angle. For decades, the mammal that has worried influenza virologists the most is the pig. Pigs have both alpha two-three and alpha two-six receptors in their respiratory tract, which means a pig can be simultaneously infected with a bird flu and a human flu. If those two viruses infect the same cell in a pig, they can swap chunks of genome with each other — a process called reassortment — and generate a new hybrid virus that has never existed before. The 2009 H one N one pandemic virus was a reassortant of pig, bird, and human flu segments that came together inside a pig. That's the mixing-vessel hypothesis, and it's the reason global influenza surveillance has always paid disproportionate attention to swine.

This paper's implication is that the cow udder, on receptor biology alone, meets the same criterion. It has both linkages. It supports productive replication of avian, swine, and human flus. And if the dairy H five N one outbreak reaches a herd that is *also* carrying a mammalian-origin flu — because a farmworker with seasonal H one N one milked an infected cow, say — the udder is a plausible site for the two viruses to coinfect the same cell and swap genome segments. That would be a genuinely bad outcome, because a reassortant that combined H five hemagglutinin with the internal genes of a human-adapted flu would be exactly the kind of virus preparedness planners have been worried about for twenty years.

Two caveats. First, the udder in a live cow is not just epithelium — it has stromal tissue, blood vessels, and infiltrating immune cells, none of which are in the organoid. The immune response could plausibly restrict some of these infections in ways this system can't see. Second, the authors used exogenous trypsin to activate the H A of the low-pathogenic viruses, which the udder in vivo might not provide, so the H seven N seven and H one N one results are more about *tissue permissiveness* than about whether these viruses would establish an outbreak in a real herd tomorrow. But the receptor-distribution finding — that cow udder has both linkages abundantly — is a physical fact about the tissue and doesn't depend on any of those caveats.

Bottom line — this paper reframes the dairy outbreak. The udder is not a strange one-off niche for H five N one. It's a general influenza reactor that we happen to be looking inside for the first time.

On the business side.

Replimune had a good Wednesday. Their oncolytic-virus candidate for advanced melanoma, called R P one — generic name vusolimogene oderparepvec — went in front of the F D A's Cellular, Tissue, and Gene Therapies Advisory Committee, and the panel voted ten-to-three that the pivotal trial, IGNYTE, produced adequate and interpretable evidence of clinical benefit. The P D U F A date — the deadline for the F D A to actually issue a decision on the biologics license application — is Sunday, August second. Tomorrow.

What matters here is not the vote count; it's the drug, and the patient population, and the fact that this is the third time R P one has been in front of the F D A.

Some background. Melanoma treatment in the last decade has been transformed by anti-P D one antibodies — drugs like Merck's pembrolizumab, Bristol Myers Squibb's nivolumab — which block a checkpoint on T cells and unleash the immune system against tumors. For advanced-stage melanoma, anti-P D one is now first-line, and it works well enough that a real fraction of patients see durable responses. But roughly half of patients whose disease progresses on an anti-P D one regimen have almost no good next option. Combination immunotherapy with anti-C T L A four antibodies helps some, at meaningful toxicity cost. Targeted therapies for the roughly forty percent of melanomas with a B R A F V six hundred mutation help some. Beyond that, the shelf is empty. That's the population R P one is aimed at — patients who have already failed anti-P D one, whose disease is advancing.

The drug itself. R P one is an engineered herpes simplex virus. Specifically, it's H S V type one — the same virus family as cold sores — modified to remove the neurovirulence genes so it can't cause meningitis, and to add two therapeutic payloads. The first payload is a viral fusion protein called G A L V-G P R-minus. That protein sits on the surface of an infected cell and physically fuses that cell with its neighbors, forming giant multi-nucleated cell blobs called syncytia that die. When you inject this virus directly into a melanoma tumor, it preferentially replicates in the cancer cells — because cancer cells have broken interferon defenses that would otherwise fight off herpes — and the fusion protein amplifies the killing beyond what the viral replication alone would do. The second payload is G M-C S F, granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, which recruits antigen-presenting cells to the dying tumor. Those cells pick up tumor antigens released from the destroyed cells, present them to T cells in draining lymph nodes, and set up a systemic anti-tumor immune response that can reach tumor deposits elsewhere in the body — not just the one you injected. In the pivotal trial, R P one is given in combination with nivolumab, on the theory that the local tumor destruction plus antigen release plus the checkpoint inhibitor gives you three complementary shots at the immune system.

The trial. IGNYTE enrolled a hundred and forty patients with anti-P D one-refractory advanced melanoma and treated them with R P one plus nivolumab. Objective response rate came in around thirty-three to thirty-nine percent depending on the analysis cut, complete responses in roughly fifteen percent, median duration of response exceeding thirty-five months, and a Grade three-plus adverse-event rate of about thirteen percent. For a population where standard-of-care effectively offers nothing, and where median survival on best-available therapy is measured in months, that's a real signal. But this was a single-arm trial. There was no randomized comparator.

Which is why F D A rejected R P one twice already. The first Complete Response Letter came in July of last year, arguing that a single-arm design in this indication was not adequate to support approval. The second, in April of this year, said essentially the same thing. So this week's advisory committee was not a normal step in a straightforward approval — it was a formal reset. And the ten-to-three vote is a strong signal from the panel that the observed response magnitude, in a population with no alternatives, meets the bar for accelerated approval — with the confirmatory trial, IGNYTE-three, already enrolling as a randomized study.

Why this matters. If R P one is approved tomorrow, it will be the first oncolytic virus to reach the U S market since Amgen's T-VEC in 2015. T-VEC has struggled commercially, in part because it's used in less-aggressive melanoma populations, in part because injecting a virus into every accessible lesion is operationally awkward. R P one enters a different market — patients who have burned through checkpoint inhibitors and are out of options — and pairs the local injection with a systemic checkpoint inhibitor to reach the disease you can't inject. If it works, it becomes a template that other oncolytic-virus companies will try to replicate in other indications. If F D A rejects a third time despite the advisory committee, it says something else — that the agency is holding a firm line on single-arm evidence in oncology even when the panel disagrees. Either way, tomorrow is worth watching.

On to A I tools.

Google DeepMind quietly disbanded the team behind AlphaFold this week. The story broke first in the Financial Times, and Engadget and several other outlets have covered it since. Less than a year after AlphaFold won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry — the second Nobel ever awarded for a computational method — the team that built it no longer exists as a unit. Its members have been redistributed across other Google DeepMind projects — mostly the Gemini foundation-model effort — and across Isomorphic Labs, Alphabet's drug-discovery subsidiary. About a quarter of the original team has left Google entirely. Notably, John Jumper — the Nobel laureate, who was DeepMind's technical lead on AlphaFold two and AlphaFold three — announced in June that he was leaving for Anthropic. Two other key coauthors of the AlphaFold papers, Jonas Adler and Alexander Pritzel, went to Anthropic with him.

Pushmeet Kohli, V P of research at DeepMind, framed the change as a strategic pivot: away from the "grand challenge" model, where you point a dedicated team at one hard scientific problem for years, and toward a model where a general-purpose foundation model — Gemini — is enhanced to do science broadly, across many problems. The AlphaFold databases and the AlphaFold Server that lets scientists submit protein complexes for prediction remain online. But the era of AlphaFold as a distinct scientific-computing project inside DeepMind is over.

Why does this matter to a working biomedical scientist. Two reasons. First, it's a real signal about the direction of AI in biology at the frontier labs. The bet DeepMind is making — that a general-purpose reasoning model with the right scaffolding will beat specialized architectures at specialized problems — is the same bet OpenAI and Anthropic are making across the board. Whether it's the right bet in a domain like protein structure, where the specialized inductive biases of AlphaFold two and three were what unlocked the accuracy gains, is genuinely unclear. The transition from a Nobel-caliber specialized team to a broader Gemini-plus-agents strategy will be read, correctly or not, as an implicit prediction that protein structure prediction is basically a solved problem and the next frontiers are broader. Coincidentally, a preprint went up on bioRxiv this same week benchmarking AlphaFold three's antibody-antigen predictions across thirty-four hundred complexes and finding systematic hallucination of epitopes — so the "solved" framing is at least contestable.

The second reason it matters. Jumper and two coauthors going to Anthropic is a real capability transfer between frontier labs. Anthropic has been much quieter about biology than DeepMind, but with Jumper on the inside, that will probably change. If you're a researcher who has been running AlphaFold Server workflows for the last two years and treating it as a stable Google service, this is the moment to at least watch for the second shoe to drop.

And finally, animals.

Biologists at the University of Alabama in Huntsville, led by Matthew Niemiller, published a paper in Scientific Reports on July twenty-seventh describing a completely new lineage of blind cavefish. New species — new genus, actually. And the story of where they found it is the fun part.

The site is called Bobcat Cave. It is a karst cave — that is, a cave formed in limestone by groundwater eating away at the rock — in northern Alabama. Karst caves are windows into the aquifer below them: the water in the pools at the cave floor is directly connected to the regional groundwater system, and animals that live in those pools are almost always cave-obligate, meaning they can't survive outside caves. Bobcat Cave sits directly beneath Redstone Arsenal, the U S Army installation outside Huntsville that produced chemical weapons — mustard gas, nerve agents — during World War Two and the early Cold War, and today is the home of Army rocket-development, N A S A's Marshall Space Flight Center, and various defense-industry tenants. It is one of the more studied caves in the southeastern United States because the arsenal has been monitoring subterranean biodiversity there for over three decades as part of environmental compliance for the installation.

Niemiller's group has known for a while that the cave contained cavefish — small pale fish in the family Amblyopsidae, historically identified as the Southern Cavefish, Typhlichthys subterraneus, which is widely distributed across the karst systems of the Interior Low Plateaus. What they found, on closer inspection with C T scanning of skeletal anatomy and mitochondrial and nuclear genetic sequencing, is that some of the fish in Bobcat Cave are Southern Cavefish and some of them are — not. They are their own thing. Superficially similar — eyeless, pigmentless, adapted to darkness — but anatomically distinct, with a longer flatter snout and a slight dorsal hunch, and genetically distant enough to warrant a new *genus*. They named it Demogorgonichthys arcanus. Demogorgonichthys, after the Demogorgon — the monster from Stranger Things that lives in a subterranean parallel dimension called the Upside Down — and arcanus, Latin for "hidden," because they had been hiding in plain sight for the thirty years the cave has been surveyed. In life, the fish are faintly, almost imperceptibly, electric blue.

Two things are cool about this. First, it's a textbook example of convergent evolution. Demogorgonichthys and Typhlichthys share the exact same pools — they live in what's called syntopy — and they've both independently evolved the classic cavefish package: no eyes, no pigment, low metabolism, sensitive lateral-line mechanoreceptors. But their most recent common ancestor is deep — millions of years back — and they are not each other's closest relatives. Two lineages, in the same cave, arriving at the same body plan by different routes.

Second, it's a reminder of how much biology is still hiding directly underneath us. Bobcat Cave has been monitored continuously since the nineteen-nineties. The Southern Cavefish there has been catalogued in surveys, sampled, cited. A new genus of vertebrate — not an invertebrate, not a microbe, a vertebrate animal you can pick up in your hand — was in those same pools the whole time, waiting for someone to look at it closely enough. It also has a real conservation implication: the entire known range of Demogorgonichthys arcanus is one cave, under an active military installation, which makes it one of the rarest fishes on Earth from the moment it was described.
