## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Friday, August fifteenth, twenty twenty six. Today: a Japanese-held H five N one vaccine stockpile that turns out to give complete protection against the bovine strain circulating in American cattle; Bristol Myers Squibb wins the first-ever FDA approval for a targeted protein degrader in multiple myeloma, and it's the first cancer approval based on a much stricter endpoint the field hasn't used before; Google drops a cheaper, faster workhorse coding model that quietly changes the economics of agentic AI; and, to close, a Vienna group puts pet dogs in a brain scanner and shows they can tell your anger from your sadness.

Let's get into it.

First, pathogen watch.

A group led by Yoshihiro Kawaoka, split between the University of Tokyo's Pandemic Preparedness Center and the University of Wisconsin's veterinary school, has a new paper in eBioMedicine — the Lancet's open-access biomedicine journal — that offers a genuinely encouraging piece of pandemic-preparedness news. It went online August eleventh. The question they set out to answer is simple, but nobody had directly answered it before. Several countries — Japan, the US, Canada, and a handful in Europe — have been stockpiling H five N one vaccines for years. Those stockpiles were built out of older H5 strains, from previous years and previous outbreaks. The virus that's now spreading in American dairy cattle, and that has crossed into humans dozens of times since 2024, is a genetically distinct beast — a bovine H five N one belonging to what virologists call clade two point three point four point four b. Does the frozen vaccine sitting in the national stockpile actually work against the virus circulating today? Nobody had run that experiment directly in an animal model until now.

A little background on why this isn't obvious. Influenza vaccines are strain-specific. The immune response to the hemagglutinin protein on the virus surface — the "H" in H five N one — is what generates the neutralizing antibodies that block infection, and those antibodies are exquisitely tuned to the exact shape of the specific hemagglutinin they were raised against. Change enough amino acids on the outside of that protein and the antibodies you have become useless. That's why the seasonal flu shot has to be reformulated every year. The stockpiled shots are what public health calls "pre-pandemic" — they're designed to be a bridge, providing early population-level protection in the first months of a pandemic while manufacturers scramble to make a strain-matched vaccine. But if the bridge doesn't actually hold up against the strain of the day, the whole strategy fails.

Kawaoka's group tested a specific stockpiled vaccine held in Japan: an inactivated preparation based on a twenty twenty strain called A slash Astrakhan slash three two one two, which is subtype H five N eight and belongs to the same broad clade two point three point four point four b — but a different subtype than the current bovine H five N one. That distinction matters. Both share the H5 hemagglutinin, but one is paired with N8, a different version of the "N" surface protein neuraminidase, and one is paired with N1. And critically, the H5N8 vaccine was formulated years before the American dairy outbreak. It was mixed with an adjuvant called AS oh three, which is an oil-in-water immune booster that ratchets up the antibody response. They then challenged both vaccinated and unvaccinated animals — mice and ferrets, the two standard influenza models — with a virus that is genetically identical to a real human isolate from a US dairy-worker case.

The results are striking. Every unvaccinated mouse and every unvaccinated ferret died. Every vaccinated animal survived. The vaccinated animals mounted robust neutralizing antibody responses against the bovine H five N one virus, viral replication in their organs was significantly suppressed, and there was complete protection against lethal disease. Animal models don't automatically translate to humans, but this is about as unambiguous a mouse-and-ferret result as pre-pandemic vaccine work ever produces.

Why does it matter? Two things. First, it validates the stockpile strategy for the specific scenario public health officials are most worried about right now — a bovine H five N one that acquires the ability to transmit between people. Countries with H5 clade two point three point four point four b stockpiles have a real head start, even though those stockpiles were built with a different neuraminidase subtype. Second, the fact that cross-protection holds across the N subtypes tells you something biologically important — the H5 hemagglutinin is doing most of the immunological heavy lifting. Which means the field can worry less about needing an exact H-N match for a stockpiled shot. There's still work to do — dose-finding and durability studies in humans, and the question of whether protection holds up against future drift within clade two point three point four four b. But if you've been losing sleep over the frozen H5 vials being useless against the strain currently in cattle, this study should let you sleep a little better tonight.

On the business side. And this is a genuinely big one.

Earlier this week, the FDA granted accelerated approval to Bristol Myers Squibb's iberdomide — brand name Zenbexus — in combination with daratumumab and dexamethasone, for adults with multiple myeloma who have received at least one prior line of therapy. This approval matters for two reasons that go well beyond the specific patient population. First, iberdomide is the first approval, ever, of a class of drugs called CELMoDs — cereblon E three ligase modulators. And second, it is the first cancer drug ever approved based on a response measure called MRD-negative complete response, which is a much more sensitive readout than the endpoints the field has traditionally used.

Let me unpack both of those, because they signal where oncology is going.

CELMoDs are a form of targeted protein degrader. To understand what that means, think about how most cancer drugs work. Traditional drugs block a bad protein — they bind to it and physically shut down its function. Degraders do something more elegant: instead of jamming the protein, they trick the cell's own recycling machinery into deleting it. Every cell has what's called the ubiquitin-proteasome system, whose job is to attach a molecular tag called ubiquitin to proteins that are supposed to be destroyed, and then feed those tagged proteins into a shredder called the proteasome. A CELMoD works by binding to a specific ubiquitin-attaching enzyme called cereblon, then physically recruiting the target protein to it, so that cereblon tags the target for destruction. In multiple myeloma, the two targets are transcription factors called Ikaros and Aiolos — proteins the myeloma cell absolutely needs to keep switching the right genes on. Delete them, and the tumor cells die.

If you know the older drugs thalidomide, lenalidomide, and pomalidomide — which have been backbone myeloma treatments for two decades — those are the accidental ancestors of this class. They work through the same cereblon mechanism, but they were discovered before anyone knew what cereblon even was, and the binding is loose. CELMoDs like iberdomide were designed from the ground up using cereblon's crystal structure, and were engineered to bind more tightly and drive faster, more complete degradation of the target proteins. That's the whole promise of the class.

The approval was based on the EXCALIBER-RRMM trial — a randomized phase three study that ultimately enrolled nine hundred thirty-nine patients with relapsed or refractory disease. For the accelerated approval, the FDA looked at the first four hundred twenty randomized — two hundred seven on iberdomide plus daratumumab and dexamethasone, and two hundred thirteen on the established regimen of daratumumab plus bortezomib and dexamethasone. And the primary endpoint the agency accepted is the interesting part: MRD-negative complete response. MRD stands for minimal residual disease — the depth to which the cancer has been driven below detectable levels. A conventional complete response means all the usual clinical signs of myeloma have disappeared. MRD-negative CR is much stricter: it means that even when you use ultra-sensitive next-generation sequencing to look for one myeloma cell in a hundred thousand bone marrow cells, you can't find one. That is a much harder target to hit, and in myeloma it correlates strongly with how long patients stay in remission.

The iberdomide arm hit MRD-negative CR in forty-one percent of patients, versus twenty-one percent on the standard regimen — nearly a doubling. That's the number the FDA approved on. Overall survival and progression-free survival are still being tracked as confirmatory endpoints, which is standard for an accelerated approval — the label holds conditionally until those mature.

Two implications. Commercially, Bristol has been staring at a cliff — its two blockbuster myeloma drugs, Revlimid and Pomalyst, are losing patent exclusivity, and analysts have been forecasting a big revenue gap. Iberdomide is the succession plan; the company is guiding to more than a billion dollars in annual US sales by twenty thirty-one, with list price around twenty-nine thousand five hundred dollars per twenty-eight-day cycle. Scientifically, this is a bigger moment for the targeted-degrader field. There are now dozens of degrader programs in the clinic, mostly in cancer but also in immunology and neurology. This is the field's first commercial validation that a degrader can win a first-line-adjacent regulatory decision — and that the FDA will accept an ultra-deep response endpoint like MRD-negative CR, which will shorten future trials in myeloma and adjacent hematologic cancers. Both things will accelerate the pipeline.

Now, AI tools.

Two days ago, Google DeepMind released Gemini three point seven Flash, and it's worth flagging even though it's an incremental release — because the incremental jump is bigger than usual, and the pricing sends a signal about where the frontier is going. Flash is Google's "workhorse" tier: not the smartest model on the market, but fast, cheap, and reliable enough to run inside agentic workflows and inside large-scale coding pipelines. The upgrade from three point six Flash to three point seven Flash roughly cuts the price in half. On Google's internal software-engineering benchmark called DeepSWE version one point one, the score moves from about forty-nine percent to sixty-five percent. On another benchmark called FrontierCode 1.1, which measures production-quality code, it moves from thirty-four percent to about forty-four percent. And Google is pricing input tokens at seventy-five cents per million through the end of the year — genuinely low for a model at this tier.

Why does this matter for a biomedical audience? Two reasons. First, if your lab or your company is running any AI-driven workflow — literature summarization, extraction from clinical documents, agentic code that runs analyses over trial datasets — the cost of that pipeline just dropped and the quality of the code the model writes just went up. That has a direct downstream effect on how ambitious you can be. Second, the shape of the trend — a workhorse model getting substantially better while getting substantially cheaper — is the pattern that unlocks agentic use cases at scale. Yesterday I talked about how a research version of Claude, orchestrating sixty subagents, produced a real advance on the Riemann hypothesis. That kind of orchestration only becomes economically feasible when the workhorse model underneath is cheap. The trend line matters more than any single benchmark.

And finally, animals.

There's a really lovely paper out this week in the Cell Press journal iScience, from a group led by Raúl Hernández-Pérez at the University of Vienna, that put pet dogs into a functional MRI scanner and showed that dogs can tell the difference between specific human emotions — not just "positive versus negative," but the difference between an angry face and a sad face and a fearful face.

A little context. Dogs are the animal we most often ascribe human-like emotional intelligence to, and a lot of what we think we see — the tilted head, the pointed ears — has been hard to pin down scientifically because behavioral cues can mislead. The gold standard for showing that an animal actually perceives a distinction between two things is neural — different stimuli produce different, reproducible patterns of brain activation. Doing that in dogs requires training pet dogs to lie still inside a very loud fMRI scanner while looking at pictures of human faces, which the Vienna group has been specializing in for years. The new study looked at eight dogs in a first experiment and twelve in a follow-up.

When the dogs looked at happy human faces, the researchers saw activation in the temporal cortex — the part of the brain used for higher-level social processing — and in the caudate nucleus, which in dogs and in humans alike is a core piece of the brain's reward system. Happy human faces literally light up the reward pathway in a dog's brain, which is a finding you can't fake and which quietly validates a lot of what dog owners have been claiming for centuries. But the more surprising result is that the dogs also distinguished among specific negative emotions — anger, fear, and sadness produced measurably different neural signatures. The authors call this the first neural evidence that dogs can not only tell positive from negative, but can pull apart specific bad moods.

The broader point is one about co-evolution. Primates learned to read facial expressions because we're primates — we evolved reading them in each other over tens of millions of years. Dogs came from a completely different evolutionary line; their ancestors were pack wolves reading each other's body posture and tail position, not faces. And yet, fifteen thousand years of close cohabitation with humans appears to have rewired that. The dog brain has, in effect, grown a small module for reading us. Which is, when you sit with it for a second, a fairly astonishing piece of convergent evolution.
