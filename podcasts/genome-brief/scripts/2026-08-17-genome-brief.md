# Episode 53: A universal flu shot aims at the neuraminidase

## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Monday, August seventeenth, twenty twenty six. Today: a Boston group publishes a preprint on a rationally engineered universal-flu immunogen — one that redirects the immune response toward a hidden, deeply conserved surface on the influenza neuraminidase, and does it with something as simple as extra sugar coating. On the business side, HUTCHMED and AstraZeneca announce topline results for the SAFFRON Phase three trial in EGFR-mutant lung cancer, and it's the first big randomized readout for adding a MET inhibitor on top of TAGRISSO after progression. In AI for biology, a group affiliated with EPFL puts out an updated preprint on BioKinema, a generative model that produces all-atom biomolecular trajectories directly — an attempt to sidestep classical molecular dynamics simulations. And to close, evolutionary biologists at Arizona State have run the largest phylogenetic test ever done on the sixty-year-old theory that a peculiar genetic quirk explains why ants, bees, and wasps built the most complex insect societies on earth, and the theory does not survive.

Let's get into it.

First, pathogen watch.

A group led out of Aaron Schmidt's lab at the Ragon Institute — with Facundo Nait Mohamed and Ruby Hecht as co-first authors — posted a preprint on August fourteenth describing a rationally designed influenza neuraminidase immunogen that redirects antibody responses toward a conserved, functionally critical part of the virus that ordinary infection or vaccination barely touches. I'll flag up front that I'm working from the abstract here — the full body text isn't rendered on bioRxiv yet — but the design principle and the outcome are unusually well captured in what's been released.

Here's the context. Influenza virus has two important surface proteins. Hemagglutinin, or HA, is the one everybody has heard of — it's the "H" in H five N one, it binds host cells, and it's what most seasonal-flu-vaccine antibody responses are aimed at. Neuraminidase, or NA — the "N" in H five N one — is the second, less-loved surface protein. Its job is to snip sialic acid off the surface of host cells so newly assembled virus particles can escape and go infect other cells. Neuraminidase inhibitors like oseltamivir — Tamiflu — target this enzyme directly. And for years, structural immunologists have known something interesting: neuraminidase has a much more conserved catalytic site than HA has anywhere on its surface. If you could raise antibodies against that catalytic site, you'd have neutralizing coverage across essentially every flu strain that has ever existed and every one that will. That's the universal-flu-vaccine grail, from the NA side.

The problem is that when you vaccinate with plain neuraminidase, the antibody response mostly ignores the conserved catalytic pocket and mostly hits variable loops around the edges — the exposed, easy-to-get-at parts of the surface. It's a classic immunological problem: the immune system focuses on whichever epitopes are easiest to see, not the ones that would give you the broadest protection.

The trick the Schmidt lab used is called hyperglycosylation. They engineered extra N-linked glycans — extra sugar chains — onto the variable, distracting parts of the NA surface, physically covering them up. The idea is that if you glycan-shield the parts of the protein you don't want the immune system to focus on, the response has to converge on the parts you left uncovered. It's a strategy that has been used before on HA and on HIV envelope; the news here is that it works, cleanly, on NA.

In mice, the hyperglycosylated immunogen produced serum antibody responses of comparable magnitude to the wild-type protein — so, importantly, they didn't lose titer by masking. But the responses had markedly increased breadth. Antibodies from hyperglycosylated-immunized mice recognized N two neuraminidases from H two N two and H three N two strains spanning nearly sixty-five years of antigenic drift, and even reached across to an N nine — a heterosubtypic recognition, which is what a universal vaccine actually needs to do. From single-B-cell analyses they isolated a monoclonal antibody that structurally recognizes a previously undefined conserved epitope at the neuraminidase tetramer interface — a spot that's essentially hidden in the intact protein and only accessible because the shielding pushed the response there. And passive transfer of that antibody partially protected mice against lethal heterologous influenza challenge.

Why it matters. This is the kind of result that supports the emerging strategy of building the next generation of flu vaccines around NA, not HA, and doing it with glycan shielding as the steering mechanism. It doesn't solve the H five N one problem tomorrow — this is a mouse-model preprint, not a clinical trial — but it's exactly the kind of design principle that a stockpile-strategy conversation, of the kind we discussed last week around the Japanese H five N eight stockpile, ultimately depends on. Anything that lets a single immunogen elicit cross-subtype, cross-decade neutralization is disproportionately valuable when the pandemic candidate you're worried about isn't the one you designed the vaccine against.

On the business side.

This morning HUTCHMED — the Hong Kong and Nasdaq-listed oncology-focused biotech spun out of Hutchison — and its partner AstraZeneca announced topline results from a Phase three trial called SAFFRON. The headline: adding HUTCHMED's oral MET inhibitor savolitinib, brand name ORPATHYS, to AstraZeneca's TAGRISSO — osimertinib — significantly improved both progression-free survival and overall survival compared to platinum-doublet chemotherapy in patients with EGFR-mutated non-small-cell lung cancer who had progressed on osimertinib and whose tumors carried high-level MET amplification or overexpression. The specific hazard ratios and median survival numbers weren't disclosed in the press release — they'll be presented at a medical meeting shortly — but the trial hit both co-primary endpoints, with a safety profile that matched what was already known for each drug individually.

Let me unpack the science, because this result matters more than the average precision-oncology readout.

EGFR — the epidermal growth factor receptor — is a cell-surface tyrosine kinase that drives roughly fifteen percent of non-small-cell lung cancers in the U.S. and about half in East Asia. Osimertinib is the third-generation covalent inhibitor of mutant EGFR and it's the global standard of care in the first-line setting; median progression-free survival on osimertinib in first-line EGFR-mutant lung cancer is around eighteen to twenty months. But eventually the tumor comes back. The interesting question in this disease is not whether resistance happens but *how* it happens, because resistance mechanisms are surprisingly diverse and treatment matters for each one.

MET amplification is one of the most common of those resistance mechanisms. MET is another receptor tyrosine kinase; when the tumor amplifies the MET gene — makes many extra copies — it can bypass EGFR blockade by activating essentially the same downstream survival pathways through a different receptor. Roughly one in three patients who progress on osimertinib turn out to have MET amplification or overexpression as the driver. Until now, the standard next-line option for these patients has been platinum-based chemotherapy, with response rates in the low thirty percent range and short response durations. It's arguably the single biggest unmet need in a disease where the front-line drugs are outstanding but the back-line options are mediocre.

SAFFRON enrolled three hundred thirty-eight patients across two hundred thirty centers in twenty-nine countries, and it prospectively required MET testing — both immunohistochemistry and fluorescence in situ hybridization — with cutoffs prospectively selected from the earlier SAVANNAH Phase two dataset. This is the crucial design detail: earlier failed MET-plus-EGFR combination trials arguably diluted their effect size by enrolling MET-low tumors. SAFFRON only took the tumors most likely to actually benefit, and it looks like that discipline paid off.

Regulatorily, savolitinib is already approved in China for this indication on the basis of an earlier SACHI Phase three trial, and Switzerland granted a temporary authorization. The SAFFRON data now form the basis for global submissions — FDA, EMA — over the coming months. If approved, this is the first oral doublet designed specifically to address post-osimertinib MET-driven progression, and it turns MET testing at the time of progression into a routine, standard-of-care question. The strategic angle for AstraZeneca is that it extends the TAGRISSO franchise into second-line and blunts the impact of impending osimertinib biosimilar competition later this decade.

Now, AI in biomedicine.

A group of authors affiliated primarily with EPFL, led by Bowen Feng and Patrick Barth, posted an updated preprint this weekend on a system called BioKinema. Again — this is a preprint, and I'm working from the abstract here — the full body isn't yet available on bioRxiv. But the framing is worth walking through.

Here's the target problem. Static protein structure prediction — AlphaFold, Boltz, ESMFold — is essentially solved for a lot of practical purposes. Give the model a sequence, get a plausible three-dimensional structure. What's not solved is dynamics. Proteins are not statues; they wiggle, flex, open cryptic binding pockets, and switch between conformations, and biology often lives in those transitions. The classical way to compute that is molecular dynamics — MD — where you write down Newton's equations for every atom, plug in an empirical force field, and integrate forward in time. Beautiful in principle, incredibly expensive in practice. Simulating a single medium-sized protein for a microsecond of biological time can cost weeks on a GPU cluster and produce terabytes of trajectory. Rare events — a ligand unbinding, an allosteric switch flipping — often occur on timescales that MD can't reach at all without enhanced sampling tricks.

BioKinema tries to sidestep this by learning a generative model over trajectories directly. The architecture is a spatial-temporal diffusion model designed around a physical prior — specifically, the exponential decay of correlations you'd expect from Langevin dynamics — and it's explicitly trained to reproduce not just plausible structures but the correct kinetics and thermodynamics of the ensemble. To avoid the classic problem where autoregressive generative models drift off the manifold over long horizons, the authors use a hierarchical strategy: predict a coarse trajectory first, then interpolate the fine time steps between them.

What they report is that the model reproduces the equilibrium free-energy landscape and the kinetics of test protein systems, that for protein–ligand complexes it captures ligand-driven conformational changes and allosteric effects, and — perhaps most interestingly — that when combined with enhanced-sampling training data it can predict rare events like ligand unbinding pathways.

Why this matters. There is a whole class of drug discovery problems — cryptic pocket detection, allosteric drug design, residence-time prediction — that hinges on cheap access to dynamics, not just structure. If a diffusion-model surrogate for MD works reliably at all-atom resolution, it changes what's tractable at the screening stage. The caveats are the usual ones for a preprint: how brittle is it out of distribution, how does it scale to the size of a real membrane-embedded target, and how faithful is the kinetics quantitatively? Those questions will play out over the coming months as people try to use it. But the direction of travel — from static-structure prediction to dynamics prediction — is the natural next arc, and BioKinema is a serious entry.

And finally, animals.

Sachin Suresh and Timothy Linksvayer at Arizona State University published a paper in Current Biology this month that pulls the rug out from under one of the classic teaching examples in evolutionary biology. Their target is the haplodiploidy hypothesis for eusocial evolution — a theory proposed in the nineteen sixties by W.D. Hamilton to explain why eusociality — the fully social lifestyle with reproductive queens and non-reproductive worker castes — evolved so many times in ants, bees, and wasps but only rarely elsewhere.

Here's the classical story. Most animals are diploid — every individual carries two copies of each chromosome. But Hymenoptera — ants, bees, and wasps — use haplodiploidy: females develop from fertilized eggs and are diploid, while males develop from unfertilized eggs and are haploid, carrying a single copy. This has a strange consequence for relatedness. A female worker shares, on average, three-quarters of her genes with her full sisters — because she inherits her father's entire single chromosome set and half of her mother's — but she'd share only half her genes with her own daughters. Under kin selection theory, if you can propagate more copies of your genes by helping your mother raise sisters than by having your own kids, evolution should favor sisterhood over motherhood. Hamilton argued that this quirk primed haplodiploid insects for eusociality in a way diploid animals aren't.

It's a beautiful hypothesis, and it has been in every undergraduate biology textbook for six decades.

Suresh and Linksvayer put it to the biggest test ever done. Using two large species-level phylogenies covering approximately sixty-nine thousand insect species, they applied comparative phylogenetic methods to ask whether haplodiploid lineages give rise to eusociality at higher rates than diploid lineages, controlling for shared ancestry. On first pass, yes, the association was there. But when they looked more carefully, essentially the entire association came from a single evolutionary branch — the aculeate Hymenoptera, meaning the stinging ants, bees, and wasps. Once that clade is treated as one event rather than many, haplodiploid insects overall are not more likely to evolve eusociality than diploid insects. In other words, the pattern that Hamilton was explaining doesn't actually exist across insects — it exists in one lineage that also happens to have stingers, controlled nesting behavior, mass provisioning of larvae, and a laundry list of other traits that any of which could plausibly be the real driver.

It's a nice reminder that a beautiful idea about mechanism doesn't automatically make it the right explanation, and that phylogenetic scale matters. The eusocial insect success story looks like it wants a lineage-specific explanation now, not a genetic-system-level one.
