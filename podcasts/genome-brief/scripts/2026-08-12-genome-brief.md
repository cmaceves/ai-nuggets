## Script

Good morning. This is The Genome Brief for Wednesday, August twelfth, twenty twenty six. Today: for the first time ever, H five N one clade two-point-three-point-four-point-four-b has been detected on mainland Australia — sequenced from two dead seabirds in Western Australia — and the phylogeography tells you exactly which route it took to get there, which is a story that starts on a sub-Antarctic island most people have never heard of. Then, on the business side, Silence Therapeutics reported Phase two data on a first-in-class small interfering R N A drug for a rare blood cancer called polycythemia vera — and the mechanism is a really elegant piece of iron biology worth walking through. In A I for medicine, a group in China posted an interesting preprint building an "auditor" agent that checks the work of drug-discovery A I — an early attempt to close what the authors call the verification gap. And to close: dolphins in Queensland just gave us the first direct evidence of a calf apparently learning tool use from its mother — and the same tool-use trick, using empty seashells to trap fish, evolved independently on the other side of Australia.

Let's get into it.

Pathogen watch, first. A large collaboration led by the CSIRO Australian Centre for Disease Preparedness posted a preprint on bioRxiv this week reporting the first confirmed detections of highly pathogenic avian influenza H five N one on mainland Australia. Two live seabirds — a brown skua and a southern giant petrel — were found on the south coast of Western Australia in June of this year. Both tested positive. Both were fully genome-sequenced. And the phylogeography, when you work it through, tells a very specific story about how the world's largest wildlife pathogen to date finally reached the last major continent that had held out against it.

Let me set that up. H five N one clade two-point-three-point-four-four-b — sometimes just called two-three-four-four-b — is a highly pathogenic subtype of avian influenza that first emerged in Europe around twenty twenty, jumped across the Atlantic to North America in twenty twenty one, ripped through wild birds and poultry across the Americas by twenty twenty two, reached Antarctica and the sub-Antarctic islands by twenty twenty three and twenty twenty four, and along the way has caused mass mortality events in seabirds, marine mammals, and — most alarmingly for the human-health people — spilled into dairy cattle in the United States starting in twenty twenty four. The virus is considered a panzootic — that's the animal version of a pandemic, a disease affecting many species across many continents. Until this June, the two big holdouts on the map were Oceania and mainland Australia. That's now over.

Here's what the CSIRO team did. They took the two positive seabird samples and generated full-genome sequences of both viruses. Then they did a time-calibrated phylogeographic analysis. That mouthful of a phrase is worth defining: a *phylogenetic* analysis builds an evolutionary tree of virus sequences to show which ones are closely related; a *time-calibrated* one uses the estimated mutation rate to place dates on the branches; and a *phylogeographic* one layers on the geographic origin of each sequence, so the tree lets you infer not just when strains diverged, but where each ancestral virus was located. If you do that with enough sampled viruses, you can effectively trace the routes a virus took across the globe.

Here's what the tree showed. Both Western Australian viruses are most closely related to H five N one sequences from sub-Antarctic islands in the Southern Indian Ocean — and specifically, both cluster tightly with viruses sampled on Heard Island, which is a small, uninhabited Australian territory about four thousand kilometers southwest of the mainland. Ancestral location reconstruction — that's the phylogeographic technique that infers, for each internal node of the tree, the most likely place the ancestor was — put Heard Island as the most probable source. The two Western Australian viruses aren't sister sequences to each other, either — they're independent introductions from Heard Island, meaning the virus made the jump to the mainland at least twice separately. This wasn't one seabird flying over; this is a corridor.

A few more things worth pulling out. There's no evidence of reassortment with any endemic Australian avian flu strain — meaning the Western Australian viruses have segments that all match the Heard Island lineage, they haven't started swapping genetic material with viruses already circulating in Australian ducks. Both viruses retain what the authors call "key avian-like genetic markers" — meaning the virus is still adapted to birds, not to mammals, and lacks any of the known substitutions that would reduce susceptibility to the standard antiviral drugs. So the immediate spillover-to-mammals risk is low. But the virus is on the continent.

Why does this matter? Three things. One: it opens up the possibility of establishment on the Australian mainland — meaning the virus takes hold in the local wild-bird population and doesn't just visit but stays. Australia has enormous poultry and wild-waterfowl populations, huge geographic area, and until now essentially no natural immunity to two-three-four-four-b in any of its bird populations. Two: the specific corridor identified here — the Southern Ocean pathway via sub-Antarctic islands — is the kind of finding that changes national surveillance strategy. Now you know where to be looking. Migrating seabirds coming north from the southern islands each austral winter are now the sentinel population. Three, and more broadly: this is what genomic surveillance is *for*. Two dead seabirds on a beach became a phylogeographic map of an intercontinental disease incursion in under two months. That is a level of pathogen-tracking speed that would have been unimaginable ten years ago.

On the business side. Yesterday, Silence Therapeutics — a London-based R N A therapeutics company that most people probably haven't heard of unless they follow the siRNA space — reported positive Phase two topline data on a drug called divesiran in a rare blood cancer called polycythemia vera. And the mechanism is one of the most elegant applications of iron biology to hematology in recent memory, so it's worth walking through carefully.

Let me start with the disease. Polycythemia vera — often just P V — is a rare, chronic blood cancer in which the bone marrow makes too many red blood cells. It's driven, in almost every case, by an acquired mutation called J A K two V-six-one-seven-F — the J A K two gene, which encodes a kinase that normally sits downstream of the erythropoietin receptor and tells red-cell progenitors when to divide. In P V, that kinase is stuck permanently on, so the marrow keeps churning out red cells whether the body needs them or not. The result is thick, sludgy blood — high hematocrit, high blood viscosity — and the medical consequences are increased risk of stroke, heart attack, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. The mainstay of treatment for the roughly one hundred sixty thousand Americans with P V has, incredibly, remained *phlebotomy* — literally drawing blood at intervals to keep the red-cell count down — supplemented in some patients with hydroxyurea or interferon or the J A K two inhibitor ruxolitinib.

Here's where the biology gets interesting. Making red blood cells is enormously iron-hungry — hemoglobin is a huge fraction of what you're building, and hemoglobin needs iron. So one way to slow red-cell production would be to starve the bone marrow of iron. The body already has a hormone that does exactly this: it's called hepcidin, produced mainly by the liver, and it acts as the master regulator of systemic iron availability. When hepcidin is high, iron gets sequestered inside gut cells and macrophages instead of released to the bloodstream, and less of it reaches the marrow. When hepcidin is low, iron flows freely. Hepcidin itself is under negative regulation — turned off — by a liver protein called T M P R S S six, a protease that, when active, tells the liver to make less hepcidin.

Divesiran is a small interfering R N A — a short piece of double-stranded R N A designed to trigger the cellular machinery that shreds a specific messenger R N A. Its target is the T M P R S S six messenger R N A in the liver. Knock that down, and the brake on hepcidin production comes off. Hepcidin rises. Iron gets held back from the marrow. Red-cell production slows. The elegance of it is that you're not blocking J A K two, you're not killing bone marrow cells; you're just closing the iron faucet upstream of the whole process.

Here's the trial result. The SANRECO study was a Phase two, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in forty-eight P V patients who were phlebotomy-dependent. They received divesiran subcutaneously either every six weeks or every twelve weeks over thirty-six weeks. Primary endpoint was clinical response — defined as avoiding needing a phlebotomy and keeping hematocrit below forty-five percent from week eighteen to week thirty-six. The results — eighty-eight percent of divesiran-treated patients hit that response, versus nineteen percent on placebo. That's a placebo-adjusted response rate of sixty-nine percentage points, and the p-value is less than one in ten thousand. The lower-frequency dose group — one shot every twelve weeks — hit an eighty-one percent response. Every-six-weeks was ninety-four percent. Safety was clean — a couple of grade-one anemias, which mechanistically is exactly what you'd expect if you overshoot the iron restriction. And it was well tolerated overall. Phase three is planned to start in the first half of next year.

Why does this matter? Two things. One: on the pure science, this is the first big Phase two proof of concept that you can drug an iron-regulatory pathway — very much a metabolism-first target — to treat a hematologic cancer whose primary driver is a signaling mutation. That's a genuine mechanistic shift. Two: siRNA drugs — you might know Alnylam's Onpattro or Amvuttra or Leqvio — are, when they work, dosed only every few weeks or few months, because they knock down a target for a long time from a single injection. Every twelve weeks in P V, if this holds up in Phase three, would be a night-and-day improvement over the current standard of scheduled bloodletting. Now, this is Phase two, forty-eight patients, thirty-six weeks. The registrational readout is still years away. But the mechanism is unusually clean and the magnitude of effect is unusually large, and this is the kind of trial that reshapes an indication.

Now, A I in biomedicine. A group in China posted a preprint on bioRxiv yesterday describing an agentic A I framework they call — and this is a lot of jargon in one sentence, so bear with me — a "self-auditing" system for A I-driven drug discovery. The paper is about immune aging — specifically, about finding small molecules that could rejuvenate the immune systems of older people — but the interesting part is not the biology, it's the *architecture*, and it maps onto a real problem in how A I is being used in biology right now.

Here's the setup. Over the last two years, several groups have built what are essentially A I agents that read the biological literature, propose hypotheses — "compound X should reverse aging phenotype Y in cell type Z" — and generate long lists of candidate compounds to test. The problem the authors point out is that those hypotheses are almost never actually *verified* against biology before the experiment is run. The A I says "this compound should do this," and unless someone puts it in a dish and looks, you don't know whether the underlying scoring the A I was using was even a sensible score. When you do run the experiment, you find out that maybe the A I was optimizing for something that doesn't really matter, or that a compound it ranked highly does nothing, or that its criterion for "success" is being met by artifacts. That's what they call the verification gap.

Their proposal is to bolt three verifiers onto the A I. The first, called P A C E, is a phenotype verifier — it takes gene-expression data from immune cells and boils "immune aging" down to ten specific cell-type-resolved gene-expression modules that are stable across four independent human cohorts, so that "did the compound do anything useful?" has a concrete, biology-anchored answer rather than a vague one. Then, when they tested this against biological-age clocks in an independent cohort of four hundred thirty-four elderly donors, it outperformed five established aging clocks. That gives you a solid phenotype to aim at. The second verifier, called Cell Q, is what they call a virtual-cell model — a large language model trained on single-cell R N A sequencing data, where each cell's gene-expression profile is compressed down into eight discrete tokens the model's vocabulary can consume. What that lets you do is simulate, computationally, what a given perturbation should do to a given cell type before you spend the money to run the experiment. Third, and this is the part I think is genuinely novel, they have an agent architecture with three components — an analyzer, a planner, and an auditor — where the auditor's job is not to run the drug-discovery workflow but to audit the analyzer's and planner's scoring criteria against actual experimental results and demand that they update those criteria when they turn out to be wrong.

Here's the fun test they ran. The system screened one hundred ten compounds in primary human immune cells. Its initial objective was to weigh all ten immune-aging modules equally in scoring. In the wet-lab data, though, some modules turned out to be much more responsive to compound treatment than others — the aged-down modules were, on the whole, easier to reverse than the aged-up modules. The auditor noticed this imbalance in the data and rewrote the system's own objective function — moving away from an equal-weighted average of the modules to a rule that requires balance across all of them. So the system caught itself using a bad objective, changed its own objective, and — the key confirmation — the revised objective generalized to a separate thirteen-compound experiment in T cells.

Why does this matter? Two things. One, at the concrete level: it's a step toward closing the verification gap that people have been complaining about for a while now in A I-for-drug-discovery. Two, more broadly: the whole design point here — an A I system that not only proposes hypotheses, but audits its own scoring logic against experiments and revises what it's optimizing for — is what people have been calling for as the next generation of scientific A I. This is early — one biology, one auditor architecture, one lab. But it's the shape of the thing.

And finally — animals. A really lovely paper published this week in the journal *Marine Mammal Science*, from Alexis Levengood and colleagues at the University of the Sunshine Coast in Queensland, Australia. They document, on the east coast of Australia, dolphins using seashells as hunting tools — and, for the first time anywhere, video of a calf appearing to learn how to do it from its mother.

Here's the setup. Between July and October of last year, the team surveyed a population of Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins in the Great Sandy Marine Park in southeast Queensland, from boats and drones. And they observed something called "shelling." Shelling is a foraging trick where a dolphin drives a fish into an empty seashell, grabs the shell — often a large, empty gastropod or bivalve — carries it to the surface, and then tips it up so the water drains out, delivering the fish straight into the dolphin's open mouth. It's an ingenious little bit of hydraulic engineering, and it's a documented behavior — but until this study, only in one place: Shark Bay, in Western Australia, more than six thousand kilometers away.

Which is the first interesting thing. Two dolphin populations on opposite ends of the same continent have independently converged on the same tool. That's the kind of parallel invention that suggests the underlying behavioral trick is one that dolphins are actually pre-adapted to work out.

But the second interesting thing is what makes the paper. In one observation session, the team recorded an adult female shelling a fish, dropping the shell — and the calf swimming behind her picking the shell up within minutes and handling it, apparently, in the same way she had. That is, so far, the first suggestive evidence that shelling can be transmitted from a mother to a calf by demonstration. In Shark Bay, the analogous behavior is called sponging — dolphins put marine sponges on their beaks to protect them while they poke around in the seafloor for camouflaged fish — and sponging has been shown, through decades of longitudinal work by the Shark Bay group, to be transmitted almost exclusively from mother to daughter down a single matrilineal chain. This Queensland observation is the first indication that shelling might follow the same social-transmission pattern.

Why is that fun? Because tool-use is one of those behaviors we used to think was mostly a human thing, then a great-ape thing, then a corvid thing — and every few years, a marine biologist rolls up with clean data showing another cetacean lineage doing it too, and, in this case, apparently teaching it. It's a good reminder that cultural transmission — knowledge that passes down a matriline rather than through the genome — is much older and much wider across the tree of life than any of us grew up hearing.
